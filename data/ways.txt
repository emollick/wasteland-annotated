part: 1
title: Ten ways into The Burial of the Dead
---
kind: listen
title: Hear it before you read it
lens: listen
body: Virginia Woolf, who heard Eliot read the poem at dinner in June 1922, wrote in her diary that "he sang it & chanted it rhythmed it." Start with his own voice: the listening room has his recordings, and the first section is where the chant is plainest. Then read the seventy-six lines silently and notice where his stresses fell.
---
kind: lens
title: Count the speakers
lens: voices
body: Switch on Voices and the section splits into at least nine: the poem's own voice, Marie, a stranger in a café, a prophet, a sailor in Wagner, a girl with hyacinths, someone who could not speak, a clairvoyante, a commuter. The typescript's title was "He Do the Police in Different Voices." Ask, of each, whether it is quoting someone else.
---
kind: do
title: Lay out the cards
lens: cards
body: Madame Sosostris deals seven cards and one vision. Turn them over one at a time on the table and read the note on each. Then ask what she leaves out: no Hanged Man, no water except the kind that drowns.
---
kind: map
title: Walk the commute
lens: places
body: London Bridge, King William Street, St Mary Woolnoth, the stroke of nine. It is a route of about six hundred yards, the one Eliot walked to Lloyds Bank. Follow it on the map, then read lines 60–68 as a man who has just walked it in fog.
---
kind: source
title: Put Chaucer beside it
source: chaucer
body: Read the first twelve lines of the General Prologue, then Eliot's first seven. Same month, same rain, same roots. Decide what has changed between 1387 and 1922, and whether it is the weather or the people.
---
kind: lens
title: Follow the water
lens: water
body: Spring rain, forgetful snow, a shower of rain, dry stone, no sound of water, a handful of dust, a drowned sailor, "Fear death by water." The section's weather is its argument. The Water lens tints every line by what it is made of.
---
kind: path
title: Meet the dead
path: dead
body: Dante's crowd at the gate of Hell, Limbo's sighs, Webster's dirge, the corpse in the garden, Frazer's buried god. A short walk through the section's underworld, with the source passages open.
---
kind: draft
title: Read what was cut
lens: drafts
body: The section did not begin with April. It began with fifty-four lines of a night out in Boston. The drafts view shows where the cut fell, and what the poem gained by starting exhausted.
---
kind: lens
title: Leave the German alone
lens: tongues
body: Four lines of Wagner and one café sentence are left untranslated. Switch on Tongues to see the translations, then switch it off and read the section as someone in 1922 without German would have read it: as sound, and as a wall.
---
kind: ask
title: Three questions with no answer
body: When were you "neither living nor dead"? What corpse have you planted in a garden, and have you checked on it? Whose shadow at evening rises to meet you? The section asks them; it does not answer. Neither need you, yet.
===
part: 2
title: Nine ways into A Game of Chess
---
kind: listen
title: Hear the two women
lens: listen
body: Fiona Shaw's performance is the one to watch for this section: the woman in the chair and the woman in the pub are the same voice at two social altitudes. Alec Guinness does the pub with a Cockney the Eliots' maid would have recognised.
---
kind: source
title: Start with Cleopatra's barge
source: shakespeare-antony
body: Read Enobarbus's speech, then lines 77–110 with it beside you. Every gilded thing in the room is a thing from the river; every change Eliot makes is a demotion. Then find the other two queens hidden in the furniture, Dido and Eve.
---
kind: lens
title: Read the man's silence
lens: voices
body: With Voices on, everything in quotation marks is hers and everything without is his, unspoken. His answers are all quotations. Read only his lines, in order, and you have a man made entirely of other people's sentences.
---
kind: do
title: Play the rag
lens: listen
body: "That Shakespearian Rag" was a real song of 1912 and there are recordings. Play it, then read lines 128–130 with Eliot's extra syllable in "Shakespeherian." The syncopation is the joke, and the joke is what he hums instead of speaking.
---
kind: source
title: Find Philomel above the mantel
source: ovid-met6
body: Ovid's story of Tereus, Procne and Philomela, and Lyly's "jug jug" song, are the two texts under lines 99–103. Read them, then trace the nightingale through the rest of the poem: lines 203–206, and the swallow at 429.
---
kind: lens
title: Watch the clock
lens: clock
body: Hot water at ten, a closed car at four, to-night, tomorrow, HURRY UP PLEASE ITS TIME, good night. The section is a timetable. The clock shows every hour the poem names; most of them are here.
---
kind: draft
title: Vivien's pencil
lens: drafts
body: Eliot's wife read this section in typescript, wrote "WONDERFUL" beside the pub scene, supplied one of its best lines, and asked for one line about "ivory men" to be removed. The drafts view says which.
---
kind: path
title: The women, one after another
path: women
body: A lady on a throne, a raped girl turned bird, a woman with bad nerves, Lil with her teeth out, her friend, Ophelia. Eliot's note says "all the women are one woman." Walk through them and decide whether you believe him.
---
kind: ask
title: Who is not in the room
body: Two people in a room; one talks, one thinks. Who is not there? Whose bones were lost in rats' alley? Whose eyes were the pearls? The section's subject is an absence, and the quotations are its shape.
===
part: 3
title: Eleven ways into The Fire Sermon
---
kind: map
title: Follow the river
lens: places
body: From the river's tent by Richmond to Greenwich Reach past the Isle of Dogs, by way of a canal behind a gasworks, the Cannon Street Hotel, the Strand, Lower Thames Street and St Magnus Martyr. The map has the real course of the Thames; the section moves down it with the tide.
---
kind: source
title: Spenser first, then the litter
source: spenser-prothalamion
body: Read the first stanza of the Prothalamion with its refrain, then lines 173–184. Eliot keeps the refrain and takes away the wedding, the nymphs and the flowers, and lists instead what is not in the river.
---
kind: lens
title: See who speaks
lens: voices
body: Voices marks the fisher on the bank, a soldiers' song, Verlaine, a nightingale, Mr. Eugenides, Tiresias, the typist, the walker, three Thames-daughters, Augustine and the Buddha. It is the section with the most voices and the most quotation marks, and the one Eliot said Tiresias holds together.
---
kind: source
title: The Fire Sermon itself
source: buddha-fire-sermon
body: Read the Buddha's sermon, in the translation Eliot used at Harvard, before the section, not after. Then everything in it, the river, the typist, the barges, the cities, is seen "on fire," and the four "burning"s at the end are the sermon's own refrain.
---
kind: draft
title: Pound's pencil on the typist
lens: drafts
body: The typist scene was longer, and Pound cut it hard, writing "verse not interesting enough as verse to warrant so much of it" in the margin. The drafts view shows his marks and the seventy lines of Popean couplets about Fresca that once opened the section.
---
kind: listen
title: The Rhine-daughters, then the Thames-daughters
lens: listen
body: Wagner's three Rhine-daughters open the last act of Götterdämmerung singing "Weialala leia." Listen to the scene, then read lines 266–306 aloud. The short lines are a swimming rhythm, and the three modern daughters take their turns like the three in the opera.
---
kind: place
title: Margate
lens: places
body: "On Margate Sands. / I can connect / Nothing with nothing." Eliot wrote much of this section in a public shelter on the Margate seafront in October 1921, on sick leave from the bank. The shelter is still there. The map has it.
---
kind: lens
title: Fire
lens: water
body: The Water lens has a third colour for fire. In this section it appears at the end only, and all at once. Watch the river run through the whole section and then burn.
---
kind: path
title: Every bird
path: birds
body: The nightingale, the gulls, the cock, the hermit-thrush, the swallow. The birds are the poem's musicians, and their songs are all complaints. A short walk with recordings where they exist.
---
kind: source
title: Tiresias in Ovid
source: ovid-met3
body: Eliot's note quotes nineteen lines of Latin about a man who was a woman for seven years. Read them in English, then read the typist scene again as something seen by a person who has been both people on the divan.
---
kind: ask
title: Whose side is the poem on
body: The typist "smooths her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone." Contempt, or the only mercy in the poem? Readers have argued for a century. Read lines 249–256 once each way.
===
part: 4
title: Six ways into Death by Water
---
kind: source
title: Read it in French first
source: dans-le-restaurant
body: The ten lines are Eliot's translation of the last stanza of his own French poem of 1918. Read the French, then the English, and note what was dropped (the tin, Cornwall) and what was added ("Gentile or Jew").
---
kind: draft
title: The eighty-three drowned lines
lens: drafts
body: In the typescript the section was a ninety-three-line voyage out of Gloucester, Massachusetts, ending on an iceberg. Pound cut it to ten and then had to talk Eliot out of cutting those too: "Phlebas is an integral part of the poem . . . Must stay in."
---
kind: path
title: The drowned, from the cards to the current
path: water
body: "Fear death by water" (55), the pearls that were his eyes (48, 125), Ophelia (172), the king's wreck (191), and then Phlebas. The Water pathway collects them.
---
kind: source
title: Ariel's song
source: shakespeare-tempest
body: "Full fathom five thy father lies." Read the song, then Phlebas. Shakespeare's drowned man suffers "a sea-change / Into something rich and strange." Eliot's has his bones picked in whispers. Decide which is the kinder.
---
kind: listen
title: Ten lines, six readers
lens: listen
body: The section is short enough to compare readers: Eliot's flat delivery, Guinness's elegy, Irons's, Shaw's. Listen to all the versions you can find, back to back. It takes a few minutes and it tells you what tone the lines will bear.
---
kind: ask
title: Consider Phlebas
body: "Consider Phlebas, who was once handsome and tall as you." The last line addresses whoever turns a wheel and looks to windward: sailors, drivers, anyone steering anything. Consider him. That is the whole instruction.
===
part: 5
title: Twelve ways into What the Thunder Said
---
kind: listen
title: Eliot reading the thunder
lens: listen
body: Eliot's own recordings are at their strangest here: the "DA" is spoken flat, without drama, and "Shantih" three times as a murmur. Listen to the last thirty lines before reading the notes on them.
---
kind: lens
title: Water at last
lens: water
body: The section is the poem's longest drought and its only rain. The Water lens shows twenty-nine lines of rock and then, at line 395, "Bringing rain." Everything after that is wet.
---
kind: do
title: Hear the hermit-thrush
lens: listen
body: "Drip drop drip drop drop drop drop." Eliot's note names the bird and quotes the field guide. A recording is in the listening room. Play it, then read line 358. His transcription is accurate.
---
kind: source
title: The road to Emmaus, and South Georgia
source: shackleton-south
body: Two texts under "Who is the third who walks always beside you?": Luke 24 and Shackleton's account of crossing South Georgia in 1916, when three men each felt a fourth. Read both. The hooded figure is a risen god and a hallucination of exhausted men, and the poem will not say which.
---
kind: lens
title: Eight languages
lens: tongues
body: The last eight lines are in English, Italian, Latin, French, English, English, Sanskrit, Sanskrit, with Provençal in the note. Switch on Tongues, read the translations, then read the lines again as a person who has none of them: as a noise made by a civilisation.
---
kind: do
title: Unstack the fragments
lens: fragments
body: "These fragments I have shored against my ruins." The Fragments view pulls the last lines apart one at a time, each back to its source, so that you can see the wall the poet says he propped them against.
---
kind: source
title: What the thunder actually said
source: brihadaranyaka
body: Read the Upanishad's fable: one syllable, three hearers, three meanings. Then notice that Eliot changes the order of the commands and confesses, after each, how it was not kept.
---
kind: map
title: From Margate to the Ganges
lens: places
body: The section's places are Jerusalem, Athens, Alexandria, Vienna, London, the Ganges and the Himalaya. The world map draws the poem's journey east in a single line.
---
kind: path
title: Every 'nothing'
path: nothing
body: "I knew nothing," "Nothing again nothing," "Do you know nothing?," "Nothing with nothing," "expect / Nothing." The pathway follows the word through the poem to its last appearance.
---
kind: draft
title: OK from here on
lens: drafts
body: Pound's note at the head of the section reads "OK from here on I think." He cut almost nothing. Eliot wrote it in a few weeks at Lausanne and said afterwards it was "the only part that justifies the whole, at all."
---
kind: lens
title: The last voices
lens: voices
body: The Fisher King, a nursery rhyme, Dante, an anonymous Latin poet, Nerval, the poet himself, Kyd's mad Hieronimo, an Upanishad. Voices colours each of the last eight lines differently. Read them as a chorus and as a man talking to himself.
---
kind: ask
title: Peace, or the word for it
body: "Shantih shantih shantih." A benediction, or a man saying "peace" until he sleeps? Eliot's 1922 note called the English "a feeble translation." Read the last line once as a blessing and once as a lullaby, and keep the one that lets you close the book.
