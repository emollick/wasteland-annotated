part: 1
title: Ways into The Burial of the Dead
---
kind: listen
title: Hear it before you read it
lens: listen
body: Virginia Woolf, who heard Eliot read the poem at dinner in June 1922, wrote in her diary that "he sang it & chanted it rhythmed it." Start with his own voice: the listening room has his recordings. Then read the seventy-six lines silently and notice where his stresses fell.
---
kind: lens
title: Count the speakers
lens: voices
body: Switch on Voices and the section splits into ten: the poem's own voice, Marie, a voice in German, a prophet, a sailor and a shepherd from Wagner, a girl with hyacinths, someone who could not speak, a clairvoyante, and a walker in the City. The typescript of this section was headed "He Do the Police in Different Voices", a phrase from Dickens. For each voice, ask whether it is quoting someone else.
---
kind: do
title: Turn the tarot cards
lens: cards
body: Madame Sosostris turns up six cards, fails to find a seventh, and reports one vision. Turn them over one at a time on the table and read the note on each. Then ask what she leaves out. The seventh card, the one she cannot find, is the Hanged Man, and the only water in her reading is the kind that drowns: a drowned sailor, and "Fear death by water."
---
kind: map
title: Follow the route in Part I
lens: places
body: Lines 60–68 follow a route: over London Bridge, "up the hill and down King William Street", and past St Mary Woolnoth as its clock strikes nine. It is about half a mile, the way into the City that thousands of clerks walked every morning from London Bridge station. Eliot's bank stood at the end of it, and his note on line 68 calls the dead sound on the final stroke "a phenomenon which I have often noticed". Follow it on the map, then read the lines again with the walk in mind.
---
kind: source
title: Put Chaucer beside it
source: chaucer
body: Read the first twelve lines of the General Prologue, then Eliot's first seven. Both begin with April, with rain, and with roots. Decide what has changed between Chaucer's April and Eliot's.
---
kind: lens
title: Follow the water
lens: water
body: Part I names water, or the lack of it, again and again: spring rain and forgetful snow in the first seven lines, a shower over the Starnbergersee, a dry stone with no sound of water, a handful of dust, a drowned sailor, and then "Fear death by water." The Water lens tints the lines that name water and the lines that name its absence.
---
kind: path
title: Meet the dead
path: dead
body: The Dead pathway goes through Part I with the sources open beside the lines: Dante's crowd at the gate of Hell and the sighs in Limbo, Webster's dirge, the corpse in Stetson's garden, and the buried god of Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i>.
---
kind: draft
title: Read what was cut
lens: drafts
body: The section did not begin with April. In the typescript it began with fifty-four lines about a night out in Boston, which were cut. The drafts view shows where the cut fell. Read the cancelled opening and then line 1, and decide what the change did.
---
kind: lens
title: Read it without the translations
lens: tongues
body: Five lines of Wagner (31–34 and 42), a sentence of German at line 12 and a line of French at line 76 are left untranslated. Switch on Tongues to see what they say, then switch it off and read the section as a reader with no German or French would have read it in 1922, hearing those lines without understanding them.
---
kind: ask
title: Three questions with no answer
body: Part I asks questions it does not answer: what roots clutch and what branches grow out of this stony rubbish (19–20); where are you lingering (34, in Wagner's German); has the corpse in the garden begun to sprout (72). One way in is to read the section with those questions open.

kind: do
title: The rag of 1912
lens: listen
body: "That Shakespearian Rag" was a real song of 1912. No recording of the day has been found; the sheet music is in the listening room. Read its chorus, then lines 128–130 with Eliot's extra syllable in "Shakespeherian": it gives the word the song's syncopation, and the man hums it instead of answering.
sources: Buck, Ruby and Stamper, "That Shakespearian Rag" (1912)
Guerrieri (2017)
---
kind: source
title: Find Philomel above the mantel
source: ovid-met6
body: Eliot's note sends you to Ovid's story of Tereus, Procne and Philomela; John Lyly's nightingale song from his play <i>Campaspe</i> (1584) is the plainest example of the Elizabethan convention, the nightingale's "jug jug", that line 103 uses. Read both, then follow the nightingale through the rest of the poem: lines 203–206, and the swallow at line 429.
sources: Ovid, Metamorphoses VI 424–674
Lyly, Campaspe V.i

kind: lens
title: Watch the clock
lens: clock
body: The poem gives three hours by the clock; two of them are here, ten and four (lines 135–136), and the third is nine, at line 68. The section also has "tonight", "tomorrow" and closing time, called five times. The Clock lens marks every time of day the poem names.

kind: path
title: The women, one after another
path: women
body: The lady in the chair, who speaks at line 111; Philomel, raped and turned into a bird; Lil, and the friend who tells her story; Ophelia. Eliot's note to line 218 says "all the women are one woman". Walk through them and decide whether you believe him.
---
kind: ask
title: Who is not in the room
body: Two people in a room; one talks, one thinks. Who is not there? Whose bones were lost in rats' alley? Whose eyes were the pearls? Two of his unspoken answers point to the dead: the men of the trenches, and the drowned father in Ariel's song from <i>The Tempest</i>.
sources: Shakespeare, The Tempest I.ii
Chasseaud (2006)

part: 3
title: Ways into The Fire Sermon
---
kind: map
title: Follow the river
lens: places
body: The section names, in order, a canal behind a gashouse, the Cannon Street Hotel, the Strand, Queen Victoria Street and Lower Thames Street with St Magnus Martyr, Greenwich Reach and the Isle of Dogs, and in the daughters' speeches Highbury, Richmond and Kew, Moorgate and Margate. The map has the real course of the Thames, with each of these on it.
---
kind: source
title: Spenser's refrain
source: spenser-prothalamion
body: Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596) is a poem for a double wedding on the Thames, and its refrain, "Sweete Themmes runne softly, till I end my Song", closes each stanza. Read its first stanza, then lines 173–184. Eliot takes the refrain at 176 and 183 and varies it at 184; Spenser's nymphs gather flowers by the river, Eliot's "are departed", and his river "bears no" bottles, papers or cigarette ends.
---
kind: lens
title: See who speaks
lens: voices
body: The Voices lens marks each speaker and each quoted voice: the "I" who fishes by the canal, the Mrs. Porter song, Verlaine's line, the nightingale, Mr. Eugenides (reported, not quoted), Tiresias, the typist, the "I" of Lower Thames Street, the three Thames-daughters, Augustine and the Buddha. Tiresias speaks only in this section; Eliot's note calls him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest".
---
kind: source
title: The Fire Sermon itself
source: buddha-fire-sermon
body: Eliot's note sends the reader to the Buddha's Fire Sermon in Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896) and calls it as important as the Sermon on the Mount. It is short. In it the Buddha says of the eye, the ear, the tongue, the body and the mind that each "is on fire", with the fire of passion, of hatred and of infatuation. The section's title is the sermon's, and "burning", five times at 308–311, is the poem's word for the sermon's "on fire".
---
kind: draft
title: Pound's pencil on the typist
lens: drafts
body: In the typescript the typist scene was longer, in regular quatrains, and Pound cut it heavily, writing in the margin that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it. The drafts page describes his marks, and the seventy or so lines of couplets in Pope's manner, about a fashionable woman called Fresca, that opened the section until he struck them out.
---
kind: listen
title: The Rhine-daughters, then the Thames-daughters
lens: listen
body: Act III of Wagner's <i>Götterdämmerung</i> opens with the three Rhine-daughters swimming and singing of their stolen gold; "Weialala leia, wallala leialala" is their refrain, and the Thames-daughters take it at 277–278 and 290–291. Listen to the scene, then read lines 266–306. Eliot's note says his three daughters "speak in turn" from 292 to 306.
---
kind: place
title: Margate
lens: places
body: "On Margate Sands. / I can connect / Nothing with nothing." Eliot spent three weeks at Margate, from late October to mid-November 1921, on three months' sick leave from Lloyds Bank. In a letter from there, to Sydney Schiff, he said he had drafted "some fifty lines" of this part, sitting in a shelter on the front. The Nayland Rock shelter on the seafront, listed in 2009 as the likely place, still stands. The map has it.
---
kind: lens
title: Fire
lens: water
body: The Water lens marks fire in a third colour. In this section it falls on the last four lines only, where "burning", from the Buddha's sermon of the title, follows Augustine's "To Carthage then I came". Eliot's note says the placing of these two together "is not an accident".
---
kind: path
title: Every bird
path: birds
body: The Birds pathway stops at each bird in the poem in order: the nightingale (here at 203–206, and in Part II), the gulls of Part IV, the hermit-thrush and the cock of Part V, and the swallow at 429, with recordings where they exist.
---
kind: source
title: Tiresias in Ovid
source: ovid-met3
body: Eliot's note at 218 quotes nineteen lines of Ovid (<i>Metamorphoses</i> III, 320–338). Tiresias, who had lived seven years as a woman, is asked to settle Jupiter and Juno's dispute over which sex has the greater pleasure in love. Juno blinds him for his answer and Jupiter gives him foresight. The library gives lines 324–331, on the serpents he struck and the change of sex that followed, in Latin and English. Read them, then the typist scene, which Tiresias says he has "foresuffered".
---
kind: ask
title: Whose side is the poem on
body: The typist "smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone" (255–256). Eliot's note gives Goldsmith's song, in which the woman who "stoops to folly" has nothing left but to die; Eliot's typist, instead, puts a record on. Critics have read the scene as contempt for her, and as the nearest the poem comes to pity; those who read it as pity point to Tiresias's "foresuffered all" (243).

---
kind: source
title: Read it in French first
source: dans-le-restaurant
body: The ten lines are Eliot's translation of the last stanza of his own French poem "Dans le Restaurant", published in 1918. Read the French, then the English, and note what was dropped (the tin, Cornwall, the offhand "Figurez-vous donc") and what was added ("Gentile or Jew", and the whole address to the man at the wheel).

kind: draft
title: The voyage Pound cut
lens: drafts
body: In the typescript this section was a sea voyage of some ninety lines, from the Dry Salvages off Cape Ann to the fishing banks, ending in ice. Pound cut it to the last ten lines and then had to talk Eliot out of cutting those too: "Phlebas is an integral part of the poem . . . Must stay in."
sources: Facsimile (1971), the typescript of Part IV, and p. 129
Pound, Letters, ed. Paige, no. 183

---
kind: path
title: The drowned before Phlebas
path: water
body: Before it reaches Phlebas the poem has warned of drowning and shown the drowned: "Fear death by water" (55); the pearls that were his eyes (48, 125); Ophelia's good night (172), said not long before she drowns; and "the king my brother's wreck" (191). The Water pathway takes them in order.
sources: Shakespeare, The Tempest I.ii
Shakespeare, Hamlet IV.v and IV.vii
---
kind: source
title: Ariel's song
source: shakespeare-tempest
body: "Full fathom five thy father lies." Read Ariel's song, then Phlebas. Shakespeare's drowned man is not in fact drowned, and the song has him suffer "a sea-change / Into something rich and strange". Eliot's Phlebas is a fortnight dead, and the current picks his bones in whispers.
sources: Shakespeare, The Tempest I.ii
---
kind: listen
title: Hear it read
lens: listen
body: Ten lines are short enough to hear several readers one after another. The listening room has six readers of Part IV: Eliot himself (in 1933, and again in the 1940s), Alec Guinness, Jeremy Irons, Fiona Shaw, Edoardo Ballerini and Elizabeth Klett. Start with Eliot's own reading and hear the others against it.
sources: The Poetry Archive
PennSound
---
kind: ask
title: Consider Phlebas
body: "Consider Phlebas, who was once handsome and tall as you." The last three lines turn from the drowned man to whoever is at a ship's wheel, "Gentile or Jew", and through him to the reader. In 1956 Herbert Musurillo compared the section to the epitaphs for drowned sailors in the <i>Greek Anthology</i>, which speak to the sailor passing by. The last line has the shape of the warning on an old gravestone: as you are now, so once was I.
sources: Greek Anthology VII 282
Musurillo (1956)
Deutsch (1952)

title: Water in Part V
lens: water
body: The Water lens tints every line that is about water or the want of it. From line 331 to line 394 there is no water. title: Hear the hermit-thrush
lens: listen
body: "Drip drop drip drop drop drop drop." Eliot's note names the bird and quotes Chapman's field guide on its song; "water-dripping" is not Chapman's phrase. A recording is in the listening room. Play it, then read line 358.
sources: Chapman, Handbook (1895), p. 400

kind: source

source: shackleton-south
body: Eliot gives two sources for "Who is the third who walks always beside you?": in his headnote, the journey to Emmaus of Luke 24; in his note on the line, Shackleton's account in <i>South</i> of crossing South Georgia in May 1916, when each of three men felt a fourth walking with them. Both are in the library.

title: OK from here on

body: Pound's note at the head of the typescript of Part V reads "OK from here on I think," and he cut almost nothing. Eliot wrote the section at Lausanne between late November 1921 and the New Year, and told Bertrand Russell in October 1923 that it was "the only part that justifies the whole, at all."

kind: lens
title: The last voices
lens: voices
body: The Voices lens gives lines 424 to 426 to the Fisher King, lines 427 to 432 to the fragments, and the last two lines to the Upanishad. The fragments are, in order, a nursery rhyme, Dante, a Latin poet and Tennyson, Nerval, the poet himself, Kyd's Hieronimo.
---
kind: ask
title: The last word
body: "Shantih shantih shantih." Eliot's note calls it the formal ending of an Upanishad; in 1922 he called "The Peace which passeth understanding" a feeble translation of the word, and from 1925 "our equivalent." Is the line a blessing on the poem, or a man saying the word to himself?
sources: Poems 1909–1925, The Waste Land, the note on line 433
