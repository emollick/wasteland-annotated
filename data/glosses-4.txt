Consider then, it was a hard fate; and yet he was once a handsome man, and tall.
body:
The ten lines of "Death by Water" are Eliot's own English version of the last stanza of his French poem "Dans le Restaurant," written in 1918. The section is the shortest in the poem and the only one with no quotation from anyone else, unless you count the poet quoting himself. Phlebas is the drowned Phoenician Sailor of Madame Sosostris's pack, the merchant who "melts into" him, Ferdinand's drowned father with pearls for eyes, and Frazer's effigy of the god thrown into the sea at Alexandria so that the year can turn. "Gentile or Jew" is St Paul's formula for everyone. "O you who turn the wheel" is the helmsman and the Wheel of Fortune at once. The warning "Fear death by water" is fulfilled, and the fulfilment is oddly gentle: a current, whispers, the stages of his age and youth passed through backwards.

title: Eighty-three lines that went overboard
body:
In the typescript "Death by Water" ran to ninety-three lines. It began as a narrative of a fishing schooner sailing out of Gloucester, Massachusetts, past the Dry Salvages, north to the Banks, in a voice modelled on Dante's Ulysses (<i>Inferno</i> XXVI) and Tennyson's: the crew, the drink, a night of "three knots," an iceberg, and the wreck. Pound cut all of it except the ten lines that stand, and marked the sea narrative with the single word "Bad." Eliot, shaken, wrote back asking "Perhaps better omit Phlebas also???" Pound's reply is the most quoted sentence in the story of the poem: "I DO advise keeping Phlebas. In fact I more'n advise. Phlebas is an integral part of the poem; the card pack introduces him, the drowned phoen. sailor, and he is needed ABSOloootly where he is. Must stay in."
