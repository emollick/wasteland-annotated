id: torchlight
line: 322
to: 330
anchor: After the torchlight red on sweaty faces
kind: echo
source: luke24
title: The Passion, in nine lines, without the resurrection
body:
Gethsemane by torchlight (the arrest, John 18:3), "the frosty silence in the gardens," "the agony in stony places," the shouting of the crowd, "Prison and palace" (Caiaphas's house, Pilate's), the thunder and earthquake at the crucifixion. It is Good Friday, told in the past tense by people who were there. "He who was living is now dead / We who were living are now dying / With a little patience." No Easter follows. Eliot's headnote to Part V names three themes: "the journey to Emmaus, the approach to the Chapel Perilous (see Miss Weston's book) and the present decay of eastern Europe." He wrote the section at Lausanne in a few weeks, and, unlike everything before it, Pound barely touched it: his note at its head reads "OK from here on I think." Eliot told Bertrand Russell in October 1923 that Part V was "not only the best part, but the only part that justifies the whole, at all."

id: no-water
line: 331
to: 359
anchor: Here is no water but only rock
kind: gloss
title: The road through the mountains
body:
Twenty-nine lines with almost no punctuation and no full stops, driven by the two words "rock" and "water" as if the speaker could think of nothing else, because he can't. It is the road to Emmaus, the approach to the Grail chapel through a waste land, the retreat through the Carpathians of a beaten army, and a man's own dry mouth: "Dead mountain mouth of carious teeth that cannot spit." The hymn of "If there were water" that follows (lines 346–358) is the poem's only stretch of pure longing, and it is built on a sound the speaker cannot hear.

Eliot's note on the hermit-thrush is the most personal in the apparatus: <i>Turdus aonalaschkae pallasii</i>, "which I have heard in Quebec County," and Chapman's <i>Handbook of Birds of Eastern North America</i> on its "purity and sweetness of tone." Its "water-dripping song" is "justly celebrated": "Drip drop drip drop drop drop drop / But there is no water." The thrush is also Whitman's, from the lilac poem behind line 2, the bird that sings the carol of death in the swamp. Listen to a recording and the poem's onomatopoeia turns out to be exact.

id: third

anchor: Who is the third who walks always beside you?
kind: echo
source: shackleton-south
title: Emmaus, and the men on South Georgia
quote: I know that during that long and racking march of thirty-six hours over the unnamed mountains and glaciers of South Georgia it seemed to me often that we were four, not three.
body:
Luke 24: two disciples walking to Emmaus on the third day are joined by a stranger who explains the scriptures to them and whom they recognise only when he breaks bread, at which point he vanishes. "Their eyes were holden that they should not know him." Eliot's note adds the second source, from memory ("I forget which, but I think one of Shackleton's"): the crossing of South Georgia in May 1916 by Shackleton, Worsley and Crean at the end of the <i>Endurance</i> expedition, told in Shackleton's <i>South</i> (1919), where each of the three men privately felt a fourth walking with them. The hooded figure, "I do not know whether a man or a woman," is the Hanged Man that Madame Sosostris could not find, and also Tiresias, and also nobody: "When I count, there are only you and I together." The question is asked and, as in the Grail castle, not answered.

Hermann Hesse's <i>Blick ins Chaos</i> (<i>A Glimpse into Chaos</i>, 1920), three essays on Dostoevsky, quoted in Eliot's note. Then the roll-call: "Jerusalem Athens Alexandria / Vienna London," the cities that were each in turn the centre of the world, each "Unreal," the word from line 60 now standing on its own with no city after it. London is the last name on the list.

id: bats
line: 378
to: 385
anchor: A woman drew her long black hair out tight
kind: draft
title: A nightmare from an older notebook
body:
These eight lines predate the rest of the poem. They come from a fragment in Eliot's papers that begins "So through the evening, through the violet air / One tortured meditation dragged me on," printed in the facsimile edition, and the images are Gothic and painterly: the woman fiddling on her own hair, the bats with baby faces, the towers upside down in air, bells that "kept the hours" like St Mary Woolnoth's, and voices "singing out of empty cisterns and exhausted wells," which is Jeremiah's "broken cisterns, that can hold no water." The violet hour of Part III has become "violet light." The poem's word for the whole passage would be the one it used of Philomel's picture: a "withered stump of time," a picture on the wall of the mind.

id: chapel
line: 389
to: 395
anchor: There is the empty chapel, only the wind’s home.
kind: echo
source: weston
title: The Chapel Perilous
body:
Weston's chapter "The Perilous Chapel" collects the episode in which the questing knight must spend a night in a chapel in a waste place where a dead body lies on the altar, a black hand puts out the candle, and voices and storms assail him; survive it, and he is fit for the Grail. Eliot's chapel is worse than perilous: it is empty, windowless, the door swinging, "Dry bones can harm no one," the test already over. Then a cock on the rooftree crows "Co co rico," in French, the cock that crowed for Peter's denial and the "bird of dawning" that sends ghosts back to their graves in the first scene of <i>Hamlet</i>. Lightning, "a damp gust / Bringing rain." Three words. It is the only rain in the poem that actually falls.

id: ganga
line: 396
to: 401
anchor: Ganga was sunken
kind: tongue
lang: Sanskrit
title: The river and the mountain
body:
Ganga is the Ganges; Himavant, "the snowy," is the Himalaya, the mountain father of the goddess Ganga in the Puranas. The scene moves in a line from a Kentish estuary and a London bridge to the Indian monsoon, the whole subcontinent crouched "humped in silence" waiting for the rains, and then the thunder speaks, in the syllable of a language Eliot had studied at Harvard for two years under Charles Lanman and never pretended to command. In <i>After Strange Gods</i> (1934) he said the Sanskrit and the year of Patañjali's metaphysics had left him "in a state of enlightened mystification."

id: da

to: 402
anchor: DA
kind: echo
source: brihadaranyaka
lang: Sanskrit
title: What the thunder said
quote: The threefold offspring of Prajāpati, gods, men and demons, dwelt with their father as students. When they had finished, the gods said: Teach us. He spoke one syllable, “Da,” and asked: Did you understand? They said: We understood; you told us, <i>dāmyata</i>, restrain yourselves. Then the men: Teach us. “Da.” We understood; you told us, <i>datta</i>, give. Then the demons: Teach us. “Da.” We understood; you told us, <i>dayadhvam</i>, be compassionate. body:
<i>Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad</i> 5.2, paraphrased here from the Sanskrit; Eliot's note gives Deussen's German translation as his source. The thunder says one syllable and each hearer understands the instruction he most needs. Eliot changes the order to Datta, Dayadhvam, Damyata (give, sympathise, control), and each word is followed by a confession in the poem's own voice of how the instruction has been failed. The section's title, and the poem's ending, come from a fable about hearing what you are able to hear.

<i>Inferno</i> XXXIII: Count Ugolino, locked in a tower at Pisa with his sons and grandsons to starve, hears the door nailed shut. Eliot's note adds a page of F. H. "A broken Coriolanus," Shakespeare's general who could not bend, is revived "for a moment" by "aetherial rumours" at nightfall, and only for a moment. Sympathy is the instruction; solitude is the report.

id: damyata
line: 419
to: 423
anchor: The boat responded
kind: gloss
title: Control: a boat off Cape Ann
body:
The third instruction gets the poem's only memory of things going well: a sailing boat answering "Gaily, to the hand expert with sail and oar," the sea calm. Eliot had sailed off Gloucester and Cape Ann as a boy, and the Dry Salvages of his later poem lie off that coast. Then the grammar turns conditional: "your heart would have responded / Gaily, when invited." Would have. The most intimate address in the poem, to a "you" who is never named, is a sentence about what did not happen.

Eliot's note sends you to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King, the wounded keeper of the Grail who fishes because he can do nothing else, while his land lies waste behind him. id: fragments
line: 431
to: 434
anchor: These fragments I have shored against my ruins
kind: gloss
title: The last eight lines, one by one
body:
<b>London Bridge is falling down falling down falling down.</b> The nursery rhyme, whose verses try one material after another (wood and clay, bricks and mortar, iron and steel, silver and gold) and whose oldest versions end with setting a watchman, or, in the folklorists' reading that Frazer would have relished, burying someone in the foundations. The bridge the commuters crossed at line 62.

<b>Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina.</b> "Then he hid himself in the fire that refines them." <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, 148: Arnaut Daniel, having spoken to Dante in his own Provençal (the only lines in the <i>Comedy</i> not in Italian), steps back into the purifying flame. He goes willingly. Eliot's note quotes Arnaut's speech; its first words, "Ara vos prec," were the title of Eliot's 1920 collection. Set this fire against the burning of the Fire Sermon.

<b>Quando fiam ceu chelidon.</b> "When shall I become like the swallow?" From the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i>, an anonymous Latin poem of the second to fourth century celebrating the eve of Venus's spring festival, whose last stanza suddenly turns on the poet: the swallow, Procne, sings, "when shall I be like the swallow, so that I may stop being silent?" The 1922 printings have "ceu"; the Latin poem has "uti," and Eliot's later editions restored it. "O swallow swallow" is Tennyson's, from <i>The Princess</i>: "O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South." Philomel's sister, still singing.

<b>Le Prince d'Aquitaine à la tour abolie.</b> The second line of Gérard de Nerval's sonnet "El Desdichado" (1854): "I am the dark one, the widower, the unconsoled, the Prince of Aquitaine with the ruined tower." The Tower is a Tarot card (XVI, struck by lightning), and Nerval's speaker, who has "twice crossed the Acheron," is another man who has been among the dead.

<b>These fragments I have shored against my ruins.</b> The poem's own line, its method confessed in a single sentence: quotations propped like timbers against a wall that is coming down.

<b>Why then Ile fit you. Hieronymo's mad againe.</b> Kyd's <i>The Spanish Tragedy</i> (c. 1587), Act IV. Hieronimo, whose son has been murdered, is asked to write an entertainment for the court and answers "Why then, I'll fit you," meaning both "I'll oblige" and "I'll pay you out"; the play he writes is in four languages, and the murderers, acting in it, are killed for real. "Hieronimo is mad again" was the subtitle on the 1615 title page. A poem in seven languages, written as revenge.

<b>Datta. Dayadhvam. Damyata.</b> The thunder's three words, now without their glosses.

<b>Shantih shantih shantih.</b> The formal ending of an Upanishad, as Eliot's note says, "the Peace which passeth understanding" (Philippians 4:7), for which the 1922 note called the English "a feeble translation" and later notes "our equivalent." Whether the last line is a benediction or a man saying "peace, peace" to himself until he can sleep, the poem does not decide, and neither should you on a first reading.
