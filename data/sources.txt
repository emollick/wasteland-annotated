id: weston
title: From Ritual to Romance
author: Jessie L. Weston
date: 1920
lang: English
kind: anthropology
what: The book Eliot’s headnote credits with the title and the plan: the Fisher King, the waste land, the Chapel Perilous.
lines: 0, 46, 386, 424
note:
Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance. Following Frazer’s <i>Golden Bough</i>, she argued that the Grail stories preserved the remains of an ancient fertility cult: a king whose wound lays his land waste, a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics, and a rite of initiation descended from the mystery cults of Attis and Mithra. Those cults, she says, were carried across the Roman Empire by Syrian merchants, slaves and soldiers (chapter XII). Medievalists have not accepted the argument. Eliot’s headnote says the book suggested “not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem,” and recommends it “to any who think such elucidation of the poem worth the trouble”; in a lecture of 1956 he said he regretted “having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail.” The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), VI (The Symbols, where the Grail hallows become the four suits of the Tarot), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel).

id: frazer
title: The Golden Bough (Adonis, Attis, Osiris)
author: Sir James George Frazer
date: 1906–15 (third edition); one-volume abridgement 1922
lang: English
kind: anthropology
what: The god who dies with the harvest and returns in spring: the Hanged God, the buried effigy, the image thrown into the sea.
lines: 46, 71, 312
note:
Frazer’s twelve-volume study of magic and religion, which Eliot’s headnote says “has influenced our generation profoundly,” argued that the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris tell one story, that of a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by rites in which a king is sacrificed or an effigy is buried or drowned, and that the Church’s Easter was adapted from the spring festivals of Adonis and Attis. The two volumes Eliot names describe the “gardens of Adonis,” pots of quick-sprouting seed carried out with images of the dead god and “flung with them into the sea,” and the effigies of Osiris “made of earth and corn” that the priests buried so that grain would sprout from the god’s body. Eliot’s note on the Tarot says the Hanged Man “is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer”; the corpse planted in Stetson’s garden (line 71) and Phlebas in the current (line 312) have been connected with the same rites. Frazer put a reproduction of Turner’s painting <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1834) at the front of his first edition and opened his first page with it, describing it as a view of Lake Nemi. It in fact shows Lake Avernus, with the Sibyl of Cumae, the prophetess of the poem’s epigraph, holding the bough that admits Aeneas to the underworld.

id: petronius
title: Satyricon, chapter 48
author: Petronius
date: 1st century AD
lang: Latin, with Greek
kind: prose
what: The epigraph: the Sibyl who wanted to die.
lines: 0
passage: Nam Sibyllam quidem Cumis ego ipse oculis meis vidi in ampulla pendere, et cum illi pueri dicerent: Σίβυλλα τί θέλεις; respondebat illa: ἀποθανεῖν θέλω.
trans: For I myself, with my own eyes, saw the Sibyl at Cumae hanging in a jar, and when the boys said to her, “Sibyl, what do you want?” she answered, “I want to die.”
note:
The speaker is Trimalchio, a freed slave grown enormously rich, boasting to his guests between courses; his dinner party is the longest continuous episode in what survives of the <i>Satyricon</i>. The Sibyl of Cumae, in Ovid’s telling (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV), asked Apollo for as many years as there were grains in a heap of dust she had scooped up, and forgot to ask for youth to go with them; she foresees that she will shrink until only her voice is left. The typescript of the poem carried a different epigraph, from Conrad’s <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, ending “The horror! the horror!”; Pound doubted that Conrad was weighty enough for the place, and Eliot put Petronius in its place before publication.

id: dante-purg26
title: Purgatorio, canto XXVI
author: Dante Alighieri
date: c. 1315
lang: Italian and Provençal
kind: poem
what: The dedication to Pound, “il miglior fabbro,” and line 428, Arnaut stepping back into the refining fire.
lines: 0, 428
passage: «Tan m’abellis vostre cortes deman,<br>qu’ieu no me puesc ni voill a vos cobrire.<br>Ieu sui Arnaut, que plor e vau cantan;<br>consiros vei la passada folor,<br>e vei jausen lo joi qu’esper, denan.<br>Ara vos prec, per aquella valor<br>que vos guida al som de l’escalina,<br>sovenha vos a temps de ma dolor!».<br>Poi s’ascose nel foco che li affina.
trans: “So much does your courteous question please me that I cannot and will not hide myself from you. I am Arnaut, who weep and go singing; in thought I see my past folly, and I see with joy, ahead, the joy I hope for. Now I pray you, by that power which guides you to the top of the stair, be mindful in time of my pain.” Then he hid himself in the fire that refines them.
note:
On the seventh terrace of Purgatory, where the lustful walk in flame, Dante meets the poet Guido Guinizelli, who points out the troubadour Arnaut Daniel as “miglior fabbro del parlar materno,” the better craftsman of the mother tongue (line 117). Arnaut answers in his own Provençal, the only passage in the <i>Comedy</i> in a vernacular other than Italian, and steps back into the fire. Eliot took the title of his 1920 collection <i>Ara Vos Prec</i> from line 145 (the Ovid Press title page misprinted it “Ara Vus Prec,” a form Eliot later blamed on his Italian edition of Dante), and line 428 of the poem from line 148, which his note quotes with the three lines before it. The dedication “For Ezra Pound / il miglior fabbro” was not printed in 1922: Eliot wrote it into Pound’s copy of the Boni & Liveright edition in January 1923, and it first appeared in print in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>. The phrase was already Pound’s own: “Il Miglior Fabbro” is the title of his chapter on Arnaut Daniel in <i>The Spirit of Romance</i> (1910). Modern texts read “li affina”; Eliot’s “gli” follows the older text he used.

author: Dante Alighieri
date: c. 1308
lang: Italian
kind: poem
what: The crowd on London Bridge: “I had not thought death had undone so many.”
lines: 62, 63, 56
passage: E io, che riguardai, vidi una ’nsegna<br>che girando correva tanto ratta,<br>che d’ogne posa mi parea indegna;<br>e dietro le venìa sì lunga tratta<br>di gente, ch’i’ non averei creduto<br>che morte tanta n’avesse disfatta.

Inside the gate (“Abandon all hope”) but before the river Acheron, Dante finds the souls who were neither good nor bad, “who lived without infamy and without praise.” Heaven has cast them out and Hell will not receive them; they run for ever after a banner, stung by wasps. Eliot’s note on line 63 quotes lines 55–57 as the source of the crowd flowing over London Bridge, and his note on line 46 says that the “crowds of people” in Madame Sosostris’s cards “appear later,” which has generally been taken to mean this crowd. Eliot said in “What Dante Means to Me” (1950) that Dante’s poetry was still, “after forty years,” “the most persistent and deepest influence” on his own verse.

id: dante-inf4
title: Inferno, canto IV
author: Dante Alighieri
date: c. 1308
lang: Italian
kind: poem
what: Limbo’s sighs, behind “Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled.”
lines: 64
passage: Quivi, secondo che per ascoltare,<br>non avea pianto mai che di sospiri<br>che l’aura etterna facevan tremare.
trans: Here, as far as I could tell by listening, there was no weeping but only sighs, which made the eternal air tremble.
note:
Limbo, the first circle of Hell, holds the virtuous who died unbaptised, Homer, Virgil and Aristotle among them; Dante calls them the “great spirits” (<i>spiriti magni</i>), and they are not tormented but live in desire without hope. Eliot’s note on line 64 cites these lines. The comparison likens the sighs of the crowd on London Bridge to those of Limbo, where there is grief but no torment, not to the cries of the damned.

id: dante-purg5
title: Purgatorio, canto V
author: Dante Alighieri
date: c. 1315
lang: Italian
kind: poem
what: La Pia’s “Siena made me, Maremma unmade me,” the model for “Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me.”
lines: 293
passage: «Deh, quando tu sarai tornato al mondo,<br>e riposato de la lunga via»,<br>seguitò ’l terzo spirito al secondo,<br>«ricorditi di me, che son la Pia;<br>Siena mi fé, disfecemi Maremma:<br>salsi colui che ’nnanellata pria<br>disposando m’avea con la sua gemma».
trans: “Ah, when you have returned to the world and rested from the long way,” the third spirit followed the second, “remember me, who am La Pia; Siena made me, Maremma unmade me: he knows it who, having first put a ring on me, wedded me with his gem.”
note:
On the lower slopes of Purgatory, among those who repented only at a violent death, a woman speaks seven lines and gives her name, her birthplace, the place of her death and, without quite saying it, that her husband had her killed. Eliot’s note on line 293 cites the passage for the first Thames-daughter, whose “Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me” follows the shape of “Siena mi fé, disfecemi Maremma”; unlike La Pia, she gives no name.

id: dante-inf33
title: Inferno, canto XXXIII
author: Dante Alighieri
date: c. 1308
lang: Italian
kind: poem
what: The key turning in the door: Ugolino’s tower.
lines: 412, 413
passage: e io senti’ chiavar l’uscio di sotto<br>a l’orribile torre; ond’io guardai<br>nel viso a’ mie’ figliuoi sanza far motto.
trans: and I heard them nailing up the door below of the horrible tower; at which I looked into the faces of my sons without a word.
note:
Count Ugolino della Gherardesca of Pisa, imprisoned in 1288 with two sons and two grandsons and left to starve, gnaws in Hell on the skull of Archbishop Ruggieri, who betrayed him, and tells Dante the story. The verb in the passage, <i>chiavar</i>, has been read both as locking and as nailing up the door; the translation above has “nailing up,” and Eliot’s “key” takes it as locking. His note on line 412 quotes these lines and then a passage from <i>Appearance and Reality</i> (1893) by F. H. Bradley, the philosopher on whom Eliot wrote his doctoral thesis; in it Bradley says that each person’s experience “falls within my own circle, a circle closed on the outside.”

title: When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom’d
author: Walt Whitman
date: 1865
lang: English
kind: poem
what: Whitman’s elegy for Lincoln: lilacs, spring, and the hermit thrush.
lines: 2, 357
passage: When lilacs last in the dooryard bloom’d,<br>And the great star early droop’d in the western sky in the night,<br>I mourn’d, and yet shall mourn with ever-returning spring. . . .<br><br>In the swamp in secluded recesses,<br>A shy and hidden bird is warbling a song.<br>Solitary the thrush,<br>The hermit withdrawn to himself, avoiding the settlements,<br>Sings by himself a song.<br>Song of the bleeding throat,<br>Death’s outlet song of life . . .

Whitman’s elegy for Lincoln, who was shot in April 1865, brings together the lilac, the evening star and the hermit thrush singing in a swamp. The case that the poem drew on Whitman all the same is made at length in S. Musgrove’s <i>T. S. Eliot and Walt Whitman</i> (1952).
