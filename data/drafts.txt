title: The title that was crossed out
body:
Across the top of the typescript of the first two parts Eliot typed "He Do the Police in Different Voices," from Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i>. "The Waste Land" replaced it, with Weston's Grail country and Malory's "waste land" of the Dolorous Stroke behind it. The Dickens title survives in the notes only as a habit of mind: the poem reads the world's news aloud in every voice it can do.

title: The epigraph that was replaced
body:
Kurtz's "The horror! the horror!" from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i> stood at the head of the typescript. Pound doubted Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation"; Eliot called it "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," and then substituted the Sibyl from Petronius. The change turns a dying man's verdict on his life into an immortal's wish to be done with hers.

id: boston
part: 1
line: 1
title: Before "April": a night in Boston
body:
The typescript of Part I opens with fifty-four lines in a loose, chatty verse about a night out with the boys in Boston: drinks at "Tom's place," a burlesque show, a girl, a run from a policeman, a sunrise walk home. The whole passage is struck through in pencil, apparently by Eliot before Pound saw it. What remains begins already tired, and "April is the cruellest month" is the second first line the poem had. Pound's comments on the rest of Part I are light; he queried a few words and let the section stand.

id: in-the-cage
part: 2
line: 77
title: "In the Cage"
body:
Part II was titled "In the Cage" in the typescript, after Henry James's story of a telegraphist who reads other people's messages, and was renamed "A Game of Chess" before publication. Pound's marginalia on the opening description are the poem's most famous editing: against the ornate lines he wrote "too tum-pum at a stretch," and beside Eliot's "Filled all the desert with inviolable voice" he wrote nothing, because it was right. Where Eliot had written that the two had "met first" in rats' alley, and where the nervous dialogue ran on, Pound cut phrases rather than lines. Vivien Eliot read the section too: she wrote "WONDERFUL" beside the pub scene, marked "Yes" and "& wonderful" in the margins, contributed "What you get married for if you don't want children?" and asked for "The ivory men make company between us" to be removed after line 137. Eliot restored that line from memory in a manuscript copy he made in 1960.

id: fresca
part: 3
line: 173
title: The seventy lines about Fresca
body:
In the typescript "The Fire Sermon" opened with some seventy lines of heroic couplets on a fashionable woman, Fresca: her waking, her breakfast in bed, her visit to the "needful stool" with a novel of Richardson's, her letter, her bath, her reading. Eliot said later that it was written in imitation of Pope's <i>Rape of the Lock</i>; critics also compare Swift's dressing-room poems. Pound struck it out. Eliot recalled in 1946 that Pound's objection was that Pope had done this so well it could not be done better, and that one cannot parody Pope without writing better verse than Pope, "and you can't". The printed section begins with the river. On Rainey's dating of the drafts, its opening lines, 173–182, were the last lines written for the poem, in Paris in January 1922.

id: typist-draft
part: 3
line: 215
title: Pound and the typist
body:
In the typescript the scene ran in regular quatrains and was longer. The draft gave the clerk his age, a spotty face, greasy hair, table manners and boasts, and gave the typist a kimono and a Japanese print. Tiresias hedged with "Perhaps a cheap house agent's clerk", one of those loiterers who might have been seen anywhere. Pound wrote that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it. Against the hedge he wrote "Perhaps be damned", and beside the typist's "one half-formed thought may pass" (printed at 251 as "allows one half-formed thought to pass") he told Tiresias to make up his mind whether he knew or not. The clerk's exit, a couplet in which he stopped at a corner to urinate and spit, was marked by Pound and dropped; the dots at 248 fall where it stood. A passage that stood before the scene, addressed to London ("London, the swarming life you kill and breed"), went too.

id: thames-draft
part: 3
line: 266
title: The Thames-daughters in draft
body:
The river song, the Elizabeth and Leicester lines and the three daughters' speeches are generally taken to be the "some fifty lines" Eliot drafted at Margate in November 1921. They reached print with few marks from Pound, and the words he did write here, "OK" and "echt" (genuine), are approving. Eliot's own revision shortened the first daughter's speech. His note, which identifies three daughters who "speak in turn" from 292 to 306, was added, like all the notes, for the book edition of December 1922.

id: sea-voyage
part: 4
line: 312
title: The voyage that was cut
body:
"Death by Water" was ninety-three lines in the typescript. It began as a sea narrative in the voice of a New England fisherman: a schooner sailing out of Gloucester, Massachusetts, past the Dry Salvages, drink and cards on board, a night of hard sailing, sirens, an iceberg, the wreck. Its models were the Ulysses of <i>Inferno</i> XXVI and Tennyson's "Ulysses." Pound marked most of it "Bad" and cut everything but the last ten lines. Eliot, unnerved, asked "Perhaps better omit Phlebas also???" Pound's reply: "I DO advise keeping Phlebas. In fact I more'n advise. Phlebas is an integral part of the poem; the card pack introduces him, the drowned phoen. sailor, and he is needed ABSOloootly where he is. Must stay in." The folder of drafts also holds "Dirge," a nasty parody of Ariel's song about a drowned Jew named Bleistein, which Pound marked "??doubtful" and which was left out.

id: ok-from-here
part: 5
line: 322
title: "OK from here on I think"
body:
At the head of Part V Pound wrote "OK from here on I think," and cut almost nothing. Eliot wrote the section at Lausanne in November and December 1921, fast, and later said it had come "in a trance." Three of the poem's passages are older than the rest: the woman with the long black hair and the bats with baby faces (lines 378–385) come from a fragment beginning "So through the evening, through the violet air"; the "red rock" of Part I came from "The Death of Saint Narcissus" (1915); and Phlebas from "Dans le Restaurant" (1918).

id: manuscript-fate
part: 5
line: 434
title: What happened to the papers
body:
Eliot gave the typescript, with Pound's and Vivien's pencil on it, to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector who had handled his American publishing, in 1922; Quinn insisted on paying $140 for it. After Quinn's death in 1924 it vanished. His niece sold it to the New York Public Library in 1958, and the library announced the acquisition in 1968, three years after Eliot's death. Valerie Eliot's facsimile edition of 1971 printed every page. The papers are in the Berg Collection; the cuts, and Pound's pencil, are the reason the poem is 434 lines long and not 800.
