id: sweet-thames
line: 176
to: 184
anchor: Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song.
kind: echo
source: spenser-prothalamion
title: Spenser's wedding river
quote: Sweete Themmes runne softly, till I end my Song.
body:
The refrain of Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596), a "spousal verse" for a double wedding in which nymphs gather flowers on the Thames bank and two swans float down to Essex House. Spenser's river is a procession; Eliot's is the morning after. "The nymphs are departed" and so have "their friends, the loitering heirs of city directors," the young men of the summer, leaving no bottles, papers or cigarette ends because the season is over. October: "the last fingers of leaf / Clutch and sink into the wet bank." The whole section was drafted at Margate and Lausanne in the autumn of 1921, and it is the only part of the poem with a season you can feel on your skin.

id: eugenides
line: 209
to: 214
anchor: Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant
kind: gloss
title: The one-eyed merchant, in person
image: smyrna-1920
body:
Eugenides means "well-born." Smyrna, now İzmir, was the great port of Asia Minor and shipped the currants that London's bakers used; a pocketful is a sample. "C.i.f. London: documents at sight" is the trade shorthand for cost, insurance and freight paid to London, with the shipping documents handed over when the sight draft is paid, language Eliot handled daily at Lloyds. (His own note expands the abbreviation slightly wrongly, as "carriage and insurance free," a banker's slip that the editors of the poem have enjoyed ever since.) "Demotic French" is the Levant's trading language. The Cannon Street Hotel stood in front of Cannon Street station, the terminus for the boat trains to the Continent, and was the usual place for City men to meet foreign clients; a weekend at the Metropole, the big hotel on the Brighton seafront, is a proposition, and has generally been read as a homosexual one.

He is the one-eyed merchant of Madame Sosostris's pack (one-eyed because a card shows a face in profile), and, Eliot's note says, he "melts into the Phoenician Sailor." The Phoenicians were the traders of the ancient Mediterranean; Weston argued that the Grail mysteries travelled Europe's trade routes with Syrian merchants. In September 1922, between the poem's completion and its publication, Smyrna burned.

It comes back as "violet air" and "violet light" in the collapsing cities of Part V.

id: tiresias
line: 218
to: 219
anchor: I Tiresias
kind: echo
source: ovid-met3
title: The man who had been a woman
image: tiresias
body:
Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> III, which Eliot's long note quotes in Latin: Tiresias struck two coupling snakes with his stick and was turned into a woman for seven years; striking them again, he became a man. Jupiter and Juno, arguing over which sex has more pleasure in love, called him in as the one witness who knew. He sided with Jupiter (women do). Juno blinded him; Jupiter, unable to undo another god's act, gave him foresight instead. He is the blind prophet of Thebes in <i>Oedipus</i>, and the dead seer Odysseus consults in the underworld: "I who have sat by Thebes below the wall / And walked among the lowest of the dead."

Eliot's note makes him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest . . . the two sexes meet in Tiresias. What Tiresias <i>sees</i>, in fact, is the substance of the poem." The note is the closest thing the poem has to a stated design, and the drafts show that the design came late: Tiresias was there in the typescript, but the claim for him was written for the notes. Take it seriously and the poem has a single consciousness; take it as one more voice and it doesn't. Pound, in the margin of the typescript, was impatient with the seer's hedging: "make up yr. mind you Tiresias if you know know damn well or else you dont."

id: typist
line: 222
to: 248
anchor: The typist home at teatime
kind: gloss
title: The typist and the clerk
body:
The most argued-over scene in the poem. A typist in a bedsit, her underwear ("combinations," one-piece, and "stays") drying out of the window, her dinner from tins, her divan a bed by night; a "small house agent's clerk," pimpled ("carbuncular"), with the confidence of new money, "a silk hat on a Bradford millionaire" (the Yorkshire wool towns made fortunes in the war); a seduction that is really an assault met with indifference. Tiresias watches, "foresuffered all," and blesses the clerk's exit with a rhyme.

It is written in rhymed quatrains, abab, and the rhymes are doing the judging: "guesses / caresses," "tired / undesired," "at once / no response." The typescript had more of them, and Pound cut hard. His marginal verdict on the passage: "verse not interesting enough as verse to warrant so much of it." Against Eliot's tentative "perhaps" in one line he wrote "Perhaps be damned." What survived is the poem's cruellest writing, and the cruelty is aimed as much at the eighteenth-century manner it imitates as at the two people on the divan. Whether Tiresias is compassionate or a voyeur is a question the poem asks about you.

title: Goldsmith's song, and a gramophone
quote: When lovely woman stoops to folly,<br>And finds too late that men betray,<br>What charm can soothe her melancholy,<br>What art can wash her guilt away?<br><br>The only art her guilt to cover,<br>To hide her shame from every eye,<br>To give repentance to her lover,<br>And wring his bosom, is—to die.
body:
Olivia's song in <i>The Vicar of Wakefield</i> (1766), chapter 24, sung after her seduction. In Goldsmith the fallen woman's only art is to die. The gramophone is the poem's newest object, and "automatic" is its most modern word: the eighteenth-century ballad meter runs on into a room where nobody feels anything and a machine does the singing. Whether you hear that as contempt for her or relief for her decides how you read the poem.

id: strand
line: 257
to: 265
anchor: This music crept by me upon the waters
kind: place
image: st-magnus-interior
title: From the Strand to Lower Thames Street
body:
Ferdinand's line from <i>The Tempest</i> again (Eliot's note: "as above"), and then a walk: along the Strand, up Queen Victoria Street, which runs from Blackfriars to the Bank, and down to Lower Thames Street, where Billingsgate fish market stood and the "fishmen" drank at noon. The mandoline is real; so is the pub. And so is St Magnus the Martyr, Wren's church at the foot of the old London Bridge, rebuilt 1671–87, with a white and gold interior of Ionic columns. In 1920 a report of the London County Council, <i>The Proposed Demolition of Nineteen City Churches</i>, listed it for demolition; Eliot's note is a protest, and the church is still there. "Inexplicable splendour" is the first unqualified praise of anything in the poem, and it is given to a building on a street of fish.

id: margate
line: 300
to: 305
anchor: On Margate Sands.
kind: place
image: margate-sands
title: Where the poem was written
body:
The third daughter speaks from Margate, the Kentish seaside resort at the mouth of the Thames estuary. Eliot went there in October 1921, on three months' sick leave from the bank, and stayed until mid-November at the Albemarle Hotel in Cliftonville; he sat in a public shelter on the front, the Nayland Rock shelter, which still stands, and wrote, he told Sydney Schiff on 4 November, "a rough draft of part of part III." "I can connect / Nothing with nothing" is the daughter's confession and the poet's. The "broken fingernails of dirty hands" and "My people humble people who expect / Nothing" are what he saw on the beach at the end of the season. The song runs down to "la la" and stops.

id: carthage
line: 307
to: 311
anchor: To Carthage then I came
kind: echo
source: augustine-confessions
title: Augustine and the Buddha, burning
quote: To Carthage then I came, where a cauldron of unholy loves sang all about mine ears.
body:
The opening of Book III of Augustine's <i>Confessions</i> (c. 400), in E. B. Pusey's translation (1838), the Latin of which puns: Carthago, sartago, a frying pan. Augustine arrived in the city as a student and threw himself into love affairs and the theatre. Then the Buddha's Fire Sermon, which gives the section its title: "All things, O priests, are on fire . . . The eye is on fire; forms are on fire . . . with the fire of passion, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation." Eliot read it at Harvard in Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896). Then Augustine again, Book X: "I entangle my steps with these outward beauties, but Thou pluckest me out, O Lord, Thou pluckest me out."

Eliot's note says that putting "these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism" together "as the culmination of this part of the poem, is not an accident." The lines fall apart as you read them: a sentence, a word four times, a prayer, a prayer with its end torn off, one word alone. The fire that burns here is lust. The fire at line 428 refines.
