The refrain of Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596), a "spousal verse" for a double wedding, in which the poet walks by the Thames and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans that stand for the brides. John Day's <i>The Parliament of Bees</i> (printed 1641), quoted in Eliot's note: a noise of horns and hunting brings Actaeon to Diana bathing. id: eugenides
line: 209
to: 214
anchor: Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant
kind: gloss
title: The one-eyed merchant
image: smyrna-1920
body:
Eugenides is a Greek surname, from <i>eugenēs</i>, well-born. Smyrna, now İzmir, was the chief port of Asia Minor and a centre of the dried-fruit trade. The currants in his pocket are presumably samples. "C.i.f. London: documents at sight" is trade shorthand of the kind Eliot handled at Lloyds Bank. The price covers cost, insurance and freight to London, and the shipping documents are handed over when the sight draft is paid. Eliot's note expands it as "carriage and insurance free", which is not the trade usage. French was the trading language of the Levant, and Mr. Eugenides speaks the "demotic", or colloquial, kind. The Cannon Street Hotel formed the front of Cannon Street station in the City and was much used for business meetings. The Metropole is usually taken as the Hotel Metropole on the Brighton seafront, and the weekend as a proposition, often read as a homosexual one.

Eliot's note to line 218 makes him the "one-eyed merchant, seller of currants" of Madame Sosostris's pack, who "melts into the Phoenician Sailor". His one eye is sometimes explained by the playing cards that show a face in profile. Weston (<i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, chapter XII) counts Syrian merchants, with slaves, officials and soldiers, among the carriers of the mystery cults behind the Grail to the western Roman Empire.

The word returns in Part V, in "the violet air" (373) and "the violet light" (380).

id: tiresias
line: 218
to: 219
anchor: I Tiresias
kind: echo
source: ovid-met3
title: The man who had been a woman
image: tiresias
body:
Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> III, which Eliot's note quotes in Latin. Tiresias struck two coupling snakes with his staff and was turned into a woman; in the eighth year he struck them again and became a man. When Jupiter and Juno argued over which sex has the greater pleasure in love they asked him, as the one who had been both, and he sided with Jupiter, who said women do. Juno blinded him; Jupiter, who could not undo another god's act, gave him foresight instead. He is also the blind prophet of Thebes in Sophocles' <i>Oedipus</i>, and the dead seer Odysseus consults in <i>Odyssey</i> XI: "I who have sat by Thebes below the wall / And walked among the lowest of the dead."

Eliot's note calls him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest", and says "What Tiresias <i>sees</i>, in fact, is the substance of the poem." The claim is Eliot's, made in the notes he wrote for the book edition of December 1922, a year after the scene was typed. In 1956 he said the notes had been expanded to fill out the volume and called them "bogus scholarship". Critics divide: some read the whole poem as what Tiresias sees, others take him as one voice among many and the note as an afterthought. In the poem he names himself three times, at 218, 228 and 243, all in this scene. Pound, in the margin of the typescript, objected to the scene's hedging: beside "Perhaps a cheap house agent's clerk" he wrote "Perhaps be damned", and beside the typist's "one half-formed thought may pass" he told Eliot that Tiresias should "make up yr. mind" whether he knew or not.

id: typist
line: 222
to: 248
anchor: The typist home at teatime
kind: gloss
title: The typist and the clerk
body:
The typist lives in one rented room. Her food comes out of tins, the divan is her bed at night, her "combinations" (a one-piece undergarment) are drying out of the window, and her "stays" (a corset) are piled on the divan with the stockings and camisoles. Her visitor is a clerk in a house agent's (an estate agent's) office; "carbuncular" means pimply. In the simile at 233–234, Bradford is the Yorkshire wool town that stood for fortunes made in the war, and a silk hat is a gentleman's top hat. Eliot's note at line 218 says that what Tiresias sees "is the substance of the poem"; here Tiresias says he has "foresuffered all" and foretells the rest.

In the typescript the scene ran in regular quatrains rhyming abab, and Pound cut several stanzas, writing beside it that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it. What survives keeps the quatrains from 231 to 246 and loosens them before and after. Critics have read Tiresias's watching as compassion and as voyeurism.

id: strand
line: 257
to: 265
anchor: This music crept by me upon the waters
kind: place
image: st-magnus-interior
title: From the Strand to Lower Thames Street
body:
Ferdinand's line in <i>The Tempest</i>, I, ii, from the speech already echoed at lines 191–192, which is what Eliot's "as above" refers to. The music goes along the Strand, up Queen Victoria Street, which runs from Blackfriars to the Bank, and down to Lower Thames Street, where Billingsgate fish market stood until 1982, hence the "fishmen". St Magnus the Martyr is Wren's church at the north end of the old London Bridge, rebuilt after the Great Fire, the body of the church complete by 1676; "Ionian" is usually taken to mean the Ionic columns that divide its nave from the aisles. In April 1920 a commission appointed by the Bishop of London and chaired by Lord Phillimore recommended removing nineteen City churches, St Magnus among them. The London County Council's reply, <i>The Proposed Demolition of Nineteen City Churches</i> (October 1920), is the pamphlet Eliot's note cites. It calls St Magnus "one of the most beautiful of all Wren's works" but finds the interior "disappointing"; Eliot's "to my mind one of the finest among Wren's interiors" takes the other side. The church stands; its interior was redecorated in 1924.

id: margate
line: 300
to: 305
anchor: On Margate Sands.
kind: place
image: margate-sands
title: Margate Sands
body:
Margate is a resort on the north Kent coast, where the Thames estuary opens to the sea. Eliot went there in the autumn of 1921, on three months' leave from Lloyds Bank after a breakdown, and stayed about three weeks, from late October to mid-November. On 4 November he told Sydney Schiff he had done "a rough draft of part of part III", written sitting in a shelter on the front. That part is usually identified as the pencil drafts of lines 266–311, from "The river sweats" to "burning". The Nayland Rock shelter on the promenade, which still stands, was given listed-building protection in 2009 as the likely place. Lyndall Gordon and others have read "I can connect / Nothing with nothing" as Eliot's own confession as well as the third daughter's.

id: carthage
line: 307
to: 311
anchor: To Carthage then I came
kind: echo
source: augustine-confessions
title: Augustine and the Buddha
quote: To Carthage I came, where there sang all around me in my ears a cauldron of unholy loves.
body:
The opening of Book III of Augustine's <i>Confessions</i> (c. 400), in Pusey's translation (1838), given above; the line, like Eliot's note, rewords it. The Latin has a pun, Carthago and sartago, a frying pan, which is Pusey's "cauldron". Augustine had come to Carthage at about seventeen to study rhetoric, and Book III begins with his love affairs and the theatre. "Burning" is from the Buddha's Fire Sermon, which gives Part III its title: "All things, O priests, are on fire . . . The eye, O priests, is on fire; forms are on fire . . . with the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation." Eliot's note sends readers to Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (Harvard Oriental Series, 1896). Eliot studied Sanskrit and Pali at Harvard in 1911–13, and it is generally assumed that he met the sermon there. "O Lord Thou pluckest me out" is Augustine again, Book X: "I . . . entangle my steps with these outward beauties; but Thou pluckest me out, O Lord, Thou pluckest me out." Eliot's note at line 309 says that putting "these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism" together here "is not an accident". Critics have connected this burning with the refining fire of <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, quoted at line 428.
