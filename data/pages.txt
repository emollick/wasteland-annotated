London Bridge, King William Street and St Mary Woolnoth lie on one route, the way City workers came in from London Bridge station; it ends a short walk from Lloyds Bank, where Eliot worked. The Strand, Queen Victoria Street, Lower Thames Street and St Magnus Martyr make a second route, in Part III. The river’s places run from Richmond and Kew past Greenwich Reach and the Isle of Dogs to Margate Sands, where the third Thames-daughter speaks. Click a place for its lines. id: drafts-lede
where: the drafts page: the paragraph under the title
text: The poem was typed out at roughly twice its published length of four hundred and thirty-four lines. Ezra Pound did most of the cutting, in Paris in January 1922 and by letter after Eliot went home; Eliot’s wife Vivien added comments and two lines of the pub scene; Eliot himself struck out the first opening of Part I. In 1922 Eliot gave the typescript to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector who had negotiated its American publication. After Quinn’s death in 1924 it went unseen until the New York Public Library bought it in 1958, and its whereabouts were made public only in 1968, three years after Eliot’s death. It was printed in facsimile in 1971. The cut passages are still in copyright, so they are described here, not reproduced; the descriptions follow the facsimile edition page by page.
---
id: drafts-lede-2

id: about-text-1
where: the About page: the text section, first paragraph
text: The poem is given as it stands in the first book edition, published by Boni &amp; Liveright in New York in December 1922, including Eliot’s notes, which were written for that edition; the poem had appeared in <i>The Criterion</i> (October 1922) and <i>The Dial</i> (November 1922) without them. That text is in the public domain in the United States, where this site is published. In the United Kingdom and the European Union the poem remains in copyright until the end of 2035, seventy years after Eliot’s death.
---
id: about-text-2
where: the About page: the text section, second paragraph (the 1922 readings kept)
text: The 1922 text differs in small ways from the one in later Faber editions. This edition keeps the 1922 readings and notes the later ones where they matter: “Quando fiam ceu chelidon” at line 429 (later “uti,” the word in the Latin poem); “aetherial” at line 416 (later “aethereal”); the note on “Shantih,” which in 1922 calls the English phrase “a feeble translation of the content of this word” and later “our equivalent.” The pub talk’s “dont” and “wont,” “alright” at line 161, “tonight” at line 111 and “smoothes” at line 255 are the 1922 book’s own spellings, kept as they stand. The dedication to Pound was not printed until 1925 and is included here because Eliot wrote it into Pound’s copy in January 1923. Three slips of the 1922 press are set right: “Oed’ und leer das Meer” at line 42 was printed with a plain O; “The hot water at ten” at line 135 lost its a; and “Dead mountain mouth of carious teeth” at line 339 reads “mount in” in many copies of the first printing, the a having dropped out as the sheets ran. Typographic quotation marks and a few spellings have been regularised from the transcription on Project Gutenberg, which was checked against a scan of the 1922 printing.

id: about-numbering
where: the About page: the paragraph on the line numbering
text: The 1922 printing numbers every tenth line in the margin. On page 42, in Part V, “If there were water” is set flush right as the run-over of “From doors of mudcracked houses” and is not counted, so from line 346 the printed numbers run one behind: 350 stands beside “A spring,” 360 beside “When I count, there are only you and I together” (the poem’s 361st line), and the last line, “Shantih shantih shantih,” is “line 433” although the poem has 434. Eliot’s notes mostly follow the printed count, but not always: the note on the hermit-thrush is numbered 357 and the note on the Antarctic expedition 360, which fit the true count, while 366–76, 401, 411, 424 and 433 fit the printed one. On this site the margin counts the lines as they are, and each note is attached to the line Eliot meant.

id: about-written
where: the About page: the paragraph on what was written for the edition
text: The annotations, the translations of the sources, the descriptions of the voices, the tagging of water and drought, the pathways and the section “ways in” were written for this site. Every claim about the poem’s composition can be traced to one of: the facsimile edition of the drafts (Valerie Eliot, ed., 1971); <i>The Letters of T. S. Eliot</i>, volume 1; <i>The Poems of T. S. Eliot</i>, edited by Christopher Ricks and Jim McCue (2015), whose commentary is the standard reference; Lawrence Rainey’s <i>The Annotated Waste Land</i> (2005); Robert Crawford’s <i>Young Eliot</i> (2015); and Matthew Hollis’s <i>The Waste Land: A Biography of a Poem</i> (2022). The recordings and source texts are linked, not copied, from the libraries and archives that hold them.

id: about-how-1
where: the About page: how the site works, first paragraph
text: The whole poem is on one page. The row of lenses above the text sets what is shown beside each line: Eliot’s notes, the sources, the speakers, the translations, water and drought, the places, the hours, or the cuts from the drafts. The keys 1 to 9 switch lenses; Escape closes anything open. Marked phrases open a card in the margin (or under the line, on a phone). The thin strip at the edge of the screen stands for the whole poem, one mark per line, coloured by the lens in use; click it to move to a line. The dark setting, called the violet hour after line 215, follows your system’s preference and can be switched with the small dot in the running head.
---
id: about-how-2
where: the About page: how the site works, second paragraph
text: Two ways of reading are for going slowly. “Line by line” dims everything but one line and brings whatever belongs to it into the margin: its source, Eliot’s note, a change of speaker, a translation. The arrow keys move between lines, or it can move on by itself at reading pace. In the Voices lens, clicking a speaker follows that voice alone through the poem, so that you can read everything one speaker says from beginning to end: the woman in the chair in Part II, the thunder, the Thames-daughters.

---
id: lens-echoes-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Sources
text: Words underlined in violet open the passage Eliot took and the book it comes from, so that a line can be read beside what it quotes; the fainter underlines open plain notes.
---
id: lens-notes-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Notes
text: The notes Eliot printed with the poem, set beside the lines they refer to as well as at the end.
---
id: lens-voices-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Voices
text: Each line coloured by its speaker, with a legend; choose one voice and follow it through the poem.
---
id: lens-tongues-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Languages
text: The German, French, Italian, Latin, Greek and Sanskrit in the poem, with the cries of birds, bells and the river marked beside them; click a phrase for its translation under the line.
---
id: lens-water-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Water
text: The lines that mention water, drought or fire, tinted by kind, and how many there are of each.
---
id: lens-places-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Places
text: The places the poem names, marked in the text; click one for a card with its lines and a link to it on the map.
---
id: lens-clock-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Clock
text: The times of day and the seasons the poem gives, marked in the text, the hours set on a dial and the seasons listed beneath it.
---
id: lens-drafts-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Drafts
text: What Ezra Pound cut, and what Eliot and his wife Vivien changed, in the typescript, described at the lines it affected.
---
id: tool-slow-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Read line by line
text: One line at a time, with its notes; go on when you like, or let the page run.
---
id: tool-cards-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Turn the tarot cards
text: The tarot pack of Madame Sosostris in Part I, made for this edition. Turn a card to read what it shows and which lines it comes from.
---
id: tool-fragments-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Unstack the last lines
text: The last eight lines set out one by one, each with its language and, for the seven that are quotations, its source.
---
id: tool-sortes-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Draw a line at random
text: One line of the poem chosen by chance, with its note; draw again as often as you like.
---
id: tool-concordance-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Find a word
text: Every line in which a word occurs, to see how the poem uses it.
---
id: tool-heart-why
where: ways to read it: the sentence under Learn it by heart
text: Words are blanked out in stages you choose, every third, then every second, then all but the first of each line, so that you can test yourself until you can say the lines without looking.
---
id: tool-walk-why

text: A sequence of stops through the poem on one subject, with a note at each. The pathways are listed below.
