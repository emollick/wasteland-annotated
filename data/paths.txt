id: first
title: A first reading
intro: Twelve stops for someone meeting the poem for the first time. Each stop is a place to stand, not an explanation. Read the lines, read the note, move on.
---
line: 1
text: Start with the sound. Read the first seven lines aloud. Five of the seven lines end on a hanging participle: breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding. The poem is about things that will not finish.
---
line: 19
text: A new voice. Nobody announces the change; the tone does. This is the poem's method for four hundred lines: voices arrive and leave without introduction.
---
line: 35
text: The one moment of feeling in Part I. Between two lines of Wagner, someone remembers being unable to speak. Hold on to "the heart of light, the silence."
---
line: 43
text: A comic fortune-teller who is also the poem's prophet. Everything she deals comes true. Note the card she cannot find.
---
line: 60
text: The real London: a bridge, a street, a clock. And the dead, from Dante, walking over it to work. The poem's trick, learned from Baudelaire, is that both are true at once.
---
line: 111
text: A conversation in which only one person speaks. Read her lines aloud and his silently. The section's title, A Game of Chess, is about moves made without speaking.
---
line: 139
text: A pub at closing time. The poem's most ordinary speech, and its most overheard. The publican's shout is also the poem's clock running out.
---
line: 215
text: Tiresias, who in Ovid was a woman for seven years, watches the typist and her visitor. Eliot's note at 218 says what Tiresias sees "is the substance of the poem", a claim critics have disputed.
---
line: 266
text: The Thames-daughters' song, in short lines, with the refrain of Wagner's Rhine-daughters (Eliot's note gives <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, III, i). Worth reading aloud.
---
line: 312
text: Ten lines. A drowned man. The tarot's warning fulfilled, gently. Read it twice.
---
line: 331
text: Rock and no water for twenty-nine lines. Let it be as dry as it is. The rain, when it comes at line 395, is two words long.
---
line: 427
text: The last eight lines are a stack of quotations in five languages. Do not look them up yet. Read them as a noise, then use the Fragments view to take the stack apart.
===
id: water
title: Water
intro: The poem's hydrology, from spring rain to the last rain. Follow the water and you follow the argument: water is what the waste land needs, water is what drowns you, and the two are never told apart.
---
line: 4
text: "Dull roots with spring rain." The first water is unwelcome.
---
line: 24
text: "the dry stone no sound of water." The first drought, and the first of the poem's many sounds of water that are not there.
---
line: 42
text: "Oed' und leer das Meer." The sea, empty. Wagner's shepherd sees no ship.
---
line: 47
text: The drowned Phoenician Sailor, and Ariel's pearls. The first death by water is a card.
---
line: 55
text: "Fear death by water." The reading's one instruction, and the reverse of what the Grail legend advises.
---
line: 125
text: The pearls again, in a room with no water in it but the hot water at ten.
---
line: 172
text: Ophelia leaves the pub. Her next scene is the brook.
---
line: 176
text: "Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song": Spenser's refrain from the <i>Prothalamion</i>, a wedding poem. Here the nymphs "are departed" and the river "bears no" litter.
---
line: 182
text: "By the waters of Leman I sat down and wept." Psalm 137 in the Prayer Book wording, "By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept", with Lake Geneva (Lac Léman) in Babylon's place. Eliot finished the poem at Lausanne, on the lake, in the winter of 1921–22.
---
line: 189
text: "While I was fishing in the dull canal", behind the gashouse. The fisher is usually taken as the Fisher King of Weston's book, whose wound lays his land waste.
---
line: 266
text: "The river sweats / Oil and tar." The working Thames of Eliot's day; then, at 279, Elizabeth's barge in 1561, from the Spanish ambassador's letter that Eliot's note quotes.
---
line: 312
text: Phlebas. The current under sea. The tarot fulfilled.
---
line: 331
text: "Here is no water but only rock." The longest drought.
---
line: 353
text: "If there were the sound of water only." A hymn to a sound that is not there, ending in a bird that imitates water.
---
line: 395
text: "Bringing rain." Two words. The only rain that falls.
---
line: 396
text: "Ganga was sunken." The monsoon waiting over the Himalaya.
---
line: 419
text: "The boat responded." Water at last as something that answers a hand.
---
line: 424
text: "I sat upon the shore / Fishing, with the arid plain behind me." The last water and the last drought, in one sentence.

id: dead
title: The dead
intro: Burials, drownings, bones and the crowd that death has undone. The poem's first section is called The Burial of the Dead, from the Book of Common Prayer, and the dead never quite stay in it.
---
line: 0
text: The Sibyl, who cannot die, in her jar.
---
line: 2
text: "Lilacs out of the dead land." The dead land is the first thing named after April.
---
line: 23
text: "the dead tree gives no shelter." Ezekiel's valley, Ecclesiastes' long home.
---
line: 39
text: "I was neither / Living nor dead." The hyacinth garden.
---
line: 62
text: Dante's crowd on London Bridge: "I had not thought death had undone so many."
---
line: 71
text: "That corpse you planted last year in your garden." Frazer's buried god, in a suburb.
---
line: 74
text: Webster's dirge for unburied men, with the wolf turned into a dog.
---
line: 115
text: "rats' alley / Where the dead men lost their bones." The trenches.
---
line: 194
text: "bones cast in a little low dry garret, / Rattled by the rat's foot only, year to year."
---
line: 245
text: Tiresias, who "walked among the lowest of the dead."
---
line: 312
text: Phlebas, "a fortnight dead."
---
line: 328
text: "He who was living is now dead / We who were living are now dying."
---
line: 388
text: "the tumbled graves, about the chapel." The Chapel Perilous, and "Dry bones can harm no one."
---
line: 407
text: "our obituaries." What the dead leave: memories, wills, empty rooms.
---
line: 412
text: Ugolino, hearing the door of the tower nailed shut.

id: women
title: The women
intro: Eliot's note on Tiresias says "all the women are one woman." Here they are, in order of appearance, so that you can test the claim. Most of them speak; several are quotations; two are queens; one is a bird.
---
line: 0
text: The Sibyl, kept in a bottle, asked by boys what she wants.
---
line: 13
text: Marie, on the sled, frightened and free.
---
line: 35
text: The hyacinth girl. Two lines, in quotation marks, and a garden.
---
line: 43
text: Madame Sosostris, with a cold, afraid of the police, right about everything.
---
line: 49
text: Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks: a card, a poison, a painting.
---
line: 77
text: The lady in the chair: Cleopatra's barge, Dido's ceiling, Eve's garden, made into furniture.
---
line: 99
text: Philomel, raped and turned into a nightingale, singing "Jug Jug to dirty ears."
---
line: 111
text: The woman with bad nerves, who wants to be spoken to.
---
line: 139
text: Lil, thirty-one, five children, no teeth, and her friend, who tells her the truth.
---
line: 172
text: Ophelia, leaving.
---
line: 199
text: Mrs Porter and her daughter, from a bawdy song of Australian troops that Eliot's note says reached him from Sydney.
---
line: 222
text: The typist, who "smoothes her hair with automatic hand."
---
line: 279
text: Elizabeth and Leicester in a barge, where they "began to talk nonsense" (the Spanish ambassador's words, in Eliot's note).
---
line: 292
text: The three Thames-daughters, who Eliot's note says speak in turn, from Highbury, Moorgate and Margate. The first echoes La Pia in Dante, "Siena made me, Maremma unmade me" (Eliot's note at 293).
---
line: 368
text: "Murmur of maternal lamentation." The women at the cross, and the mothers of Europe.
---
line: 378
text: The woman who draws her long black hair out tight and plays it.

id: cities
title: Cities
intro: The poem's geography, in the order the poem gives it. It begins in Munich and ends on the Ganges, with London at its centre and five capitals falling in two lines.
---
line: 8
text: The Starnberger See and the Hofgarten: Munich, where Eliot spent August 1911.
---
line: 12
text: Lithuania, by way of a sentence in German.
---
line: 60
text: "Unreal City": London, from Baudelaire's Paris.
---
line: 66
text: King William Street and St Mary Woolnoth. Eliot's route to work.
---
line: 70
text: Mylae, the sea battle of 260 BC.
---
line: 182
text: Leman is Lake Geneva. Eliot was at Lausanne, on the lake, from late November 1921 to early January 1922, and finished the poem there.
---
line: 209
text: Smyrna, now İzmir, the chief port of Asia Minor, through which the Levant's dried fruit was shipped; Eliot's note on 211 gives his reading of the line's trade shorthand.
---
line: 213
text: The Cannon Street Hotel, at the City station; and a weekend at the Metropole, usually taken as the Brighton hotel.
---
line: 245
text: Thebes, in Greece, Tiresias's city.
---
line: 258
text: The Strand, Queen Victoria Street, Lower Thames Street and, by London Bridge, Wren's church of St Magnus Martyr (Eliot's note at 264).
---
line: 275
text: Downstream of the City: Greenwich Reach, with the Isle of Dogs on its north bank.
---
line: 293
text: Highbury, in north London; Richmond and Kew, up the river; Moorgate, in the City; Margate, on the Kent coast.
---
line: 307
text: Carthage, where Augustine went as a student; his <i>Confessions</i> call it "a cauldron of unholy loves" (Eliot's note).
---
line: 375
text: "Jerusalem Athens Alexandria / Vienna London / Unreal."
---
line: 396
text: Ganga and Himavant. The poem's last places are a river and a mountain.
===
id: birds
title: Birds
intro: Every bird in the poem is a musician, and every song is a complaint. Recordings, where they exist, are in the listening room.
---
line: 23
text: "the cricket no relief." Not a bird, but the first singer, from Ecclesiastes.
---
line: 99
text: Philomel, the nightingale, "Jug Jug."
---
line: 203
text: "Twit twit twit / Jug jug jug jug jug jug." The nightingale again (line 100), singing the words Elizabethan poets gave her song; "Tereu" is both one of those words and the name of Tereus, who raped her.
---
line: 313
text: "the cry of gulls," which Phlebas forgot.
---
line: 354
text: "Not the cicada." Another singer that is not a bird and not water.
---
line: 357
text: The hermit-thrush, whose "water-dripping song" Eliot heard in Quebec. Listen to it.
---
line: 380
text: "bats with baby faces." Not birds. Included for the wings.
---
line: 392
text: "Only a cock stood on the rooftree / Co co rico co co rico." Peter's cock, Hamlet's, and the French farmyard.
---
line: 429
text: "O swallow swallow." Procne, Philomel's sister, at the end.

id: nothing
title: Nothing
intro: One word, followed through the poem. It is said by a lover, a woman with bad nerves, a man who will not answer, a Thames-daughter, and the poor on Margate sands.
---
line: 40
text: "I knew nothing, / Looking into the heart of light, the silence."
---
line: 120
text: "Nothing again nothing."
---
line: 122
text: "Do you know nothing? Do you see nothing? Do you remember / Nothing?"
---
line: 126
text: "Is there nothing in your head?"
---
line: 302
text: "I can connect / Nothing with nothing."
---
line: 305
text: "My people humble people who expect / Nothing."
---
line: 402
text: "what have we given?" The question that answers the word.

---

---

---

id: eliot
title: The poem in Eliot's life
intro: Places and days from the poet's own year, 1921 into 1922, that surface in the text. Not a key to the poem, but a map of where he was standing.
---
line: 8
text: Munich, August 1911: the Hofgarten's colonnade, the lake where a king drowned.
---
line: 35
text: The hyacinth garden: Emily Hale in Boston, or Jean Verdenal in the Luxembourg Gardens with a branch of lilac.
---
line: 66
text: Lloyds Bank, Lombard Street, across the road from St Mary Woolnoth. The clock struck nine as he arrived.
---
line: 111
text: The flat at 9 Clarence Gate Gardens. Vivien's nerves and his.
---
line: 139
text: Ellen Kellond, the Eliots' maid, who told them about Lil.
---
line: 182
text: Eliot spent late November and December 1921 at Lausanne, on Lake Geneva, in the care of the nerve doctor Roger Vittoz, and wrote Parts IV and V there.
---
line: 300
text: Eliot spent three weeks at Margate in October and November 1921, on sick leave from the bank, and drafted "some fifty lines" of Part III in a shelter on the front, probably the Nayland Rock shelter, listed in 2009, which still stands. The lines are thought to be the river song and the Thames-daughters' speeches.
---
line: 312
text: Gloucester, Massachusetts: the drowned sailor's original voyage, cut by Pound.
---
line: 419
text: A boat off Cape Ann, the summers of his boyhood.
---
line: 0
text: Paris, January 1922: Pound's flat, the pencil, "il miglior fabbro."
