intro: Twelve stops through the poem for a reader meeting it for the first time. Each stop gives a passage and a short note on it, in the order of the poem. Read the lines first, then the note, then go on to the next stop.
why: Start here if you have not read the poem before.

id: grail
title: The Grail and the waste land
intro: The Grail legend as Eliot took it from Weston and Frazer. Eliot’s headnote to his notes credits the title, the plan and “a good deal of the incidental symbolism” to Jessie L. Weston’s <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920), a study of the Grail legend, and names beside it the anthropologist J. G. Frazer’s <i>The Golden Bough</i>. Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve an old rite, in which the wound of a king, the Fisher King, leaves his land waste and its waters dry until a knight asks the right question, so that the king is healed and the waters flow. Frazer supplied the rite, the god (Adonis, Attis, Osiris) who dies with the year and is buried or drowned so that he may return in spring. The critic Cleanth Brooks made the legend the key to the poem in 1939; in 1956 Eliot regretted having sent readers on a “wild goose chase” after the Tarot and the Grail.
why: The Grail legend behind the title, followed through the poem from the dead land of the first lines to the king fishing on the shore.
sources: Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II
Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXIX
Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXII
Brooks (1939)
Eliot, "The Frontiers of Criticism" (1956)
---
line: 0
text: Weston's summary of the romances is that the object of the Grail quest is "the restoration to health and vigour of a King" whose infirmity "reacts disastrously upon his kingdom". Earlier scholars, she says, had treated the land's misfortunes as an accident of the story; for her the "Waste Land" is "the very heart of our problem", and it is that land the title names. The stops that follow say what the poem does with her king and his waters.
sources: Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II
Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. V
---
line: 1
text: The rites Frazer collected were performed to bring the spring back. The dead, he writes, "came back in the sprouting corn" and were imagined to return in the spring flowers, "the violets and the hyacinths", which "sprang from their dust". The poem's first voice feels that return as cruelty and prefers the "forgetful snow". Brooks (1939) wrote that the poem's people "are afraid to live in reality", so that "April, the month of rebirth, is not the most joyful season but the cruelest".
sources: Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXII
Brooks (1939)
---
line: 19
text: These lines are the poem's first picture of the waste land, dry stone and dead tree and "no sound of water". Weston traces the Grail hero's oldest task, the freeing of the waters, to the <i>Rig-Veda</i>, whose ancient Sanskrit hymns praise the god Indra for killing the giant that had "imprisoned" the rivers: "Indra has filled the rivers, he has inundated the dry land." The waters of this land are still imprisoned; no rain is offered to it until the "damp gust / Bringing rain" of line 394.
sources: Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. III
Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II

line: 43
text: Weston's chapter on the symbols holds that the four Grail talismans, Cup, Lance, Sword and Dish, survive as the four suits of the Tarot, and suggests that the pack may first have been used "to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land". Brooks (1939) wrote that Madame Sosostris "has fallen a long way from the high function of her predecessors". Eliot's note on line 46 ties one of her cards, the man with three staves, "quite arbitrarily" to the Fisher King.
sources: Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. VI

line: 55
text: The missing card is the dying god's. Eliot's note on line 46 ties the Hanged Man to "the Hanged God of Frazer", a chapter of Frazer's <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i> on the god Attis, whose effigy hung on a pine and whose human representative, Frazer conjectures, was once hanged there. Weston's dead god is "not buried in the earth, but thrown into the water", and Brooks (1939) read "Fear death by water" as spoken by one who does not know "that the way into life may be by death itself".

line: 71
text: In Frazer, the Egyptian priests of Osiris buried effigies of the god made of earth and corn at the sowing and took them up with the corn "found to have sprouted from the body of Osiris", a sprouting hailed as the cause of the crops (the card at this line gives the rites). "Will it bloom this year?" is the rite's own question. The poem gives it to a man who asks, in the same speech, that the Dog be kept from digging the corpse up again.
sources: Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXIX

line: 189
text: Brooks (1939) read the man fishing here, for "the reader who knows the Weston references", as the Fisher King (the card at this line has Ferdinand of <i>The Tempest</i>, whose lament the lines adapt). In Weston the king's sickness keeps his land waste, and his castle stands by water, "on or near the sea, or on the banks of an important river". The poem gives its king no wound and no castle, only a canal, a rat and the "White bodies naked on the low damp ground".

line: 202

line: 312
text: "Death by Water is executed in Part IV", Eliot's note on line 46 says. In Frazer the women of Alexandria carried the image of the dead Adonis "to the sea-shore and committed it to the waves", and "sang that the lost one would come back again". The poem keeps the drowning and drops the return; a current picks Phlebas's bones, and the section ends in a warning, not in a rising. Brooks (1939) nevertheless suggested that the section "gives an instance of the conquest of death and time".

line: 386
text: Weston's Perilous Chapel (the card at line 389 gives it) is an adventure "fraught with extreme peril to life". The poem's chapel has no body, no hand and no voice, "only the wind's home", and "Dry bones can harm no one". Brooks (1939) and Southam's note both take it for Weston's chapel. After the cock and the lightning comes "a damp gust / Bringing rain", the first rain offered to the dry land of line 24, though the poem does not say that it falls.

line: 424
text: Eliot's note on line 425 sends the reader to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King, and the card at this line gives her argument. In Weston the quest is achieved when a knight comes to the castle and asks the question, and the king is healed or, in the oldest version, "the waters flowed again thro' their channel". Here no knight has come. The king is still fishing, the plain behind him is still arid, and the one question asked is his own, about his lands and not his healing.

id: passion
title: The Bible and the Prayer Book
intro: The Bible and the Book of Common Prayer in the poem. Part I is named after the Prayer Book’s burial service; the desert of lines 19 to 30 speaks in words from Ezekiel and Ecclesiastes, and its rock is usually set beside Isaiah’s. Part V opens with the arrest and death of Christ told without his name, as Cleanth Brooks read it in 1939, and goes on to the road to Emmaus, where the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples; Eliot’s last note glosses “Shantih” with the blessing that ends the Communion service. Eliot was baptised into the Church of England in June 1927, five years after the poem. This pathway does not read the poem as the record of a conversion, or as a step towards one. It follows what the poem does with the scriptures it quotes, and says at each stop what a passage keeps of its source and what it does not.
why: What the poem takes from the Bible and the Prayer Book, and what it leaves out, from the burial service that names Part I to the blessing quoted in Eliot’s last note.
sources: Book of Common Prayer, the Order for the Burial of the Dead
Brooks (1939)
Southam, Guide, Biographical Table

The service begins "I am the resurrection and the life" and commits the body "earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust; in sure and certain hope of the Resurrection to eternal life". The part so named has its dead, the crowd of line 62 and the corpse of line 71, and none of the service's hope; the questions asked over the corpse, "Has it begun to sprout?" among them, get no answer.

id: east
title: The Fire Sermon and the thunder
intro: The Indian texts in the poem, the Buddha’s Fire Sermon and an Upanishad. Eliot studied Sanskrit at Harvard for two years from 1911 with Charles Rockwell Lanman, and spent a year on the metaphysics of the ancient Indian philosopher Patañjali with James Haughton Woods, a study that left him, he wrote in 1934, in “a state of enlightened mystification”. Part III takes its title from the sermon in which the Buddha says that all things are on fire; the sermon is heard at line 308, beside St Augustine’s <i>Confessions</i>, a pair Eliot’s note calls “representatives of eastern and western asceticism”. Part V’s title and last lines come from the <i>Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad</i>, where the thunder’s one syllable is heard as three commands; “Shantih”, repeated, is the formula that closes an Upanishad. Cleo McNelly Kearns’s <i>T. S. Eliot and Indic Traditions</i> (1987) is the study behind this pathway.
why: The Buddha and the Upanishads in the poem, from the sermon that names Part III to the thunder’s three commands and the last word.
sources: Warren, Buddhism in Translations, §73
Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, V.2
Eliot, After Strange Gods (1934)
Kearns (1987)

id: method
title: The past beside the present
intro: Old poems and modern scenes in the same lines. In November 1923, reviewing Joyce’s <i>Ulysses</i> in <i>The Dial</i>, Eliot wrote that “in manipulating a continuous parallel between contemporaneity and antiquity, Mr Joyce is pursuing a method which others must pursue after him”, and that the method gave “a shape and a significance to the immense panorama of futility and anarchy which is contemporary history”. He was writing about another man’s book, but the critic F. O. Matthiessen was applying the sentences to Eliot’s own work by 1935. This pathway stops where the parallel is plainest, where Chaucer, Baudelaire, Shakespeare, Spenser, Marvell, John Day or Goldsmith is set beside a modern scene in the same lines, so that the reader sees both at once. Cleanth Brooks (1939) wrote that the poem’s surface parallels are really contrasts and its surface contrasts really parallels, and the stops do not assume that the past comes off better.
why: The places where an old poem and a modern scene are made to share the same lines, from Chaucer’s April to the fragments at the end.
sources: Eliot, "Ulysses, Order, and Myth" (1923)
Matthiessen (1935), ch. II
Brooks (1939)

title: After the war
intro: The war of 1914–1918 and its aftermath, as the poem carries them. Eliot wrote the poem in 1921, three years after the Armistice, and published it in October 1922; at Lloyds Bank, where he worked, he had been put in charge in 1920 of settling the bank’s pre-war debts with Germany. The poem never names the war. The eight stops go in the poem’s order. They are a claim of nationality (line 12) and a sea battle of 260 BC (70), set by readers beside the war just ended; the trench name in “rats’ alley” (115); a husband “demobbed” after four years in the army (139); the song the Australian troops sang (199); a Bradford millionaire’s silk hat (233); the “hooded hordes” of Eliot’s note on eastern Europe (369); and London Bridge falling down (427). Each says what of the war is in the passage, and names the reader who first said so where the claim is a reading.

text: Paris, January 1922. Eliot stopped there on his way back from Lausanne, and Pound went through the typescript with him, cutting it hard. The dedication came later. In January 1923 Eliot inscribed Pound's copy to him as "miglior fabbro", and from 1925 the dedication was printed with the poem. The words are Dante's, for the poet Arnaut Daniel, and mean the better craftsman.
