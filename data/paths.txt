title: A first reading
intro: The Waste Land is a poem of some four hundred and thirty lines, published in 1922 by T. S. Eliot, an American from St Louis who had settled in London and was working in a bank. It is the most famous poem of the twentieth century, and it has a reputation for difficulty that it has earned: it has lines in German, French, Italian, Latin and Sanskrit as well as English, and Greek before it even starts, it quotes the Bible, Dante, Shakespeare, Wagner, a Roman novel, a Sanskrit scripture, a nursery rhyme and a ragtime song, mostly without saying so, and it changes speaker without warning. But the difficulty is the surface. Underneath it the poem is about things everyone knows: what it is like to be alive without quite living, to have memory and desire stirred when you would rather stay numb, and what it is like to be in a crowd of people who cannot speak to each other. That is where the poem begins, with April, cruel because it wakes things up.

It came out of the years just after the First World War, in the same year as Joyce’s Ulysses, and those two books between them are a good part of what people mean by modernism: the moment when writers decided that the way the world now felt could not be said in the old forms, and broke them. The Waste Land showed that a poem could be built out of pieces, out of voices, quotations and scraps of song, with the joins left showing and the reader trusted to feel the whole, and poems have been made that way ever since. Virginia Woolf heard Eliot read it aloud in June 1922, months before it was printed, and wrote in her diary that he “sang it & chanted it rhythmed it”, that what connected it together she was “not so sure”, and that she was left, all the same, with “some strong emotion”.

So read it aloud, or listen to it read; this edition’s listening room will take you to several recordings, two of them by Eliot himself, from 1933 and the 1940s. Read the whole of it, all five parts, at one sitting, which takes about half an hour, and do not stop to look things up. Read aloud, it works before it makes sense. The fortune teller with a cold, the woman in the pub at closing time talking about her friend Lil, the typist’s bleak evening, the thunderstorm in the mountains at the end, in which the thunder speaks: these come across at once, and the lines you cannot follow still carry you along. Edmund Wilson, the American critic who reviewed it in December 1922 for The Dial, the New York magazine that had printed it the month before, had Eliot’s notes in front of him and said you could do without them: the poem is “intelligible at first reading”, and you need not follow its quotations “to feel the force of the intense emotion” in it.

What was Eliot trying to do? He gave two answers in his life, and both are true. He finished the poem at the end of 1921, on leave from the bank after a breakdown, first at Margate and then in Lausanne under a doctor for his nerves, and his marriage was miserable. Later he said that to him it had been only “a piece of rhythmical grumbling”, the working off of a private grievance, and that the critics who called it the disillusionment of a generation were talking “nonsense”. You can hear that private poem in it: the fear, the nerves, and what sounds like his marriage in the second part. The other answer is in the first of the notes he printed at the back of the book, which says that the title, the plan and “a good deal of the incidental symbolism” came from a book by Jessie Weston, a scholar of medieval romance, on the legend of the Holy Grail. In the legend a king is wounded and his land dries up with him, and both wait for a knight who will ask the right question. Eliot laid that old story under London in 1921, and under the whole of Europe after the war, so that each shows through the other: the crowd flowing over London Bridge at nine in the morning is also the dead of Dante’s hell, and the dry rock and thunder of the last part are the Grail knight’s last road, through a dead country to a ruined chapel, and also, his note says, eastern Europe falling apart after the war.

Why is it so famous? Partly because it was argued over from the start. The Dial gave Eliot its yearly award of two thousand dollars, and Time, in its first issue, reported a rumour that the whole thing had been written as a hoax. Partly because it caught the mood of the years after the war: readers took its dryness and its broken pieces for a picture of their own generation, and it did not matter that Eliot said he had meant nothing of the kind. Partly because of the lines. “April is the cruellest month”, “I will show you fear in a handful of dust”, “HURRY UP PLEASE ITS TIME”, “These fragments I have shored against my ruins”: people who have never opened the poem know those. And partly because of the notes. When the poem came out as a book, Eliot added several pages of notes on his sources. He said long afterwards that he had done it to fill the volume out, and called the notes “bogus scholarship”. They sent readers off to hunt down every quotation, and made the poem look like a puzzle with a key, which it is not. They are worth reading, but afterwards.

How do you enjoy it? The first time, by not working at it. The voices come and go, and you can let them, without knowing yet who is speaking. Notice the water: there is rain in the first lines and the Thames runs through the middle of the poem, and in the last part there is no water at all, only rock and sand and thunder without rain, until near the end “a damp gust / Bringing rain”. Notice that it is funny in places. The scene in the pub at the end of the second part is funny until its last line, which is Ophelia’s good night from Hamlet. This pathway stops at twenty-eight places and tells you, at each, who is speaking, what the foreign words say, or what story lies behind a name, and otherwise leaves you alone with the lines. The rest of this edition, the notes on every line, the maps, the fortune teller’s cards, the recordings, is there when you want it.

text: Before the first line there is a paragraph of Latin with a few words of Greek in it, and under it a dedication. The Latin is from the <i>Satyricon</i>, a Roman novel of the first century by Petronius, and the speaker is Trimalchio, a freed slave who has grown enormously rich and is showing off at his own dinner party. Among his boasts is this: he once saw the Sibyl at Cumae, near Naples, with his own eyes, hanging in a jar. The Sibyl was a prophetess, and Ovid tells her story. The god Apollo offered her whatever she wanted; she pointed to a heap of dust and asked to live as many years as there were grains in it, and forgot to ask to stay young. So she went on ageing for centuries and shrinking, until, in Trimalchio's story, she could be kept in a jar. The few words of Greek are what the boys who come to look at her call up, and what she answers: Sibyl, what do you want? I want to die. In the novel it is a boast at a vulgar dinner, told for a laugh. The poem takes it seriously.<br><br>Eliot put that in front of his poem; the first line then calls April cruel, and the poem is full of people like the Sibyl, who go on living past the point of wanting to. Keep her dust in mind: at line 30 someone will say "I will show you fear in a handful of dust". The dedication is to Ezra Pound, the American poet who had championed Eliot since 1914 and who cut this poem's manuscript to about half its length. "Il miglior fabbro" is Italian for "the better craftsman"; in Dante's <i>Purgatorio</i> one poet says it of another, and Eliot borrowed it to say what Pound had been to this poem.

text: The poem is in five parts, and this first one is called The Burial of the Dead, after the funeral service in the Church of England's Prayer Book. Then someone is speaking, and the poem does not say who. Keep reading and the voice becomes a woman remembering: a summer shower by a lake outside Munich, coffee in a park, a sled ride when she was a child staying with her cousin the archduke, and her name, Marie, when he tells her to hold on tight. Whether the first seven lines are hers too, or a voice that hands over to her at line 8, the poem does not say. The same "us" that winter kept warm is surprised by summer three lines later, and nobody is introduced. That is how the whole of <i>The Waste Land</i> works. Voices arrive unannounced, speak for a while and give way to the next, and you learn who they are from what they say, if you learn at all. And the opening lines, whoever owns them, are the poem's first surprise: spring is cruel, because it stirs "memory and desire" in someone who would rather not be stirred, and winter was kind, because its snow was "forgetful".<br><br>The first line also answers an older April, the opening of Chaucer's <i>Canterbury Tales</i>, more than five centuries earlier, where April's sweet showers end the drought of March and everyone longs to go on pilgrimage. In Eliot's poem the same rain is not welcome.

line: 12
text: The line is German, and nothing in the poem translates it: "I'm not Russian at all, I come from Lithuania, real German." Russin is the feminine form, so it is a woman speaking, and it sounds like something said across a café table, a little sharply, to someone who has got her wrong. Lithuania had been part of the Russian empire until the war, so a woman from there might well be taken for Russian, and she will not have it: German by birth, she says, whatever the map said. Whether she is Marie, whose sled ride follows straight after, or a stranger overheard, the poem does not say; the German drops into the English and the English goes on. You get used to it. <i>The Waste Land</i> has lines in German, French, Italian, Latin and Sanskrit, and Greek in its epigraph, and it never stops to explain them. Eliot's typescript of the first two parts carried a title he later dropped, "He Do the Police in Different Voices", the words of an old woman in Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> praising a boy who reads her the police news from the paper and does all the voices. That is what the poem does.

line: 19
text: The voice changes here, and nothing tells you so but the sound. Marie's memories stop, and someone begins to talk like a prophet, in the cadence of the Bible: "Son of man" is how God addresses the prophet Ezekiel, and Eliot's note says so. The one addressed is "you", which means the reader as much as anyone, and what you are told is that you know only "a heap of broken images", in a country of dry stone, dead trees and no water. It is the first sight of the waste land of the title, and the poem comes back to it at the end, in the last part, where there is rock and no water for nearly thirty lines. The bracketed line is the same voice offering shade under a red rock, and the two shadows are a day's, one behind you at morning, one rising to meet you at evening; what it will show you instead is "fear in a handful of dust". The dust is the Sibyl's, from her story at the front of the poem.

line: 31
text: Four lines of German, and this time they are sung. They are a sailor's song from the opening of Wagner's opera <i>Tristan and Isolde</i>, which Eliot's note names: fresh blows the wind towards home; my Irish child, where are you lingering? Isolde is an Irish princess being shipped to Cornwall to marry its king; Tristan, who is taking her there, is the man she loves, and the opera ends with both of them dead. The sailor sings from the mast, and Isolde hears him from her tent on the deck. Then the poem gives you its own love story, in seven lines. The two in quotation marks are a girl's, the one who was given hyacinths a year ago; the five after them belong to the person who gave them, remembering the walk back from the garden, when he could not speak and "knew nothing", looking "into the heart of light". It is the nearest the poem comes to happiness, and it is a memory. The last line of the passage is German again, from the end of the same opera, where Tristan lies dying, waiting for Isolde's ship, and a shepherd looking out to sea reports it "desolate and empty". So the garden sits between two moments from Tristan, a song of longing and the empty sea.
sources: Wagner, Tristan und Isolde, Act I and Act III

line: 43
text: Madame Sosostris is a fortune teller with a cold and a pack of Tarot cards, the picture cards used for telling fortunes. The name is probably a joke: Aldous Huxley's novel <i>Crome Yellow</i>, published the year before, has a man telling fortunes at a village fair as "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana". But take the cards seriously, because the poem does; it is dealing out its own characters. The drowned Phoenician Sailor turns up in Part IV; the one-eyed merchant in Part III; the "crowds of people, walking round in a ring" in the next lines, on London Bridge; and "Fear death by water" hangs over the whole poem. The Hanged Man, which she cannot find, is a real Tarot card, a man hanging upside down by one foot, and Eliot's note says he linked it with the hooded figure who walks beside the travellers in Part V. The line in brackets, "Those are pearls that were his eyes", is from Shakespeare's <i>Tempest</i>, a song sung to a prince about his father, supposedly drowned, whose bones are turning to coral and his eyes to pearls; it comes back later, and so does the sailor. Eliot's note admits that he did not know the real Tarot pack and made up cards to suit himself: Belladonna, which is Italian for a beautiful woman and the name of a poison, the "lady of situations" and the one-eyed merchant are his. And "One must be so careful these days" is a joke with a point: telling fortunes for money was an offence under an Act of 1824 against rogues and vagabonds, and she has the police in mind.

line: 60
text: This is London, and the details are exact. The crowd is the morning rush of clerks crossing London Bridge from the railway station on the south bank into the City, the business district, where Eliot himself worked at a bank. King William Street runs up from the bridge, and St Mary Woolnoth is a real church on it, whose clock, Eliot's note says with a straight face, does keep a dead sound on the ninth stroke, "a phenomenon which I have often noticed". What makes the real city "unreal" is two things laid over it. "Unreal City" comes from the French poet Baudelaire, who wrote of Paris as a swarming city where a ghost accosts you in broad daylight; Eliot's note quotes him. And "I had not thought death had undone so many" is Dante, at the gate of Hell, seeing the crowd of the dead who in life never chose good or evil; the sighs in the next line are from the circle after that. So the commuters are the dead, and do not know it.

line: 69
text: The speaker stops a man in the crowd and says strange things to him. Stetson is just a name; the poem says nothing else about him. Mylae was a sea battle of 260 BC between Rome and Carthage, so "you who were with me in the ships at Mylae" makes the two of them veterans of a war more than two thousand years old, which is a way of saying that the war just over was the same war. The corpse planted in the garden that might sprout is the oldest idea in the poem: the buried god or king whose burial brings the spring, from the ancient rituals described in the two books of anthropology Eliot names at the head of his notes, Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i> and Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>; here it is asked about as if it were a matter of bulbs. "Oh keep the Dog far hence" turns a line from a funeral song in Webster's play <i>The White Devil</i>, where it is a wolf that must be kept away because it will dig the body up; Eliot makes it a dog, "friend to men", which would dig the body up out of friendliness and so undo the burial before anything could grow from it. And the last line is French, the last line of the poem that opens Baudelaire's <i>Flowers of Evil</i>: hypocrite reader, my likeness, my brother. It is said to Stetson, and past him to you.

text: A new part, called A Game of Chess; the game itself comes at the end of this first scene, and its point with it. The first line is Shakespeare, from <i>Antony and Cleopatra</i>, where Cleopatra's barge, "like a burnished throne, burned on the water", and it opens a description that runs for more than thirty lines through a rich woman's dressing room: marble, gilt, candlelight, a heap of jewels, perfumes "unstoppered" in bottles of ivory and coloured glass, a carved ceiling, a fire of sea-wood burning green and orange. It is an inventory of luxury that gets heavier as it goes, and by the end of it you can hardly breathe in there, which is the idea. The Latin word "laquearia" means a panelled ceiling, and Eliot's note takes it from Virgil's description of the banquet Dido gave for Aeneas, the queen who killed herself when he left her. Above the fireplace hangs a picture, of a scene from Ovid.

text: The picture over the fireplace tells the story of Philomela, from Ovid. Tereus, king of Thrace, raped his wife's sister Philomela and cut out her tongue so that she could not tell; she wove what had happened into a tapestry, her sister read it, and when Tereus pursued them the gods turned all three into birds, Philomela into the nightingale, whose song has been heard as her cry ever since. "Jug jug" is how the poets of Shakespeare's day wrote the nightingale's note; "to dirty ears" is Eliot's addition, and the point is that the world still hears a raped woman's cry as a pretty sound. Philomela comes back twice: in Part III, where "jug jug" and "Tereu", the king's name, break into the river scene, and in the last lines, in the Latin "when shall I be as the swallow". The "withered stumps of time" on the walls are other old stories, unnamed, and the room is full of them, staring.

text: Two people, and only one of them speaks aloud. The woman's words are in quotation marks; the man's replies are not, because he does not make them, and what you read in between is what he thinks and does not say. She begs him to speak, to think, to tell her what he remembers; he thinks of rats' alley and dead men's bones, and of "those are pearls that were his eyes", the drowned man's song from the fortune teller's scene. When she asks whether he is alive he answers, in his head, with a line of a ragtime song, "That Shakespearian Rag", a real song of 1912, spelt here to be sung. Readers have always heard Eliot's own marriage in the scene, and his wife Vivien, who was ill and unhappy, wrote "WONDERFUL" beside it on the typescript. The plan for the day, hot water at ten, a closed car at four, a game of chess, is a life with nothing in it, and the game of chess is the title of the part, taken from a play by Thomas Middleton, <i>Women Beware Women</i>, in which a mother-in-law is kept busy at chess downstairs while, upstairs, her son's wife is seduced: a game to pass the time while the thing that matters happens somewhere else, which is what these two are doing with their day.

text: A different woman and a different room: a London pub near closing time, and one woman telling the others what she said to her friend Lil. Nobody announces the change; the voice does it. Lil's husband Albert has been "demobbed", discharged from the army after four years in the war, and he wants a good time; Lil, at thirty-one, has lost her teeth and her looks, has had five children and nearly died having one of them, and has taken pills "to bring it off", which means to end a pregnancy. Her friend's advice is to get some teeth and look sharp or lose him. "HURRY UP PLEASE ITS TIME" is the landlord calling closing time, which the licensing laws of the day made early and strict. It is funny in the way overheard talk is funny, and Lil's story is not. The last line is not from the pub. "Good night, ladies, good night, sweet ladies, good night, good night" is Ophelia's, in <i>Hamlet</i>, said as she goes out mad, before she drowns.

text: Part III is called The Fire Sermon, after a sermon of the Buddha's, and the reason comes at its very end. It opens on the Thames in autumn, with the leaves going and the litter of the summer gone too, no bottles, no sandwich papers, no cigarette ends, and the "nymphs", the girls who were here with their young men, "the loitering heirs of city directors", departed. "Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song" is Spenser, from a wedding poem of 1596 in which nymphs gather flowers on the same river; Eliot's note gives it, and the contrast is the point. "By the waters of Leman I sat down and wept" rewrites the psalm of the exiles in Babylon, "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept"; Leman is Lake Geneva, and Lausanne, where Eliot went for treatment as he finished the poem, stands on it. "But at my back in a cold blast I hear" is Marvell, from a poem urging a woman to make love before time runs out, "But at my back I always hear / Time's wingèd chariot hurrying near"; here what is heard is rattling bones. Then a rat on the canal bank, and a man fishing who thinks of "the king my brother's wreck" and "the king my father's death", which are nearly the words of the shipwrecked prince in Shakespeare's <i>Tempest</i>, mourning a father he thinks drowned. The man fishing is, as near as the poem comes to saying so, its Fisher King, the wounded king of the Grail legend, and he is fishing still at the end.

text: Three quotations and a dirty song in ten lines, none of them announced. "The sound of horns and motors" comes from a poem by John Day, a playwright of Shakespeare's time, in which a noise of horns and hunting brings Actaeon to Diana, the hunter who saw the goddess bathing and was torn apart for it; Eliot's note quotes it. The French line, "and O those children's voices, singing in the dome", is the last line of a sonnet by Verlaine about Wagner's opera <i>Parsifal</i>, where the knight who has resisted temptation hears the choirboys in the Grail chapel. Then the nightingale again, "jug jug", and "Tereu", the name of the king who raped Philomela, in the form the Elizabethan songs used.

text: "Unreal City" again, and this time a single figure out of the crowd: Mr Eugenides, a currant merchant from Smyrna, the port on the coast of Turkey, unshaven, with his pocket full of samples and his trade terms; "C.i.f. line: 215
text: Tiresias is the blind prophet of Greek myth, and the reason he has "wrinkled female breasts" is that he was a woman for seven years. Eliot prints the story from Ovid in his note: Tiresias struck two coupling snakes with his stick and was turned into a woman, and seven years later struck them again and was turned back; so when the gods Jupiter and Juno argued over which sex has more pleasure in love, they asked him, and he said women do. Juno blinded him for it and Jupiter gave him prophecy in return. He is here as the one who has been on both sides, who has "foresuffered all" and sees what is coming, "the violet hour" being dusk. Eliot's note says that Tiresias, "although a mere spectator", is "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest": the characters run into one another, all the women are one woman, and what Tiresias sees "is the substance of the poem". What he sees here you can read for yourself: a typist, a "young man carbuncular", which means pimply, and an evening that neither of them will remember with pleasure. The verse turns into neat rhymed stanzas, and the neatness against the shabbiness of the act is the effect. "When lovely woman stoops to folly" is the first line of a song in Goldsmith's novel <i>The Vicar of Wakefield</i>, where a seduced girl's only remedy is to die; here she smooths her hair and puts on the gramophone.

text: The verse breaks into short lines and a song. Eliot's note says the three Thames-daughters sing here, and gives the model: the Rhine-daughters in Wagner's <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, the river spirits who mourn their stolen gold with a wailing refrain, "Weialala leia". So the Thames gets river-girls of its own. They sing first the modern river, oil and tar and barges drifting down past Greenwich and the Isle of Dogs, and then the river of Queen Elizabeth, rowed in a gilded barge with her favourite, the Earl of Leicester, in a scene Eliot's note takes from a Spanish ambassador's report that the two of them talked of marriage on the water. Then the three girls speak in turn, in plain quotation marks: one was seduced at Richmond, lying on the floor of a canoe; one at Moorgate, and "made no comment" when he wept and promised a new start; and the third "on Margate Sands", where she can "connect / Nothing with nothing". Margate is where Eliot sat in a shelter on the seafront in the autumn of 1921 and wrote this part of the poem. Highbury, Richmond, Kew, Moorgate and Margate are all real places, and the girls are ordinary Londoners; the "la la" that ends the song is what is left of the Rhine-daughters' wail.

text: The part ends in five lines made of two men's words, and its title makes sense at last. "To Carthage then I came" is St Augustine, from his <i>Confessions</i>, remembering his youth in the city where "a cauldron of unholy loves sang all about mine ears"; Eliot's note quotes it. "Burning" is the Buddha, from the Fire Sermon, in which he tells his monks that everything is on fire, the eye, the ear, the whole world, with the fire of passion, hatred and infatuation, and that the way out is to stop feeding the fire. title: Dante and the dead
intro: The poem’s dead, and what they keep of Dante’s. Five of Eliot’s notes send the reader to Dante, more than to any other writer. Two send the crowd on London Bridge (63 and 64) to the <i>Inferno</i>, and a third sends line 412 there, to Count Ugolino, starved in his tower. Two send the reader to the <i>Purgatorio</i>, for La Pia, a murdered wife (293), and for the troubadour Arnaut Daniel in the refining fire (428). The dedication to Ezra Pound, who cut the poem, is taken from the canto in which Arnaut Daniel appears. Dante’s dead, met and known by name, tell the living who they were and ask to be remembered. This pathway follows the poem’s dead in order and asks what each passage keeps of Dante’s dead. Eliot wrote on Dante in <i>The Sacred Wood</i> (1920) and in his <i>Dante</i> of 1929, and in 1950 gave a talk, “What Dante Means to Me”.
why: The poem’s dead taken in order and set beside Dante’s, from the crowd on London Bridge to the poet Arnaut Daniel in the fire of Purgatory.

title: The past beside the present
intro: Old poems and modern scenes in the same lines. In November 1923, reviewing Joyce’s <i>Ulysses</i> in <i>The Dial</i>, Eliot wrote that “in manipulating a continuous parallel between contemporaneity and antiquity, Mr Joyce is pursuing a method which others must pursue after him”, and that the method gave “a shape and a significance to the immense panorama of futility and anarchy which is contemporary history”.
