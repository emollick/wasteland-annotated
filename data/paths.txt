title: The Grail and the waste land
intro: The Grail legend as Eliot took it from two books. They are Jessie L. Weston’s study of it, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920), and the anthropologist J. G. Frazer’s <i>The Golden Bough</i>. The note at the head of Eliot’s notes credits the title, the plan and “a good deal of the incidental symbolism” to Weston and names Frazer beside her. In 1956 Eliot regretted having sent readers on a “wild goose chase” after the Tarot and the Grail. Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve an old rite. In it the Fisher King’s wound leaves his land waste and its waters dry. When a knight asks the right question, the king is healed and the waters flow. In Frazer’s account the god of that rite (Adonis, Attis, Osiris) dies with the year and is buried or drowned so that he may return in spring. In 1939 the critic Cleanth Brooks made the legend the key to the poem.
why: The Grail legend behind the title, followed through the poem from the dead land of the first lines to the king fishing on the shore.
sources: Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II
Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXIX
Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXII
Brooks (1939)
Eliot, "The Frontiers of Criticism" (1956)

line: 0
text: The note at the head of Eliot's notes says the title, the plan and "a good deal of the incidental symbolism" were suggested by Jessie L. Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920), a study of the Grail legend. Weston's summary of the romances is that the object of the Grail quest is "the restoration to health and vigour of a King" whose infirmity "reacts disastrously upon his kingdom". Earlier scholars, she says, treated the land's misfortunes as an accident of the story. For her the "Waste Land" is "the very heart of our problem". The title names that land.
sources: Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II

line: 1
text: The anthropologist J. G. Frazer, in the chapter of <i>The Golden Bough</i> on the rites of Adonis, a god who dies with the year, writes that as the dead "came back in the sprouting corn", so they "might be thought to return in the spring flowers", "the violets and the hyacinths", which "sprang from their dust". The poem's first voice feels that return as cruelty and prefers the "forgetful snow". The critic Cleanth Brooks (1939) wrote that men "are afraid to live in reality", so that "April, the month of rebirth, is not the most joyful season but the cruelest".

text: The anthropologist J. G. Frazer, in <i>The Golden Bough</i>, describes how Egyptian priests buried effigies of the god Osiris, made of earth and corn, at the sowing. When the effigies were taken up again, the corn was "found to have sprouted from the body of Osiris". title: The Bible and the Prayer Book
intro: The Bible and the Book of Common Prayer in the poem. Part I is named after the Prayer Book’s burial service. The desert of lines 19 to 30 speaks in words from Ezekiel and Ecclesiastes, and its red rock is usually set beside Isaiah’s “great rock in a weary land”. Part V opens with the arrest and death of Christ told without his name, as the critic Cleanth Brooks read it in 1939, and goes on to the road to Emmaus, where the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples. Eliot’s last note glosses “Shantih” with the blessing that ends the Communion service. Eliot was baptised into the Church of England in June 1927, five years after the poem.
why: What the poem takes from the Bible and the Prayer Book, and what it leaves out, from the burial service that names Part I to the blessing quoted in Eliot’s last note.
sources: Book of Common Prayer, the Order for the Burial of the Dead
King James Bible, Isaiah 32:2
Brooks (1939)

title: The Fire Sermon and the thunder
intro: The Indian texts in the poem, the Buddha’s Fire Sermon and an Upanishad. Eliot studied Sanskrit at Harvard for two years from 1911 with Charles Rockwell Lanman. He also spent a year on the metaphysics of the ancient Indian philosopher Patañjali with James Haughton Woods, a study that left him, he wrote in 1934, in “a state of enlightened mystification”. Part III takes its title from the sermon in which the Buddha says that all things are on fire. The sermon is heard at line 308, beside St Augustine’s <i>Confessions</i>, a pair Eliot’s note on line 309 (numbered 312 in 1922) calls “representatives of eastern and western asceticism”. Part V’s title and last lines come from the <i>Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad</i>, where the thunder’s one syllable is heard as three commands. “Shantih”, repeated, is the formula that closes an Upanishad. Cleo McNelly Kearns’s <i>T. S. Eliot and Indic Traditions</i> (1987) is the study of Eliot and these texts followed here.
why: The Buddha and the Upanishads in the poem, from the sermon that names Part III to the thunder’s three commands and the last word.
sources: Warren, Buddhism in Translations, §73
Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, V.2
Eliot, After Strange Gods (1934)
Kearns (1987)

line: 400
text: The thunder's words are from the <i>Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad</i>, V.2. Gods, men and demons ask their father Prajāpati for teaching. To each he says "Da", and each hears a different word in it: the gods Damyata, restrain yourselves; the men Datta, give; the demons Dayadhvam, be compassionate. In the Upanishad the thunder repeats "Da Da Da". Eliot's note on line 402 numbers the passage 5, 1 and cites page 489 of Paul Deussen's German translation. title: The past beside the present
intro: Old poems and modern scenes in the same lines. In November 1923, reviewing Joyce’s <i>Ulysses</i> in <i>The Dial</i>, Eliot wrote that “in manipulating a continuous parallel between contemporaneity and antiquity, Mr Joyce is pursuing a method which others must pursue after him”, and that the method gave “a shape and a significance to the immense panorama of futility and anarchy which is contemporary history”. The critic Cleanth Brooks (1939) wrote that the poem’s surface parallels are really contrasts and its surface contrasts really parallels.

title: Cities
intro: The places the poem names, in the order it names them. At the start come the Starnbergersee, a lake near Munich, and the Hofgarten in the city. Through the middle comes London: the bridge, the streets and churches, a public bar in Lower Thames Street, and the river at Greenwich and at Richmond. Among the London stops are the places the poem’s people come from or remember: Lithuania, Mylae, Leman, Smyrna, Thebes, Margate and Carthage. In Part V come the five cities named in two lines, “Jerusalem Athens Alexandria / Vienna London” (375–376), and the Ganges and Himavant (396–398).
