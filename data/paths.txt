id: first
title: A first reading
intro: Twelve stops through the poem for a reader meeting it for the first time. Each stop gives a passage and a short note on it, in the order of the poem. Read the lines first, then the note, then go on to the next stop.
---
line: 1
text: Start with the sound. Read the first seven lines aloud. Five of the seven end on a participle left hanging at the line break: breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding.
---
line: 19
text: The speaker changes here. Marie's talk stops and a prophet's voice begins, and nothing marks the change but the tone. The poem does this throughout: new voices begin without being introduced.
---
line: 35
text: Between the two passages from <i>Tristan</i> (31–34 and 42), someone remembers coming back late from a garden with a companion and being unable to speak or see. Who the two are is not said.
---
line: 43
text: Madame Sosostris is a comic fortune-teller with a cold, but some of the cards she deals come back later in the poem, and Eliot's note on line 46 says which. The card she cannot find is the Hanged Man.
---
line: 60
text: The place is real: London Bridge, King William Street, St Mary Woolnoth and its clock. The crowd crossing the bridge is also Dante's crowd of the dead; Eliot's notes on lines 63 and 64 give the passages. His note on line 60 quotes Baudelaire, whose Paris is a city where a ghost stops a passer-by in daylight.
---
line: 111
text: A conversation in which only one person speaks. Read her lines aloud and his silently. The title comes from Middleton's play, where a game of chess downstairs covers what is done to a woman upstairs (the note at line 137).
---
line: 139
text: A pub at closing time. A woman tells a friend what she said to Lil, whose husband is back from the army. The line in capitals is the landlord calling time.
---
line: 215
text: Tiresias, who in Ovid was a woman for seven years, watches the typist and her visitor. Eliot's note at 218 says what Tiresias sees "is the substance of the poem", a claim critics have disputed.
---
line: 266
text: The Thames-daughters' song, in short lines, with the refrain of Wagner's Rhine-daughters (Eliot's note gives <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, III, i). Worth reading aloud.
---
line: 312
text: The poem's shortest section gives ten lines to a drowned sailor. Madame Sosostris warned of this in Part I: "Fear death by water" (55).
---
line: 331
text: No water from here to line 359. At line 395 a gust brings rain.
---
line: 427
text: The last eight lines are mostly quotations, in five languages. The Fragments view gives each its source.
===
id: water
title: Water
intro: The passages where water is present or lacking, in the order of the poem, from the spring rain of line 4 to the fisher on the shore at line 424: the dry stone of line 24 and the empty sea of line 42, the drowned sailor in Madame Sosostris’s pack and her warning “Fear death by water” (47–55), the Thames of Part III and the waters of Leman (182), the drowning of Phlebas in Part IV, the rock without water in Part V (331–359), the rain of line 395, the sunken Ganges (396) and the boat of line 419. Eliot’s headnote to his notes credits Jessie L. Weston’s book on the Grail legend, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, one chapter of which is called “The Freeing of the Waters.”
---
line: 4
text: "Dull roots with spring rain." The first water in the poem is spring rain reaching the roots, in a sentence that calls the month that brings it the cruellest.
---
line: 24
text: "the dry stone no sound of water." The first drought. The sound of water that is missing here is wished for again, at length, in Part V.
---
line: 42
text: "Oed' und leer das Meer." Desolate and empty the sea: Wagner's shepherd sees no ship.
---
line: 47
text: The drowned Phoenician Sailor. The first drowned man in the poem is a card in a pack. The line in brackets, "Those are pearls that were his eyes," is from Ariel's song in <i>The Tempest</i>.
---
line: 55
text: "Fear death by water." Madame Sosostris warns against water. In the Grail legend that Eliot's headnote points to, water is what the waste land lacks and needs, and Cleanth Brooks (1939) read her warning as a warning against the one death that might bring life. Part IV of the poem is called Death by Water.
---
line: 125
text: Ariel's song again (line 48): the drowned man's eyes. The only other water in this section is the hot water at ten and the rain that may come at four (lines 135–136); the sea is in the room only as driftwood on the fire and a carved dolphin (94–96).
---
line: 172
text: The pub's goodnights turn into Ophelia's last line in Hamlet; the next we hear of her is the Queen's report of her drowning.
---
line: 176
text: "Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song": Spenser's refrain from the <i>Prothalamion</i>, a wedding poem. Here the nymphs "are departed" and the river "bears no" litter.
---
line: 182
text: "By the waters of Leman I sat down and wept." Psalm 137 in the Prayer Book wording, "By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept", with Lake Geneva (Lac Léman) in Babylon's place. Eliot finished the poem at Lausanne, on the lake, in the winter of 1921–22.
---
line: 189
text: "While I was fishing in the dull canal", behind the gashouse. The fisher is usually taken as the Fisher King of Weston's book, whose wound lays his land waste.
---
line: 266
text: "The river sweats / Oil and tar." The working Thames of Eliot's day; then, at 279, Elizabeth's barge in 1561, from the Spanish ambassador's letter that Eliot's note quotes.
---
line: 312
text: Phlebas is the drowned Phoenician Sailor of the tarot reading (47); here the current under sea picks his bones.
---
line: 331
text: "Here is no water but only rock." Rock and no water to line 359, and no rain until the gust of line 395.
---
line: 353
text: "If there were the sound of water only." Lines 353 to 359 imagine the sound of water and end on the hermit-thrush, whose song Eliot's note calls "water-dripping."
---
line: 395
text: "Bringing rain." The first rain since line 9. The poem does not show it fall.
---
line: 396
text: "Ganga was sunken." The monsoon waiting over the Himalaya.
---
line: 419
text: "The boat responded." A calm sea, and a boat under control.
---
line: 424
text: "I sat upon the shore / Fishing, with the arid plain behind me." The Fisher King, by water, with the waste land behind him.

id: dead
title: The dead
intro: The poem’s dead, in the order they appear, among them the crowd on London Bridge (lines 62–63), the corpse planted in the garden (71), Phlebas in Part IV and the “Dry bones” of line 391. Part I takes its title, The Burial of the Dead, from the burial service in the Book of Common Prayer.
---
line: 0
text: The Sibyl, who cannot die, in her jar.
---
line: 2
text: "Lilacs out of the dead land." The poem's second line already calls the land dead.
---
line: 23
text: "the dead tree gives no shelter." Eliot's notes send these lines to the Bible: "Son of man" (20) to Ezekiel 2:1, where God so addresses the prophet, and this line to Ecclesiastes 12:5, where the grasshopper is a burden and "man goeth to his long home," the grave.
---
line: 39
text: "I was neither / Living nor dead." The hyacinth garden.
---
line: 62
text: Dante's crowd on London Bridge: "I had not thought death had undone so many."
---
line: 71
text: "That corpse you planted last year in your garden." The buried god of Frazer's vegetation rites, put in the ground so that he will come up again, here a corpse in a garden.
---
line: 74
text: Webster's dirge for unburied men, with the wolf turned into a dog and the foe into a friend.
---
line: 115
text: "rats' alley / Where the dead men lost their bones." The trenches.
---
line: 194
text: "bones cast in a little low dry garret, / Rattled by the rat's foot only, year to year."
---
line: 245
text: Tiresias, who "walked among the lowest of the dead."
---
line: 312
text: By the time his section opens Phlebas has been dead a fortnight. The drowning itself is never shown.
---
line: 328
text: "He who was living is now dead / We who were living are now dying."
---
line: 388
text: "the tumbled graves, about the chapel." The Chapel Perilous, and "Dry bones can harm no one."
---
line: 407
text: "our obituaries." What the dead leave: memories, wills, empty rooms.
---
line: 412
text: Ugolino, hearing the door of the tower locked, or nailed, below him.

id: women
title: The women
intro: Eliot’s note on Tiresias (line 218) says that in the poem “all the women are one woman.” This pathway gives the poem’s women in the order they appear, so that you can judge the claim for yourself. Some speak in their own words; some are figures from older books, named or alluded to; Philomel, from Ovid, appears as a nightingale.
---
line: 0
text: The Sibyl, kept in a jar, asked by boys what she wants.
---
line: 13
text: Marie, who was frightened on the sled as a child, and who says that in the mountains you feel free.
---
line: 35
text: The hyacinth girl. She speaks the two lines in quotation marks, 35–36. The garden that follows is remembered by someone else.
---
line: 43
text: Madame Sosostris, who has a cold, deals cards that come back later in the poem. "One must be so careful these days" is usually read as a fortune-teller's caution about the law, since telling fortunes for money could be prosecuted.
---
line: 49
text: Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks. Belladonna is deadly nightshade, a poison and a cosmetic, and the word means beautiful lady; her title recalls Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>.
---
line: 77
text: The lady in the chair. Her room is made from Cleopatra's barge (line 77), Dido's ceiling (92) and Eve's garden (98).
---
line: 99
text: Philomel, raped and turned into a nightingale, singing "Jug Jug" to dirty ears.
---
line: 111
text: The woman with bad nerves, who wants to be spoken to.
---
line: 139
text: Lil, thirty-one, five children, bad teeth, and the friend who did not mince her words.
---
line: 172
text: Ophelia, leaving.
---
line: 199
text: Mrs Porter and her daughter, from a bawdy song of Australian troops that Eliot's note says reached him from Sydney.
---
line: 222
text: The typist, who "smoothes her hair with automatic hand."
---
line: 279
text: Elizabeth and Leicester in a barge, where they "began to talk nonsense" (the Spanish ambassador's words, in Eliot's note).
---
line: 292
text: The three Thames-daughters, who Eliot's note says speak in turn, from Highbury, Moorgate and Margate. The first echoes La Pia in Dante, "Siena made me, Maremma unmade me" (Eliot's note at 293).
---
line: 368
text: "Murmur of maternal lamentation." Commentators connect it with the women who lamented on the way to the cross (Luke 23:27) and with the headnote's "present decay of eastern Europe."
---
line: 378
text: The woman who draws her long black hair out tight and plays it.

id: cities
title: Cities
intro: The places the poem names, in the order it names them: the Starnbergersee and the Hofgarten, in Munich, at the start; London through the middle, with the bridge, the streets and churches, a public bar in Lower Thames Street and the river at Greenwich and at Richmond, and among the London stops the places the poem’s people come from or remember, Lithuania, Mylae, Leman, Smyrna, Thebes, Margate and Carthage; then, in Part V, the five cities named in two lines, “Jerusalem Athens Alexandria / Vienna London” (375–376), and the Ganges and Himavant (396–398).
---
line: 8
text: The Starnbergersee and the Hofgarten: Munich, where Eliot spent the summer of 1911.
---
line: 12
text: Lithuania, by way of a sentence in German: "I am not Russian at all, I come from Lithuania, real German."
---
line: 60
text: "Unreal City": London, from Baudelaire's Paris.
---
line: 66
text: King William Street and St Mary Woolnoth: the commuters' way into the City from London Bridge. Eliot's bank stood by the church, and his note on line 68 says he "often noticed" the dead sound at nine.
---
line: 70
text: Mylae, the sea battle of 260 BC between Rome and Carthage.
---
line: 182
text: Leman is Lake Geneva. Eliot was at Lausanne, on the lake, from late November 1921 to early January 1922, and finished the poem there.
---
line: 209
text: Smyrna, now İzmir, the chief port of Asia Minor, through which the Levant's dried fruit was shipped; Eliot's note on 211 gives his reading of the line's trade shorthand.
---
line: 213
text: The Cannon Street Hotel, at the City station; and a weekend at the Metropole, usually taken as the Brighton hotel.
---
line: 245
text: Thebes, in Greece, Tiresias's city.
---
line: 258
text: The Strand, Queen Victoria Street, Lower Thames Street and, by London Bridge, Wren's church of St Magnus Martyr (Eliot's note at 264).
---
line: 275
text: Downstream of the City: Greenwich Reach, with the Isle of Dogs on its north bank.
---
line: 293
text: Highbury, in north London; Richmond and Kew, up the river; Moorgate, in the City; Margate, on the Kent coast.
---
line: 307
text: Carthage, where Augustine went as a student; his <i>Confessions</i> call it "a cauldron of unholy loves" (Eliot's note).
---
line: 375
text: "Jerusalem Athens Alexandria / Vienna London / Unreal."
---
line: 396
text: Ganga and Himavant: the Ganges and the Himalaya. After them the poem names only London Bridge and Aquitaine.

id: birds
title: Birds
intro: The poem’s birds, in the order they appear, with the lines where each is heard or named: the nightingale into which Philomel was changed (99–103 and 203–206), the gulls that Phlebas forgot (313), the hermit-thrush of line 357, which Eliot’s note says he heard in Quebec County, the cock on the rooftree (392) and the swallow of line 429. Three stops are for other creatures the poem sets beside them: the cricket of line 23, the cicada of line 354 and the bats of line 380. Recordings of the birds themselves, where they exist, are in the listening room.
---
line: 23
text: "the cricket no relief." Not a bird, but the first singer: Eliot's cricket stands in for the grasshopper of Ecclesiastes 12:5, the verse his note cites.
---
line: 99
text: Philomel, the nightingale, "Jug Jug."
---
line: 203
text: "Twit twit twit / Jug jug jug jug jug jug." The nightingale again (line 100), singing the words Elizabethan poets gave her song; "Tereu" is both one of those words and the name of Tereus, who raped her.
---
line: 313
text: Phlebas, a fortnight drowned, has forgotten "the cry of gulls".
---
line: 354
text: "Not the cicada." Not a bird: the dry sound that is there instead of water.
---
line: 357
text: The hermit-thrush, whose "water-dripping song" Eliot heard in Quebec. Listen to it.
---
line: 380
text: "bats with baby faces." Not birds, but they whistle and beat their wings.
---
line: 392
text: "Co co rico": the French spelling of a cock-crow. Readers have heard in it the cock of Peter's denial and the cock of <i>Hamlet</i> I.i.
---
line: 429
text: "O swallow swallow." Procne, sister of the Philomel of lines 99 and 203.

title: Nothing
intro: The word “nothing” wherever the poem uses it: at line 40, by the one who came back from the hyacinth garden and could not speak; at lines 120–126, in the exchange between the woman with bad nerves and the man who does not answer her; and at lines 302 and 305, by the third Thames-daughter on Margate Sands. A last stop, at line 402, is the thunder’s first question, “what have we given?”, where the word does not occur.

---
line: 302

---

id: eyes
title: Eyes
intro: Eyes in the poem, from the epigraph, where Trimalchio says he saw the Sibyl with his own eyes, to Part V: the eyes that fail in the hyacinth garden (line 39), the pearls that were the drowned man’s eyes (48), the one-eyed merchant (52), the eyes each man fixes before his feet on London Bridge (65), the “lidless eyes” of line 138, and the blind Tiresias (218). Eliot’s note on Part V names the journey to Emmaus, where, in Luke 24, the disciples’ eyes “were holden,” held, so that they did not know who walked beside them.

id: eliot
title: The poem in Eliot's life
intro: Places and dates from Eliot’s life that lie behind particular lines of the poem, from the summers of his boyhood at Gloucester, Massachusetts, and Munich in 1911 to Margate and Lausanne in the autumn of 1921 and Paris in January 1922, each set beside the lines it bears on.
---
line: 8
text: Munich, summer 1911: the Hofgarten and its colonnade, and the Starnbergersee, where Ludwig II of Bavaria was found drowned in 1886.
---
line: 35
text: The hyacinth garden. Two people from Eliot's life have been proposed for it: Emily Hale, in Lyndall Gordon's reading of the letters Eliot wrote her, and Jean Verdenal, whom Eliot remembered in 1934 crossing the Luxembourg Gardens "waving a branch of lilac". The poem names neither.
---
line: 66
text: Lloyds Bank's head office, at 71 Lombard Street, a few doors from St Mary Woolnoth. Eliot's note on line 68 says the dead sound on the final stroke of nine was "a phenomenon which I have often noticed."
---
line: 111
text: The Eliots' flat at 9 Clarence Gate Gardens, by Regent's Park, where he and his wife Vivien were both ill in 1921.
---
line: 139
text: Ellen Kellond, the Eliots' maid, whose story this was: Eliot called the passage "pure Ellen Kellond".
---
line: 182
text: Eliot spent late November and December 1921 at Lausanne, on Lake Geneva, in the care of the nerve doctor Roger Vittoz, and wrote Parts IV and V there.
---
line: 300
text: Eliot spent three weeks at Margate in October and November 1921, on sick leave from the bank, and drafted "some fifty lines" of Part III in a shelter on the front, probably the Nayland Rock shelter, listed in 2009, which still stands. The lines are thought to be the river song and the Thames-daughters' speeches.
---
line: 312
text: The voyage Pound cut from this section set out past the Dry Salvages, rocks off Cape Ann in Massachusetts. Eliot's family spent its summers at Gloucester, on the cape, when he was a boy.
---
line: 419
text: Probably a boat off Cape Ann, where Eliot sailed in the summers of his youth.
---
line: 0
text: Paris, January 1922. Eliot stopped there on his way back from Lausanne, and Pound went through the typescript with him, cutting it hard. The dedication came later. In 1923 Eliot wrote "il miglior fabbro" in Pound's copy, and from 1925 it was printed with the poem. The words are Dante's, for the poet Arnaut Daniel, and mean the better craftsman.
