anchor: like a burnished throne
kind: echo
source: shakespeare-antony
title: Cleopatra's barge, indoors
quote: The barge she sat in, like a burnish’d throne,<br>Burn’d on the water: the poop was beaten gold;<br>Purple the sails, and so perfumed that<br>The winds were love-sick with them . . .
body:
Enobarbus's description of Cleopatra on the Cydnus, <i>Antony and Cleopatra</i> Act II scene ii. Eliot changes one word, barge to Chair, and the river pageant becomes a woman's room: the dressing table, the mirror on its standards of carved vines with a golden cupid peeping out, the seven-branched candlestick, the jewels, the bottled perfumes. What follows is the poem's one sustained stretch of grand-manner blank verse, seventeen lines in a single sentence that winds through clause after clause until the reader is as "troubled, confused / And drowned" in the syntax as the sense is in odours. It is a parody, and it is also very beautiful, and it is meant to be both at once.

Two more queens are folded into the furniture. "Laquearia," the coffered ceiling (line 92), is Virgil's word for the gilded ceiling of Dido's palace at the feast where she falls in love with Aeneas, who will leave her (<i>Aeneid</i> I, 726, Eliot's note quotes the line). And the "sylvan scene" of line 98 is Milton's phrase for Eden as Satan first sees it (<i>Paradise Lost</i> IV, 140). Cleopatra, Dido, Eve: three women about to be undone in their gardens and palaces, and then Philomel.

id: philomel
line: 99
to: 103
anchor: The change of Philomel
kind: echo
source: ovid-met6
title: The nightingale's story
image: philomela
body:
<i>Metamorphoses</i> VI. Tereus, king of Thrace, married to Procne, rapes her sister Philomela and cuts out her tongue so she cannot tell. Philomela weaves what happened into a tapestry and sends it to Procne; the sisters kill Tereus's son Itys and serve him to his father at dinner; when he pursues them, all three are turned into birds. In the version the Elizabethans inherited, Philomela became the nightingale, Procne the swallow, Tereus the hoopoe. Her story hangs "above the antique mantel" as a painting, one of the "withered stumps of time" on the walls, and also as a tongue: "stumps." She sings anyway, "inviolable," and the world hears it as "Jug Jug," the Elizabethan poets' conventional spelling of nightingale-song, which the poem's "dirty ears" hear as a dirty joke. The rape comes back in Part III (lines 203–206) and the swallow in the last lines of the poem.

id: nerves
line: 111
to: 114
anchor: My nerves are bad tonight
kind: voice
title: The woman who wants to be spoken to
body:
Four lines in inverted commas, then one without. She speaks; he thinks. Every reader has heard Vivien Eliot in this voice, and there is reason to: the Eliots' marriage in 1921 was two people ill in the same flat, and the typescript of this scene carries Vivien's pencilled comments. But the writing is doing something more exact than transcription. Her questions are all about his silence ("Why do you never speak. Speak."), and his replies, which she cannot hear, are all quotations: rats' alley, the wind under the door, pearls that were his eyes, a rag. He is a man whose inner life is made of other people's lines. That is a diagnosis of the poem as much as of the marriage.

to: 116
anchor: rats’ alley
kind: gloss
title: The trenches
body:
"Rats' Alley" was a trench name on the Western Front, and rats in the trenches ate the unburied dead. Eliot's note sends you forward to line 195, where a rat drags its belly along the river bank and the bones are "Rattled by the rat's foot only, year to year." The war is three years over. It is the thing he is thinking about while she asks him what he is thinking about.

id: wind-door
line: 118
anchor: The wind under the door.
kind: echo
source: webster-devils-law-case
title: Is the wind in that door still?
quote: Is the wind in that door still?
body:
Eliot's note credits "Webster." The line is from <i>The Devil's Law-Case</i> (1623), Act III scene ii. Two surgeons have watched Romelio stab the wounded Contarino and have taken him for dead; when he groans, one asks the other whether the wind is "in that door still," meaning is that how things stand. The man is not dead: the stab has lanced his old wound and saved him. Neither is the man in this room, though "Nothing again nothing" is his answer to what the wind is doing.

id: rag
line: 128
to: 130
anchor: Shakespeherian Rag
kind: echo
source: shakespearian-rag
title: A hit song from 1912
quote: That Shakespearian rag,<br>Most intelligent, very elegant,<br>That old classical drag,<br>Has the proper stuff, the line “Lay on Macduff” . . .
body:
"That Shakespearian Rag" was a song of 1912, words by Gene Buck and Herman Ruby, music by David Stamper, which its publisher advertised as a hit of that year's Ziegfeld Follies; whether it was ever sung in the show is disputed. Its chorus, which rhymes "elegant" and "intelligent," is what the man in the chair has running through his head instead of an answer. Eliot adds a syllable ("Shakespeherian") to get the ragtime stutter into the word, and the four "O"s can be read as Lear's and Hamlet's dying "O, O, O, O" turned into a vamp. The high and the low do not so much collide as turn out to have been the same thing all along: Shakespeare is what you can hum.

id: chess
line: 137
to: 138
anchor: a game of chess
kind: echo
source: middleton-wbw
title: Middleton's chess game
body:
In Thomas Middleton's <i>Women Beware Women</i> (c. 1621), Act II scene ii, the procuress Livia keeps a mother busy over a chessboard downstairs while, in a gallery above, the Duke corners and rapes the mother's daughter-in-law Bianca. Every move on the board is a line about what is happening upstairs ("I have given thee blind mate twice"). The section's title comes from this scene, and from Middleton's <i>A Game at Chess</i> (1624), a political satire so popular the government shut it down. "Pressing lidless eyes" is insomnia; "a knock upon the door" is what people in Jacobean tragedy wait for.

In the typescript a further line stood between these two: "The ivory men make company between us." Vivien asked for it to be taken out, and it was. Eliot wrote it back in, from memory, when he made a fair copy of the poem in 1960.

id: hurry-up
line: 141
anchor: HURRY UP PLEASE ITS TIME
kind: voice
title: The publican's call
body:
Closing time. The landlord's shout, five times, breaking into the monologue at the moments it least wants interrupting. British licensing hours, fixed during the war and confirmed by the Licensing Act of 1921, meant every pub emptied at a set hour every night with the same words. The line has no apostrophe in the 1922 printings, which is how it would have been shouted. It is also the poem's clock: time is up, for Lil, for Albert, for everyone in the bar, and for the woman in the chair upstairs waiting for her hot water at ten.

id: lil
line: 139
to: 171
anchor: When Lil’s husband got demobbed
kind: voice
title: The pub monologue
body:
A woman telling a friend how she told Lil the truth. "Demobbed" (demobilised) was new slang in 1919–20 as the army came home. Albert has been away four years; Lil is thirty-one, has had five children, has lost her teeth and her looks, and has taken pills from the chemist "to bring it off," an abortion. The friend's advice is to get a nice set of false teeth and be smart, or lose him. The whole of it is in the friend's mouth, so we never hear Lil herself except as quoted, and the friend's version of her is as unsympathetic as Tiresias's version of the typist. It is the most direct speech in the poem and the most overheard.

Valerie Eliot recorded that the episode was told to the Eliots by Ellen Kellond, the maid who worked for them. In the typescript Vivien wrote "WONDERFUL" and "Yes" beside these lines, and the line "What you get married for if you dont want children?" is hers, pencilled in the margin and taken into the text.

quote: I hope all will be well. We must be patient: but I cannot choose but weep, to think they should lay him i’ the cold ground. My brother shall know of it: and so I thank you for your good counsel. Come, my coach! Good night, ladies; good night, sweet ladies; good night, good night.
body:
Ophelia's exit in <i>Hamlet</i> Act IV scene v, mad, before she drowns. The pub's "Goonight" slides without a break into her line, and the section that began with Cleopatra's barge ends with a girl in the water. "Good Night, Ladies" was also a parlour song everyone in the bar would have known, so the line is Shakespeare and a sing-song at once, like the rag. Count the women in this section: the lady in the chair, Philomel, Lil, the friend, Ophelia. Eliot's note on Tiresias says "all the women are one woman."
