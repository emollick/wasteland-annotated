Pathways

Nine walks through the poem, each a sequence of places to stand. Choose one and the poem page opens with a small guide that takes you from stop to stop. They are not summaries; they are routes.

A first reading

Twelve stops for someone meeting the poem for the first time. Each stop is a place to stand, not an explanation. Read the lines, read the note, move on.

12 stops

Water

The poem's hydrology, from spring rain to the last rain. Follow the water and you follow the argument: water is what the waste land needs, water is what drowns you, and the two are never told apart.

18 stops

The dead

Burials, drownings, bones and the crowd that death has undone. The poem's first section is called The Burial of the Dead, from the Book of Common Prayer, and the dead never quite stay in it.

15 stops

The women

Eliot's note on Tiresias says "all the women are one woman." Here they are, in order of appearance, so that you can test the claim. Most of them speak; several are quotations; two are queens; one is a bird.

16 stops

Cities

The poem's geography, in the order the poem gives it. It begins in Munich and ends on the Ganges, with London at its centre and five capitals falling in two lines.

15 stops

Birds

Every bird in the poem is a musician, and every song is a complaint. Recordings, where they exist, are in the listening room.

9 stops

Nothing

One word, followed through the poem. It is said by a lover, a woman with bad nerves, a man who will not answer, a Thames-daughter, and the poor on Margate sands.

7 stops

Eyes

Eyes that fail, eyes turned to pearls, eyes fixed on feet, lidless eyes, a one-eyed merchant, a blind seer, eyes that were holden on the road to Emmaus. The poem's people cannot see, or see too much.

11 stops

The poem in Eliot's life

Places and days from the poet's own year, 1921 into 1922, that surface in the text. Not a key to the poem, but a map of where he was standing.

10 stops
