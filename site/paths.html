Pathways

Thirteen pathways through the poem, each a sequence of stops. Choose one and the poem page opens with a small guide in the corner that takes you from stop to stop.

A first reading

Start here if you have not read the poem before.

Twelve stops through the poem for a reader meeting it for the first time. Each stop gives a passage and a short note on it, in the order of the poem. Read the lines first, then the note, then go on to the next stop.

12 stops

The Grail and the waste land

The Grail legend behind the title, followed through the poem from the dead land of the first lines to the king fishing on the shore.

The Grail legend as Eliot took it from two books. They are Jessie L. Weston’s study of it, From Ritual to Romance (1920), and the anthropologist J. G. Frazer’s The Golden Bough. The note at the head of Eliot’s notes credits the title, the plan and “a good deal of the incidental symbolism” to Weston and names Frazer beside her. In 1956 Eliot regretted having sent readers on a “wild goose chase” after the Tarot and the Grail. Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve an old rite. In it the Fisher King’s wound leaves his land waste and its waters dry. When a knight asks the right question, the king is healed and the waters flow. In Frazer’s account the god of that rite (Adonis, Attis, Osiris) dies with the year and is buried or drowned so that he may return in spring. In 1939 the critic Cleanth Brooks made the legend the key to the poem.

13 stops

References Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II; Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXIX; Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXII; Brooks (1939); Eliot, “The Frontiers of Criticism” (1956).

The Bible and the Prayer Book

What the poem takes from the Bible and the Prayer Book, and what it leaves out, from the burial service that names Part I to the blessing quoted in Eliot’s last note.

The Bible and the Book of Common Prayer in the poem. Part I is named after the Prayer Book’s burial service. The desert of lines 19 to 30 speaks in words from Ezekiel and Ecclesiastes, and its red rock is usually set beside Isaiah’s “great rock in a weary land”. Part V opens with the arrest and death of Christ told without his name, as the critic Cleanth Brooks read it in 1939, and goes on to the road to Emmaus, where the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples. Eliot’s last note glosses “Shantih” with the blessing that ends the Communion service. Eliot was baptised into the Church of England in June 1927, five years after the poem.

13 stops

References Book of Common Prayer, the Order for the Burial of the Dead; King James Bible, Isaiah 32:2; Brooks (1939); Southam, Guide, Biographical Table.

The Fire Sermon and the thunder

The Buddha and the Upanishads in the poem, from the sermon that names Part III to the thunder’s three commands and the last word.

The Indian texts in the poem, the Buddha’s Fire Sermon and an Upanishad. Eliot studied Sanskrit at Harvard for two years from 1911 with Charles Rockwell Lanman. He also spent a year on the metaphysics of the ancient Indian philosopher Patañjali with James Haughton Woods, a study that left him, he wrote in 1934, in “a state of enlightened mystification”. Part III takes its title from the sermon in which the Buddha says that all things are on fire. The sermon is heard at line 308, beside St Augustine’s Confessions, a pair Eliot’s note on line 309 (numbered 312 in 1922) calls “representatives of eastern and western asceticism”. Part V’s title and last lines come from the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, where the thunder’s one syllable is heard as three commands. “Shantih”, repeated, is the formula that closes an Upanishad. Cleo McNelly Kearns’s T. S. Eliot and Indic Traditions (1987) is the study of Eliot and these texts followed here.

10 stops

References Warren, Buddhism in Translations, §73; Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, V.2; Eliot, After Strange Gods (1934); Kearns (1987).

Dante and the dead

The poem’s dead taken in order and set beside Dante’s, from the crowd on London Bridge to the poet Arnaut Daniel in the fire of Purgatory.

The poem’s dead, and what they keep of Dante’s. Five of Eliot’s notes send the reader to Dante, more than to any other writer. Two send the crowd on London Bridge (63 and 64) to the Inferno, and a third sends line 412 there, to Count Ugolino, starved in his tower. Two send the reader to the Purgatorio, for La Pia, a murdered wife (293), and for the troubadour Arnaut Daniel in the refining fire (428). The dedication to Pound is taken from the canto in which Arnaut Daniel appears. Dante’s dead, met and known by name, tell the living who they were and ask to be remembered. This pathway follows the poem’s dead in order and asks what each passage keeps of Dante’s dead. Eliot wrote on Dante in The Sacred Wood (1920) and in his Dante of 1929, and in 1950 gave a talk, “What Dante Means to Me”.

10 stops

References The Sacred Wood, “Dante”; Eliot, Dante (1929); Eliot, “What Dante Means to Me” (1950).

The loves that fail

The poem’s couples and seductions, from the hyacinth garden to the typist’s divan, with what Tiresias and the thunder make of them.

The loves in the poem, and how each of them fails. Eliot’s note on line 218 says “all the women are one woman, and the two sexes meet in Tiresias”, the blind seer who has lived as both. The note at the head of Eliot’s notes credits Jessie Weston’s book on the Grail legend, From Ritual to Romance, with the poem’s plan. In the legend as she tells it the Fisher King is wounded. His wound is “the loss of virility”, and because of it nothing grows in his land, “a suspension of the reproductive processes of Nature”, until he is healed. The critic Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the poem through that legend, in which the Fisher King, the lord of the land, has been “rendered impotent by maiming or sickness”. The pathway takes the couples in order and sets each beside that wound. It ends at the thunder, where at line 421 a heart “would have responded”, in the conditional.

13 stops

References Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II; Brooks (1939).

Prophecy and sight

The poem’s prophets, fortune-tellers and blind seers, from the Sibyl in the epigraph to Isaiah’s warning near the end, with the eyes that fail between them.

Prophecy in the poem, and the sight it depends on. The poem is full of people who claim to see what others cannot, and it shows what each of them is allowed to see. Its epigraph, from the Satyricon of Petronius, is spoken by a man who says he saw the Sibyl of Cumae, a prophetess, with his own eyes, hanging in a jar; when boys asked her what she wanted, she said that she wanted to die. Eliot’s note on line 218 calls the blind prophet Tiresias the poem’s most important personage, and says that what he sees is the substance of the poem. What he foretells is a scene he says he has “foresuffered”, enacted before on the same bed. The thunder near the end says one syllable, and each hearer must make out the word for himself. Between the seers come the eyes that fail, from the hyacinth garden to the road to Emmaus, where two disciples walk beside the risen Christ and do not know him.

12 stops

Metamorphosis

Ovid’s changed bodies and the sea-change of Ariel’s song, from Philomel to the swallow, and whether anyone in the poem is changed.

The poem’s changed bodies, from Ovid and from The Tempest. Ovid’s Metamorphoses gives it Philomel, raped and turned into a nightingale (99), and Tiresias, who was a woman for seven years (218). The spirit Ariel’s song in The Tempest gives it a drowned king whose eyes are turned to pearls and whose body suffers “a sea-change / Into something rich and strange”. The poem quotes the pearls at 48 and 125 and does not quote the sea-change. This pathway follows what Ovid’s and Shakespeare’s bodies are turned into, what becomes of the poem’s own people, the drowned Phlebas among them, and the change the Latin of line 429 asks for, when shall I be like the swallow. The critic Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the clairvoyante Madame Sosostris’s warning, “Fear death by water”, as the words of one who does not know that “the way into life may be by death itself”, and held that nobody in the poem knows it.

9 stops

References Ovid, Metamorphoses VI 424–674; Ovid, Metamorphoses III 316–338; Shakespeare, The Tempest I.ii; Brooks (1939).

The past beside the present

The places where an old poem and a modern scene are made to share the same lines, from Chaucer’s April to the fragments at the end.

Old poems and modern scenes in the same lines. In November 1923, reviewing Joyce’s Ulysses in The Dial, Eliot wrote that “in manipulating a continuous parallel between contemporaneity and antiquity, Mr Joyce is pursuing a method which others must pursue after him”, and that the method gave “a shape and a significance to the immense panorama of futility and anarchy which is contemporary history”. He was writing about another man’s book, but the critic F. O. Matthiessen was applying the sentences to Eliot’s own work by 1935. This pathway follows the places where the parallel is plainest, where Chaucer, Baudelaire, Shakespeare, Spenser, Marvell, John Day or Goldsmith is set beside a modern scene in the same lines, so that the reader sees both at once. The critic Cleanth Brooks (1939) wrote that the poem’s surface parallels are really contrasts and its surface contrasts really parallels.

9 stops

References Eliot, “Ulysses, Order, and Myth” (1923); Matthiessen (1935), ch. II; Brooks (1939).

After the war

What the poem has of the war that had ended three years before, taken in order from the German line at 12 to London Bridge falling down.

The war of 1914–1918 and its aftermath, as the poem carries them. Eliot wrote the poem in 1921, three years after the Armistice, and published it in October 1922. At Lloyds Bank, where he worked, he had been put in charge in 1920 of settling the bank’s pre-war debts with Germany. The poem never names the war. Eliot’s own note at the head of Part V names “the present decay of eastern Europe” as a theme. The pathway follows the war’s traces in the poem’s order. Some are in the poem’s own words, and others are critics’ readings.

8 stops

References The Criterion, October 1922; Facsimile (1971), introduction; Letters, vol. 1, Eliot to his mother, 15 February 1920.

Wagner

What Wagner’s operas are doing in the poem, quotation by quotation, and where to hear the music.

Three of Wagner’s operas are in the poem. Tristan und Isolde is quoted twice in German: the sailor’s song that opens it (lines 31–34) and the shepherd’s report from its last act that the sea is empty (42). The Rhine-daughters’ cry from the last act of Götterdämmerung is set twice on the Thames (277–278, 290–291) and, as it is usually read, worn down to “la la” at 306. Parsifal, Wagner’s Grail opera, enters at line 202 through a sonnet on it by the French poet Paul Verlaine. Jessie Weston’s From Ritual to Romance is the book on the Grail legend that the note at the head of Eliot’s notes credits with the poem’s plan. Its preface says that conversations at the Bayreuth Festival of 1911, the festival of Wagner’s operas, gave her some of her evidence. Jean Verdenal, Eliot’s friend in Paris, wrote to him in 1912 that Tristan at a first hearing “leaves you prostrate with ecstasy.”

8 stops

References Weston, From Ritual to Romance, preface; Letters, vol. 1, Jean Verdenal to Eliot, 5 February 1912.

Cities

The places the poem names, from the Starnbergersee to the Ganges, taken in order with a note on each.

The places the poem names, in the order it names them. At the start come the Starnbergersee, a lake near Munich, and the Hofgarten in the city. Through the middle comes London: the bridge, the streets and churches, a public bar in Lower Thames Street, and the river at Greenwich and at Richmond. Among the London stops are the places the poem’s people come from or remember: Lithuania, Mylae, Leman, Smyrna, Thebes, Margate and Carthage. In Part V come the five cities named in two lines, “Jerusalem Athens Alexandria / Vienna London” (375–376), and the Ganges and Himavant (396–398).

Places and dates from Eliot’s life that lie behind particular lines of the poem, from the summers of his boyhood at Gloucester, Massachusetts, and Munich in 1911 to Margate and Lausanne in the autumn of 1921 and Paris in January 1922, each set beside the lines it bears on.
