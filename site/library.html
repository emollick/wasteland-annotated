Purgatorio, canto XXVI

Dante Alighieri · c. 1315 · Italian and Provençal

"il miglior fabbro" (the dedication) and "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina" (line 428).

«Tan m’abellis vostre cortes deman,
qu’ieu no me puesc ni voill a vos cobrire.
Ieu sui Arnaut, que plor e vau cantan;
consiros vei la passada folor,
e vei jausen lo joi qu’esper, denan.
Ara vos prec, per aquella valor
que vos guida al som de l’escalina,
sovenha vos a temps de ma dolor!».
Poi s’ascose nel foco che li affina.

"So much does your courteous question please me that I cannot and will not hide myself from you. I am Arnaut, who weep and go singing; in thought I see my past folly, and I see with joy, ahead, the joy I hope for. Now I pray you, by that power which guides you to the top of the stair, be mindful in time of my pain." Then he hid himself in the fire that refines them.

On the seventh terrace of Purgatory, where the lustful walk in flame, Dante meets Guido Guinizelli, who points out Arnaut Daniel as "miglior fabbro del parlar materno," the better craftsman of the mother tongue (line 117). Arnaut answers in his own Provençal, the only passage in the Comedy in a vernacular other than Italian, and steps back into the fire. Eliot took the dedication to Pound from line 117, the title of his 1920 collection Ara Vos Prec from line 145, and line 428 of the poem from line 148. Modern texts read "li affina"; Eliot's "gli" follows the editions he owned.

Inferno, canto III

Dante Alighieri · c. 1308 · Italian

The crowd on London Bridge: "I had not thought death had undone so many."

E io, che riguardai, vidi una ’nsegna
che girando correva tanto ratta,
che d’ogne posa mi parea indegna;
e dietro le venìa sì lunga tratta
di gente, ch’i’ non averei creduto
che morte tanta n’avesse disfatta.

And I, who looked, saw a banner which, whirling, ran so fast that it seemed to scorn all pause; and behind it came so long a train of people that I would never have believed death had undone so many.

Inside the gate ("Abandon all hope") but before the river Acheron, Dante finds the souls who were neither good nor bad, "who lived without infamy and without praise," rejected by Heaven and refused by Hell, running forever after a banner and stung by wasps. Eliot's commuters, going round in a ring, are these. He said in 1950 that Dante's poetry was still, "after forty years," "the most persistent and deepest influence" on his own verse.

Inferno, canto IV

Dante Alighieri · c. 1308 · Italian

"Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled."

Quivi, secondo che per ascoltare,
non avea pianto mai che di sospiri
che l’aura etterna facevan tremare.

Here, as far as I could tell by listening, there was no weeping but only sighs, which made the eternal air tremble.

Limbo, the first circle, home of the virtuous who died unbaptised: Homer, Virgil, Aristotle, the "great-hearted" souls who are not tormented but live in desire without hope. One line of the poem, one canto of Dante: the office crowd sigh like the noble pagans, not like the damned.

Purgatorio, canto V

Dante Alighieri · c. 1315 · Italian

La Pia's "Siena mi fé, disfecemi Maremma" behind "Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me."

«Deh, quando tu sarai tornato al mondo,
e riposato de la lunga via»,
seguitò ’l terzo spirito al secondo,
«ricorditi di me, che son la Pia;
Siena mi fé, disfecemi Maremma:
salsi colui che ’nnanellata pria
disposando m’avea con la sua gemma».

"Ah, when you have returned to the world and rested from the long way," the third spirit followed the second, "remember me, who am La Pia; Siena made me, Maremma unmade me: he knows it who, having first put a ring on me, wedded me with his gem."

Among the late-repentant who died by violence, a woman speaks seven lines and gives her name, her birthplace, the place of her death and, by implication, her murderer, her husband. Early commentators named her Pia de' Tolomei of Siena and her husband Nello dei Pannocchieschi; nothing certain is known of her. Eliot's first Thames-daughter borrows the grammar and keeps the anonymity.

Inferno, canto XXXIII

Dante Alighieri · c. 1308 · Italian

The key turning in the door: Ugolino's tower.

e io senti’ chiavar l’uscio di sotto
a l’orribile torre; ond’io guardai
nel viso a’ mie’ figliuoi sanza far motto.

and I heard them nailing up the door below of the horrible tower; at which I looked into the faces of my sons without a word.

Count Ugolino della Gherardesca of Pisa, imprisoned in 1288 with his sons and grandsons and left to starve, gnaws in Hell on the skull of the archbishop who betrayed him and tells Dante the story. Eliot's note pairs the passage with Bradley's philosophy of the closed circle of experience: everyone in a tower, listening for the key.

The Canterbury Tales, General Prologue

Geoffrey Chaucer · c. 1387 · Middle English

The kind April that Eliot's first line contradicts.

Whan that Aprill with his shoures soote
The droghte of March hath perced to the roote,
And bathed every veyne in swich licour
Of which vertu engendred is the flour;
Whan Zephirus eek with his sweete breeth
Inspired hath in every holt and heeth
The tendre croppes, and the yonge sonne
Hath in the Ram his half cours yronne,
And smale foweles maken melodye,
That slepen al the nyght with open ye
(So priketh hem Nature in hir corages),
Thanne longen folk to goon on pilgrimages.

When April with its sweet showers has pierced the drought of March to the root and bathed every vein in the liquid whose power engenders the flower; when Zephyrus with his sweet breath has quickened the tender shoots in every wood and heath, and the young sun has run half his course in the Ram, and small birds make melody that sleep all night with open eye (so Nature pricks them in their hearts), then people long to go on pilgrimages.

English poetry's first great spring, in which rain ends drought, sap rises and the whole country wants to travel. Eliot's poem also ends in a kind of pilgrimage, to the Chapel Perilous, but its April breeds lilacs out of dead land and its people would rather have stayed under the snow.

When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd

Walt Whitman · 1865 · English

Lilacs, spring, death, and a hermit thrush.

When lilacs last in the dooryard bloom’d,
And the great star early droop’d in the western sky in the night,
I mourn’d, and yet shall mourn with ever-returning spring. . . .

In the swamp in secluded recesses,
A shy and hidden bird is warbling a song.
Solitary the thrush,
The hermit withdrawn to himself, avoiding the settlements,
Sings by himself a song.
Song of the bleeding throat,
Death’s outlet song of life . . .

Whitman's elegy for Lincoln, shot in April 1865, binds three things together: the lilac, the evening star, and the hermit thrush singing in the swamp. Eliot's poem opens on lilacs out of dead land and, three hundred and fifty lines later, longs for the hermit-thrush's "water-dripping song." He rarely admitted to reading Whitman. The lilacs and the thrush say otherwise.

Les Sept Vieillards (from Les Fleurs du mal)

Charles Baudelaire · 1859; in the 1861 edition of Les Fleurs du mal · French

"Unreal City."

Fourmillante cité, cité pleine de rêves,
Où le spectre en plein jour raccroche le passant!
Les mystères partout coulent comme des sèves
Dans les canaux étroits du colosse puissant.

Swarming city, city full of dreams, where the ghost in broad daylight accosts the passer-by! Mysteries everywhere flow like sap in the narrow channels of the mighty giant.

The poet, walking through a yellow fog in a Paris morning, meets a hideous old man, then another exactly like him, and another, seven in all, and flees home half mad. Eliot's note quotes only the first two lines, and the whole "Unreal City" passage is built on their method: a real street, and a ghost on it at nine in the morning.

Au Lecteur (the preface poem of Les Fleurs du mal)

Charles Baudelaire · 1857 · French

"hypocrite lecteur!—mon semblable,—mon frère!"

C’est l’Ennui! — l’œil chargé d’un pleur involontaire,
Il rêve d’échafauds en fumant son houka.
Tu le connais, lecteur, ce monstre délicat,
— Hypocrite lecteur, — mon semblable, — mon frère!

It is Boredom! Its eye charged with an involuntary tear, it dreams of scaffolds while smoking its hookah. You know it, reader, this delicate monster, hypocrite reader, my double, my brother!

Baudelaire opens his book by listing the sins of mankind, saving the worst for last, boredom, and then turning on whoever is reading. Eliot closes his first section the same way. He said in 1930 that Baudelaire was "the greatest exemplar in modern poetry in any language," and in 1950 that he had learned from him "the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric."

Aeneid, Book I

Virgil · 19 BC · Latin

"laquearia," the coffered ceiling of Dido's palace.

dependent lychni laquearibus aureis
incensi et noctem flammis funalia vincunt.

Lit lamps hang from the golden coffered ceiling, and torches conquer the night with their flames.

Lines 726–727, quoted in Eliot's note: the banquet at which Dido, queen of Carthage, falls in love with Aeneas, who will sail away and leave her to the pyre. The word comes into the poem as a piece of interior decoration, and brings the whole story with it. Carthage again at line 307.

Paradise Lost, Book IV

John Milton · 1667 · English

"the sylvan scene": Eden as Satan first sees it.

. . . and overhead up grew
Insuperable height of loftiest shade,
Cedar, and pine, and fir, and branching palm,
A silvan scene, and as the ranks ascend
Shade above shade, a woody theatre
Of stateliest view.

Book IV, lines 137–142. Satan, arriving at Eden, looks at Paradise from outside like a man looking at a stage set; "sylvan scene" is Milton's phrase for what he sees. In the poem it is the painted landscape above the mantelpiece in which Philomel is raped.

Metamorphoses, Book VI: Tereus, Procne and Philomela

Ovid · AD 8 · Latin

"The change of Philomel."

Tereus of Thrace, escorting his wife's sister Philomela to visit Procne, rapes her in a hut in the woods and cuts out her tongue; she weaves the crime into a cloth and sends it to her sister; Procne kills her son Itys and serves him to his father; Tereus, pursuing the sisters with a sword, and the sisters, fleeing, all become birds. Ovid does not say which sister became the nightingale; later tradition made Philomela the nightingale and Procne the swallow, and Eliot follows it. The story sits above the mantel in Part II, breaks into birdsong in Part III, and ends the poem as a swallow. Arthur Golding's 1567 translation, the one Shakespeare read, is the English to go to.

Metamorphoses, Book III: Tiresias

Ovid · AD 8 · Latin

The man who had been a woman, and who sees the future.

Nam duo magnorum viridi coeuntia silva
corpora serpentum baculi violaverat ictu
deque viro factus, mirabile, femina septem
egerat autumnos; octavo rursus eosdem
vidit et “est vestrae si tanta potentia plagae,”
dixit “ut auctoris sortem in contraria mutet,
nunc quoque vos feriam!” percussis anguibus isdem
forma prior rediit genetivaque venit imago.

For once, in a green wood, he had struck with his staff the bodies of two great serpents as they coupled, and, from a man made a woman, wonderful to tell, had lived seven autumns; in the eighth he saw the same snakes again and said, "If a blow to you has such power that it changes its author's condition into its opposite, I will strike you now as well!" He struck the snakes, and his former shape returned, the form he was born with.

Lines 324–331 of the passage Eliot's note quotes at length, calling it "of great anthropological interest." Jupiter and Juno, disputing which sex takes more pleasure in love, consult the one person who has been both; his answer costs him his sight and earns him prophecy. Eliot's note makes him the poem's uniting consciousness. Ovid makes him a man who lost an argument with a goddess.

Prothalamion

Edmund Spenser · 1596 · English

"Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song."

Calme was the day, and through the trembling ayre
Sweete breathing Zephyrus did softly play
A gentle spirit, that lightly did delay
Hot Titans beames, which then did glyster fayre:
When I whom sullein care,
Through discontent of my long fruitlesse stay
In Princes Court, and expectation vayne
Of idle hopes, which still doe fly away,
Like empty shaddowes, did aflict my brayne,
Walkt forth to ease my payne
Along the shoare of silver streaming Themmes,
Whose rutty Bancke, the which his River hemmes,
Was paynted all with variable flowers,
And all the meades adornd with daintie gemmes,
Fit to decke maydens bowres,
And crowne their Paramours,
Against the Brydale day, which is not long:
Sweete Themmes runne softly, till I end my Song.

A "spousal verse" for the double wedding of the Earl of Worcester's daughters, in which the poet, out of favour at court, walks by the Thames and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans who are the brides. Eliot keeps the refrain and empties the river. Notice that Spenser's poet is also unhappy, also walking off his "sullein care" by the river, before the wedding cheers him up.

To His Coy Mistress

Andrew Marvell · written c. 1650; published 1681 · English

"But at my back . . . I hear."

But at my back I always hear
Time’s wingèd chariot hurrying near;
And yonder all before us lie
Deserts of vast eternity.
Thy beauty shall no more be found,
Nor, in thy marble vault, shall sound
My echoing song; then worms shall try
That long preserved virginity,
And your quaint honour turn to dust,
And into ashes all my lust:
The grave’s a fine and private place,
But none, I think, do there embrace.

The great English argument for going to bed now, because later there is only the grave. Eliot quotes the turn of the argument twice and gives it a rattle of bones the first time and motor horns the second. His 1921 essay on Marvell, written months before the poem, praised "a tough reasonableness beneath the slight lyric grace."

Parsifal

Paul Verlaine · 1886 · French

"Et O ces voix d'enfants, chantant dans la coupole!"

Parsifal a vaincu les Filles, leur gentil
Babil et la luxure amusante — et sa pente
Vers la Chair de garçon vierge que cela tente
D’aimer les seins légers et ce gentil babil;

Il a vaincu la Femme belle, au cœur subtil,
Étalant ses bras frais et sa gorge excitante;
Il a vaincu l’Enfer et rentre sous sa tente
Avec un lourd trophée à son bras puéril,

Avec la lance qui perça le Flanc suprême!
Il a guéri le roi, le voici roi lui-même,
Et prêtre du très saint Trésor essentiel.

En robe d’or il adore, gloire et symbole,
Le vase pur où resplendit le Sang réel.
— Et, ô ces voix d’enfants chantant dans la coupole!

Parsifal has overcome the Maidens, their pretty chatter and amusing lust, and his own leaning, a virgin boy's flesh tempted to love light breasts and that pretty chatter; he has overcome the beautiful Woman with the subtle heart, spreading her cool arms and her exciting throat; he has overcome Hell and returns to his tent with a heavy trophy on his boyish arm, with the lance that pierced the supreme Side! He has healed the king, and here he is king himself, and priest of the most holy essential Treasure. In a robe of gold he adores, glory and symbol, the pure vessel where the real Blood shines. And, oh, those children's voices singing in the dome!

Verlaine's sonnet on Wagner's last opera, written for the Revue wagnérienne, compresses the whole plot into thirteen lines and then, in the fourteenth, simply reports the sound of the boys' choir in the Grail temple. Eliot places that line after a barrack-room song about a brothel, so that the temptation and the temple stand one line apart. Parsifal is Weston's Grail knight; his healing of the king is the thing the poem never manages.

Fragment 104a (Hesperus)

Sappho · c. 600 BC · Greek

The evening hour that "brings the sailor home from sea."

Ἔσπερε πάντα φέρων ὄσα φαίνολις ἐσκέδασ’ Αὔως,
φέρεις ὄιν, φέρεις αἶγα, φέρεις ἄπυ μάτερι παῖδα.

Evening star, bringing back everything that shining dawn scattered: you bring the sheep, you bring the goat, you bring the child back to its mother.

Two lines that survive because a critic, Demetrius in his treatise On Style, quoted them. Eliot's note says his lines "may not appear as exact as Sappho's" and that he was thinking of the "longshore" fisherman coming in at nightfall. Byron and Stevenson ("Home is the sailor, home from sea") are the English relay stations between Sappho and the typist's teatime.

Pervigilium Veneris (The Vigil of Venus)

Anonymous · 2nd–4th century AD · Latin

"Quando fiam ceu chelidon": when shall I be like the swallow?

iam loquaces ore rauco stagna cycni perstrepunt;
adsonat Terei puella subter umbram populi,
ut putes motus amoris ore dici musico,
et neges queri sororem de marito barbaro.
illa cantat, nos tacemus. quando ver venit meum?
quando fiam uti chelidon, ut tacere desinam?
perdidi Musam tacendo, nec me Phoebus respicit.
sic Amyclas, cum tacerent, perdidit silentium.
cras amet qui numquam amavit quique amavit cras amet.

Now the chattering swans with hoarse voices make the pools ring; Tereus's girl sings under the poplar's shade, so that you would think the stirrings of love were spoken by her musical mouth, and not that a sister complained of a barbarous husband. She sings; we are silent. When does my spring come? When shall I become like the swallow, so that I may stop being silent? By silence I have lost the Muse, and Phoebus does not regard me. So Amyclae, when its people kept silent, was destroyed by silence. Tomorrow let him love who has never loved, and let him who has loved love tomorrow.

A late Latin poem of ninety-three lines for the eve of the spring festival of Venus, with the refrain "cras amet qui numquam amavit." After all the flowers and nymphs, the last stanza turns to the poet's own silence, and envies the swallow, who is Procne, sister of the raped Philomela. Eliot's 1922 text reads "ceu" for the poem's "uti," and so do his manuscripts; the line was changed to "uti" in Collected Poems 1909–1935 (1936). The whole of the poem's spring, its birds and its silence are in these nine lines.

El Desdichado (from Les Chimères)

Gérard de Nerval · 1854 · French

"Le Prince d'Aquitaine à la tour abolie."

Je suis le Ténébreux, — le Veuf, — l’Inconsolé,
Le Prince d’Aquitaine à la Tour abolie:
Ma seule Étoile est morte, — et mon luth constellé
Porte le Soleil noir de la Mélancolie.

Dans la nuit du Tombeau, Toi qui m’as consolé,
Rends-moi le Pausilippe et la mer d’Italie,
La fleur qui plaisait tant à mon cœur désolé,
Et la treille où le Pampre à la Rose s’allie.

Suis-je Amour ou Phébus? . . . Lusignan ou Biron?
Mon front est rouge encor du baiser de la Reine;
J’ai rêvé dans la Grotte où nage la Sirène . . .

Et j’ai deux fois vainqueur traversé l’Achéron:
Modulant tour à tour sur la lyre d’Orphée
Les soupirs de la Sainte et les cris de la Fée.

I am the dark one, the widower, the unconsoled, the Prince of Aquitaine with the ruined tower: my only star is dead, and my starred lute bears the black sun of Melancholy. In the night of the tomb, you who consoled me, give me back Posillipo and the Italian sea, the flower that so pleased my desolate heart, and the arbour where the vine and the rose are joined. Am I Love or Phoebus? Lusignan or Biron? My brow is still red from the queen's kiss; I have dreamed in the grotto where the siren swims. And twice, victorious, I have crossed the Acheron, modulating in turn on Orpheus's lyre the sighs of the saint and the cries of the fairy.

The first of Nerval's Chimères, written in the intervals of madness a year before he hanged himself in a Paris alley. The title is Spanish, "the disinherited one," from a shield in Ivanhoe; the "tour abolie" is often read as the Tarot's Tower. A poet who has twice crossed the river of the dead and come back, the sonnet says, and can still play. Eliot takes the second line and leaves the consolation.

Dans le Restaurant

T. S. Eliot · 1918 · French

The French original of "Death by Water."

Phlébas, le Phénicien, pendant quinze jours noyé,
Oubliait les cris des mouettes et la houle de Cornouaille,
Et les profits et les pertes, et la cargaison d’étain:
Un courant de sous-mer l’emporta très loin,
Le repassant aux étapes de sa vie antérieure.
Figurez-vous donc, c’était un sort pénible;
Cependant, ce fut jadis un bel homme, de haute taille.

Phlebas the Phoenician, drowned a fortnight, forgot the cries of the gulls and the swell off Cornwall, and the profits and the losses, and the cargo of tin: an undersea current carried him far away, taking him back through the stages of his former life. Consider then, it was a hard fate; and yet he was once a handsome man, and tall.

One of the four poems Eliot wrote in French in 1917–18, in which a waiter tells a customer about a childhood fumble under a tree in the rain, and the customer, disgusted, tells him to go and wash; then, out of nowhere, the drowned Phoenician. Eliot translated the last stanza into English for Part IV, dropping the tin and Cornwall and adding "Gentile or Jew." He is the only poet the poem quotes in two languages.

The Death of Saint Narcissus

T. S. Eliot · 1915 (withdrawn from publication) · English

"Come in under the shadow of this red rock."

Come under the shadow of this gray rock—
Come in under the shadow of this gray rock,

Set in type for Poetry (Chicago) in 1915 and withdrawn at proof stage, the poem about a saint who wanted to be a tree, a fish and a girl, and died pierced with arrows, survived among the manuscripts Eliot sent to John Quinn in 1922 along with the Waste Land drafts. Its opening goes on, in the cadence the later poem keeps, to promise the listener something other than his own shadow on the sand or against the fire: the saint's bloody cloth and limbs. The gray rock's shadow, made red, became the poem's first prophecy. The poem is still in copyright, so only its first two lines are given here.

Maud, and The Princess

Alfred Tennyson · 1855; 1847 · English

"a handful of dust"; "O swallow swallow."

Dead, long dead,
Long dead!
And my heart is a handful of dust,
And the wheels go over my head,
And my bones are shaken with pain,
For into a shallow grave they are thrust,
Only a yard beneath the street . . . (Maud, II.v)

O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South,
Fly to her, and fall upon her gilded eaves,
And tell her, tell her, what I tell to thee. (The Princess)

The mad speaker of Maud, who imagines himself buried alive under a city street with the traffic going over him, is a Victorian ancestor of the man in rats' alley; the swallow song in The Princess gives Eliot the English half of a Latin line. Eliot's respect for Tennyson's ear ("the finest ear of any English poet since Milton," he wrote in 1936) shows in what he took.

The White Devil

John Webster · 1612 · English

Cornelia's dirge ("keep the wolf far thence") and Flamineo on the spider and the winding-sheet.

Call for the robin-redbreast and the wren,
Since o’er shady groves they hover,
And with leaves and flowers do cover
The friendless bodies of unburied men.
Call unto his funeral dole
The ant, the field-mouse, and the mole,
To rear him hillocks that shall keep him warm,
And (when gay tombs are robb’d) sustain no harm;
But keep the wolf far thence, that’s foe to men,
For with his nails he’ll dig them up again. (V.iv)

O men,
That lie upon your death-beds, and are haunted
With howling wives! ne’er trust them; they’ll re-marry
Ere the worm pierce your winding-sheet, ere the spider
Make a thin curtain for your epitaphs. (V.vi)

Webster's tragedy of Vittoria Corombona, the "white devil," and her murderous lover Brachiano. Cornelia sings the dirge over her son Marcello, killed by his brother Flamineo; Flamineo gives the speech about widows as he rises from a feigned death, having just learned that his sister would have let him die. Eliot's essays on the Elizabethan dramatists and his poem "Whispers of Immortality" ("Webster was much possessed by death / And saw the skull beneath the skin") explain why these two speeches were in his head.

The Devil's Law-Case

John Webster · 1623 · English

"The wind under the door."

Is the wind in that door still?

Act III scene ii. Two surgeons have just been bribed by Romelio, who has stabbed the wounded Contarino in his bed to finish him off; when the body groans, one of them asks whether "the wind" is "in that door still," meaning whether that is still how things stand. It is: the stab has lanced the old wound, and Contarino lives. Eliot's note gives only the line and the name.

The Tempest, Act I scene ii

William Shakespeare · c. 1611 · English

The Elizabethan convention that the nightingale sings "jug jug" and calls "tereu," the vocative of Tereus, is at its plainest in this song from Lyly's court comedy; the songs in Lyly's plays were first printed in the collected edition of 1632, and whether he wrote them is disputed. Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 5.2: what the thunder says

Anonymous (Vedic) · c. 700 BC · Sanskrit

"DA. Datta. Dayadhvam. Damyata." and "Shantih."

The threefold offspring of Prajāpati, gods, men and demons, lived with their father as students. When they had finished, the gods said: Teach us. He spoke one syllable, "Da," and asked: Did you understand? They said: We understood; you told us, dāmyata, restrain yourselves. Then the men: Teach us. "Da." We understood; you told us, datta, give. Then the demons: Teach us. "Da." We understood; you told us, dayadhvam, be compassionate. This is what the divine voice, the thunder, repeats: da, da, da: restrain yourselves, give, be compassionate. Eliot read the Upanishads in Sanskrit at Harvard with Charles Lanman in 1911–13, and his note cites Paul Deussen's German translation of 1897. The fable is about hearing what you need to hear in a single syllable. Eliot reorders the three commands and gives each a confession. "Shantih," the peace that closes an Upanishad, is spoken three times at the end of a recitation; the poem ends the way a Vedic text ends, and the notes end by calling the English "a feeble translation."

Anthropology

From Ritual to Romance

Jessie L. Weston · 1920 · English

The title, the plan, the Fisher King, the Chapel Perilous, the Syrian merchants.

Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance who came to believe, under the influence of Frazer, that the Grail stories preserved the remains of an ancient fertility cult: a wounded king, a land gone waste, a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics, and a ritual of initiation whose secrets travelled Europe with Syrian merchants. Almost no medievalist now accepts the argument. It does not matter. Eliot's note says the book suggested "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism," and thirty-four years later he said he regretted "having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail." Read chapters II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel) and you have the poem's skeleton; read the rest and you have a very good bad book.

The Golden Bough (Adonis, Attis, Osiris)

Sir James George Frazer · 1906–15 (third edition); one-volume abridgement 1922 · English

The dying and reviving god; the buried corpse that sprouts; the Hanged God; the effigy thrown into the sea.

Frazer's twelve-volume comparative study of magic and religion, which Eliot's headnote says "has influenced our generation profoundly," argued that behind the myths of Adonis, Attis, Osiris and, by implication, Christ lay a single agricultural drama: the god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped along by ritual (a sacrificed king, an effigy buried or drowned). The volumes Eliot names describe the "gardens of Adonis," pots of quick-sprouting seed thrown into the sea, and the effigies of Osiris "made of earth and corn," buried to sprout. Stetson's planted corpse, Phlebas in the current, the Hanged Man and the whole question of whether April can bring anything back are Frazer's. The book's title and its opening page come from Turner's painting The Golden Bough (1834), which Frazer took for a view of Nemi; it shows the Sibyl at Lake Avernus.

"London Bridge is falling down falling down falling down."

London Bridge is falling down,
Falling down, falling down,
London Bridge is falling down,
My fair lady.

Build it up with wood and clay . . .
Build it up with iron and steel . . .
Build it up with silver and gold . . .
Set a man to watch all night . . .

The rhyme runs through materials that fail until a watchman is set; folklorists of Frazer's generation liked to connect it with the old custom of burying a victim under a bridge's foundations to make it stand. Eliot's commuters crossed the real bridge at line 62. The nursery version is the first of the fragments shored against the ruins, and the only one in English a child would know.

That Shakespearian Rag

Gene Buck and Herman Ruby (words), David Stamper (music) · 1912 · English

"O O O O that Shakespeherian Rag— / It's so elegant / So intelligent."

That Shakespearian rag,
Most intelligent, very elegant,
That old classical drag,
Has the proper stuff, the line “Lay on Macduff” . . .

Published in 1912 and advertised as a number from that year's Ziegfeld Follies, though it seems never to have been sung in the show and was first performed in vaudeville in January 1913; Shakespeare's characters are made to dance the ragtime steps of the day. Eliot knew the popular songs of his Harvard years well enough to misquote one on purpose: his extra syllable, "Shakespeherian," gives the word the rag's syncopation. No recording from the period is known.
