Purgatorio, canto XXVI

Dante Alighieri · c. 1315 · Italian and Provençal

The dedication calls Ezra Pound, who cut the poem, “il miglior fabbro,” the better craftsman, Dante’s phrase for the Provençal poet Arnaut Daniel; line 428 is Arnaut stepping back into the refining fire.

«Tan m’abellis vostre cortes deman,
qu’ieu no me puesc ni voill a vos cobrire.
Ieu sui Arnaut, que plor e vau cantan;
consiros vei la passada folor,
e vei jausen lo joi qu’esper, denan.
Ara vos prec, per aquella valor
que vos guida al som de l’escalina,
sovenha vos a temps de ma dolor!».
Poi s’ascose nel foco che li affina.

“So much does your courteous question please me that I cannot and will not hide myself from you. I am Arnaut, who weep and go singing; in thought I see my past folly, and I see with joy, ahead, the joy I hope for. Now I pray you, by that power which guides you to the top of the stair, be mindful in time of my pain.” Then he hid himself in the fire that refines them.

On the seventh terrace of Purgatory, where the lustful walk in flame, Dante meets the poet Guido Guinizelli, who points out the troubadour Arnaut Daniel as “miglior fabbro del parlar materno,” the better craftsman of the mother tongue (line 117). Arnaut answers in his own Provençal, the only passage in the Comedy in a vernacular other than Italian, and steps back into the fire. Eliot took the title of his 1920 collection Ara Vos Prec from line 145 (the Ovid Press title page misprinted it “Ara Vus Prec,” a form Eliot later blamed on his Italian edition of Dante), and line 428 of the poem from line 148, which his note quotes with the three lines before it. The dedication “For Ezra Pound / il miglior fabbro” was not printed in 1922: Eliot wrote it in January 1923 into the copy of the edition published by Boni & Liveright that belonged to Pound, who had cut the poem, and it first appeared in print in Poems 1909–1925. The phrase was already Pound’s own: “Il Miglior Fabbro” is the title of his chapter on Arnaut Daniel in The Spirit of Romance (1910). Modern texts read “li affina”; Eliot’s “gli” follows the older text he used.

II; Southam, Guide, note on the title Ara Vos Prec; Southam, Guide, note on the dedication of The Waste Land.

Inferno, canto III

Dante Alighieri · c. 1308 · Italian

The crowd on London Bridge: “I had not thought death had undone so many.”

E io, che riguardai, vidi una ’nsegna
che girando correva tanto ratta,
che d’ogne posa mi parea indegna;
e dietro le venìa sì lunga tratta
di gente, ch’i’ non averei creduto
che morte tanta n’avesse disfatta.

And I, who looked, saw a banner which, whirling, ran so fast that it seemed to scorn all pause; and behind it came so long a train of people that I would never have believed death had undone so many.

Inside the gate (“Abandon all hope”) but before the river Acheron, Dante finds the souls who were neither good nor bad, “who lived without infamy and without praise.” Heaven has cast them out and Hell will not receive them; they run for ever after a banner, stung by wasps. Eliot’s note on line 63 quotes lines 55–57 as the source of the crowd flowing over London Bridge, and his note on line 46 says that the “crowds of people” in Madame Sosostris’s cards “appear later,” which has generally been taken to mean this crowd. Eliot said in “What Dante Means to Me” (1950) that Dante’s poetry was still, “after forty years,” “the most persistent and deepest influence” on his own verse.

References Dante, Inferno III 34–69; Eliot, “What Dante Means to Me” (1950).

Inferno, canto IV

Dante Alighieri · c. 1308 · Italian

Limbo’s sighs, behind “Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled.”

Quivi, secondo che per ascoltare,
non avea pianto mai che di sospiri
che l’aura etterna facevan tremare.

Here, as far as I could tell by listening, there was no weeping but only sighs, which made the eternal air tremble.

Limbo, the first circle of Hell, holds the virtuous who died unbaptised, Homer, Virgil and Aristotle among them; Dante calls them the “great spirits” (spiriti magni), and they are not tormented but live in desire without hope. Eliot’s note on line 64 cites these lines. The comparison likens the sighs of the crowd on London Bridge to those of Limbo, where there is grief but no torment.

References Dante, Inferno IV 25–45 and 118–120.

Purgatorio, canto V

Dante Alighieri · c. 1315 · Italian

La Pia’s “Siena made me, Maremma unmade me,” the model for “Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me.”

«Deh, quando tu sarai tornato al mondo,
e riposato de la lunga via»,
seguitò ’l terzo spirito al secondo,
«ricorditi di me, che son la Pia;
Siena mi fé, disfecemi Maremma:
salsi colui che ’nnanellata pria
disposando m’avea con la sua gemma».

“Ah, when you have returned to the world and rested from the long way,” the third spirit followed the second, “remember me, who am La Pia; Siena made me, Maremma unmade me: he knows it who, having first put a ring on me, wedded me with his gem.”

On the lower slopes of Purgatory, among those who repented only at a violent death, a woman speaks seven lines and gives her name, her birthplace, the place of her death and, without quite saying it, that her husband had her killed. The Maremma is the marsh country on the Tuscan coast. Early commentators named her Pia de’ Tolomei of Siena and her husband Nello dei Pannocchieschi; nothing certain is known of her. Eliot’s note on line 293 cites the passage for the first Thames-daughter, whose “Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me” follows the shape of “Siena mi fé, disfecemi Maremma”; unlike La Pia, she gives no name.

References Dante, Purgatorio V 130–136; Temple Classics Purgatorio, note on V 130–136.

Inferno, canto XXXIII

Dante Alighieri · c. 1308 · Italian

The key turning in the door: Ugolino’s tower.

e io senti’ chiavar l’uscio di sotto
a l’orribile torre; ond’io guardai
nel viso a’ mie’ figliuoi sanza far motto.

and I heard them nailing up the door below of the horrible tower; at which I looked into the faces of my sons without a word.

Count Ugolino della Gherardesca of Pisa, imprisoned in 1288 with two sons and two grandsons and left to starve, gnaws in Hell on the skull of Archbishop Ruggieri, who betrayed him, and tells Dante the story. The verb in the passage, chiavar, has been read both as locking and as nailing up the door; the translation above has “nailing up,” and Eliot’s “key” takes it as locking. His note on line 412 quotes the first of these lines and the start of the second, and then a passage from Appearance and Reality (1893) by F. H. Bradley, the philosopher on whom Eliot wrote his doctoral thesis; in it Bradley says that each person’s experience “falls within my own circle, a circle closed on the outside.”

References Dante, Inferno XXXIII 46–48; Bradley, Appearance and Reality, p. 346; Eliot, Knowledge and Experience (1964).

The Canterbury Tales, General Prologue

Geoffrey Chaucer · c. 1387 · Middle English

Chaucer’s April of sweet showers and pilgrimages, behind Eliot’s “cruellest month.”

Whan that Aprill with his shoures soote
The droghte of March hath perced to the roote,
And bathed every veyne in swich licour
Of which vertu engendred is the flour;
Whan Zephirus eek with his sweete breeth
Inspired hath in every holt and heeth
The tendre croppes, and the yonge sonne
Hath in the Ram his half cours yronne,
And smale foweles maken melodye,
That slepen al the nyght with open ye
(So priketh hem Nature in hir corages),
Thanne longen folk to goon on pilgrimages.

When April with its sweet showers has pierced the drought of March to the root and bathed every vein in the liquid whose power engenders the flower; when Zephyrus with his sweet breath has quickened the tender shoots in every wood and heath, and the young sun has run half his course in the Ram, and small birds make melody that sleep all night with open eye (so Nature pricks them in their hearts), then people long to go on pilgrimages.

The Prologue opens with April rain ending the drought of March, sap rising, birds singing and people longing to go on pilgrimage. Eliot’s notes say nothing of Chaucer; readers have long set “April is the cruellest month” against these lines, in which the same month and the same rain are welcome. In Eliot’s poem it is winter that “kept us warm” (lines 5–6).

References Chaucer, General Prologue, lines 1–12; Southam, Guide, note on line 1.

When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom’d

Walt Whitman · 1865 · English

The American poet Walt Whitman’s elegy for Abraham Lincoln, shot in April 1865: lilacs, spring, and the hermit thrush.

When lilacs last in the dooryard bloom’d,
And the great star early droop’d in the western sky in the night,
I mourn’d, and yet shall mourn with ever-returning spring. . . .

In the swamp in secluded recesses,
A shy and hidden bird is warbling a song.
Solitary the thrush,
The hermit withdrawn to himself, avoiding the settlements,
Sings by himself a song.
Song of the bleeding throat,
Death’s outlet song of life . . .

Whitman’s elegy for President Abraham Lincoln, who was shot in April 1865, brings together the lilac, the evening star and the hermit thrush singing in a swamp. Eliot’s poem opens with lilacs bred out of the dead land (line 2) and the hermit-thrush’s “water-dripping song” is at line 357; Eliot’s note on that line describes the bird and quotes the Handbook of Birds of Eastern North America by the ornithologist Frank M. Chapman, and says nothing of Whitman. In 1928, introducing the Selected Poems of Ezra Pound, who had cut the poem, Eliot wrote that he “did not read Whitman until much later in life, and had to conquer an aversion to his form, as well as to much of his matter, in order to do so.” Two years earlier, reviewing a life of Whitman, he had written that “when Whitman speaks of the lilacs or of the mocking-bird, his theories and beliefs drop away like a needless pretext” (“Whitman and Tennyson,” Nation and Athenaeum, 18 December 1926). The Auckland scholar S. Musgrove argued at length in 1952 that the poem drew on Whitman all the same, and set the lilacs bred out of the dead land and the hermit-thrush of line 357 beside the lilac and the thrush of this elegy.

References Whitman, “When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom’d”, sections 1 and 4; Eliot, introduction to Pound’s Selected Poems (1928); Eliot, “Whitman and Tennyson” (1926); Musgrove (1952).

Les Sept Vieillards (from Les Fleurs du mal)

Charles Baudelaire · 1859; in the 1861 edition of Les Fleurs du mal · French

“Unreal City”: the swarming Paris of “The Seven Old Men.”

Fourmillante cité, cité pleine de rêves,
Où le spectre en plein jour raccroche le passant!
Les mystères partout coulent comme des sèves
Dans les canaux étroits du colosse puissant.

Swarming city, city full of dreams, where the ghost in broad daylight accosts the passer-by! Mysteries everywhere flow like sap in the narrow channels of the mighty giant.

The poet, walking through a dirty yellow fog on a Paris morning, meets a hideous old man, then another exactly like him, and another, seven in all, and flees home half mad. Eliot’s note on line 60 quotes the first two lines. Stetson, hailed on King William Street at nine in the morning as a shipmate from the battle of Mylae, a sea battle of 260 BC (lines 69–70), has been read as Eliot’s version of Baudelaire’s daylight ghost.

References Baudelaire, Les Fleurs du mal, “Les Sept Vieillards”, lines 1–4.

Au Lecteur (the preface poem of Les Fleurs du mal)

Charles Baudelaire · 1855; the preface poem of the 1857 volume · French

“hypocrite lecteur!—mon semblable,—mon frère!”

C’est l’Ennui! — l’œil chargé d’un pleur involontaire,
Il rêve d’échafauds en fumant son houka.
Tu le connais, lecteur, ce monstre délicat,
— Hypocrite lecteur, — mon semblable, — mon frère!

It is Boredom! Its eye charged with an involuntary tear, it dreams of scaffolds while smoking its hookah. You know it, reader, this delicate monster, hypocrite reader, my double, my brother!

Baudelaire opens his book by listing the sins of mankind, keeping the worst, boredom, for last, and then turning on whoever is reading. Eliot closes his first section the same way. In his essay “Baudelaire” (1930) he called him “the greatest exemplar in modern poetry in any language,” and in “What Dante Means to Me” (1950) he said he had learned from him “the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric.”

References Baudelaire, Les Fleurs du mal, “Au Lecteur”, lines 37–40; Eliot, “Baudelaire” (1930); Eliot, “What Dante Means to Me” (1950).

Aeneid, Book I

“laquearia,” the coffered ceiling of the hall where Dido, queen of Carthage, feasts Aeneas.

dependent lychni laquearibus aureis

Lit lamps hang from the golden coffered ceiling, and torches conquer the night with their flames.

Eliot’s note on line 92 quotes lines 726–727, from the banquet at which Dido, queen of Carthage, falls in love with Aeneas; he will sail away, and she will kill herself on a pyre. Paradise Lost, Book IV

John Milton · 1667 · English

“the sylvan scene”: Eden as Satan first sees it.

. . . and overhead up grew
Insuperable height of loftiest shade,
Cedar, and pine, and fir, and branching palm,
A silvan scene, and as the ranks ascend
Shade above shade, a woody theatre
Of stateliest view.

Eliot’s note on line 98 cites Book IV, line 140. Satan, arriving at Eden, looks in at Paradise from outside, the trees rising rank above rank into “a woody theatre”; “silvan scene” (the poem spells it “sylvan”) and “theatre” are Milton’s words for the view. In the poem the phrase describes the painting over the mantel, which shows the change of Philomel.

Metamorphoses, Book VI: Tereus, Procne and Philomela

Ovid · AD 8 · Latin

“The change of Philomel.”

corpora Cecropidum pennis pendere putares:
pendebant pennis. quarum petit altera silvas,
altera tecta subit, neque adhuc de pectore caedis
excessere notae, signataque sanguine pluma est.
ille dolore suo poenaeque cupidine velox
vertitur in volucrem, cui stant in vertice cristae.
prominet inmodicum pro longa cuspide rostrum;
nomen epops volucri, facies armata videtur.

As they fly from him you would think that the bodies of the two Athenians were poised on wings: they were poised on wings! One flies to the woods, the other rises to the roof. And even now their breasts have not lost the marks of their murderous deed, their feathers are stained with blood. Tereus, swift in pursuit because of his grief and eager desire for vengeance, is himself changed into a bird. Upon his head a stiff crest appears, and a huge beak stands forth instead of his long sword. He is the hoopoe, with the look of one armed for war. (Frank Justus Miller, 1916; Metamorphoses VI, 667–674)

Tereus, king of Thrace, is bringing his wife Procne’s sister Philomela to visit her when he rapes Philomela in a hut deep in the woods and cuts out her tongue; she weaves the crime into a cloth and sends it to her sister; the sisters kill Procne’s son Itys and serve him to his father; Tereus, pursuing the sisters with a sword, and the sisters, fleeing, are all turned into birds. Ovid names only Tereus’s bird, the hoopoe, and does not say which sister became the nightingale and which the swallow; the later tradition that Eliot follows makes Philomela the nightingale. Eliot’s note on line 99 cites the story for the picture over the mantel in Part II; the “Jug Jug” of lines 103 and 204 is the nightingale’s song as Elizabethan poets wrote it; and his note on line 429 refers the swallow back to “Philomela in Parts II and III.” Arthur Golding’s translation of 1567, the one Shakespeare knew, gives the story in Book VI.

References Ovid, Metamorphoses VI 424–674; Golding, Metamorphoses (1567), bk. VI.

Metamorphoses, Book III: Tiresias

Ovid · AD 8 · Latin

Tiresias, who lived seven years as a woman and was then struck blind and given prophecy.

Nam duo magnorum viridi coeuntia silva
corpora serpentum baculi violaverat ictu
deque viro factus, mirabile, femina septem
egerat autumnos; octavo rursus eosdem
vidit et “est vestrae si tanta potentia plagae,”
dixit “ut auctoris sortem in contraria mutet,
nunc quoque vos feriam!” percussis anguibus isdem
forma prior rediit genetivaque venit imago.

For once, in a green wood, he had struck with his staff the bodies of two great serpents as they coupled, and, from a man made a woman, wonderful to tell, had lived seven autumns; in the eighth he saw the same snakes again and said, “If a blow to you has such power that it changes its author’s condition into its opposite, I will strike you now as well!” He struck the snakes, and his former shape returned, the form he was born with.

The passage is Book III, lines 324–331; Eliot’s note on line 218 quotes the whole episode (lines 320–338) in Latin, calling it “of great anthropological interest.” Jupiter and Juno, disputing in jest which sex takes more pleasure in love, put the question to Tiresias, who has been both; he sides with Jupiter, Juno strikes him blind, and Jupiter gives him foresight in return. Eliot’s note calls Tiresias “the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest,” and says that “what Tiresias sees, in fact, is the substance of the poem.”

References Ovid, Metamorphoses III 316–338.

Prothalamion

Edmund Spenser · 1596 · English

“Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song.”

Calme was the day, and through the trembling ayre
Sweete breathing Zephyrus did softly play
A gentle spirit, that lightly did delay
Hot Titans beames, which then did glyster fayre:
When I whom sullein care,
Through discontent of my long fruitlesse stay
In Princes Court, and expectation vayne
Of idle hopes, which still doe fly away,
Like empty shaddowes, did aflict my brayne,
Walkt forth to ease my payne
Along the shoare of silver streaming Themmes,
Whose rutty Bancke, the which his River hemmes,
Was paynted all with variable flowers,
And all the meades adornd with daintie gemmes,
Fit to decke maydens bowres,
And crowne their Paramours,
Against the Brydale day, which is not long:
Sweete Themmes runne softly, till I end my Song.

A “Spousall Verse,” as its title page calls it, for the double wedding of the Earl of Worcester’s daughters, Elizabeth and Katherine Somerset. The poet, out of favour at court, walks by the Thames and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans, who stand for the brides. Eliot’s note to line 176 cites the poem. He keeps Spenser’s refrain; his nymphs are twice said to have departed (lines 175 and 179), and where Spenser’s river carries the swans, his bears no litter of summer nights. Spenser’s poet is himself unhappy, walking by the river to ease the “sullein care” of a fruitless wait at court.

References Spenser, Prothalamion, lines 1–18 and the title page.

To His Coy Mistress

Andrew Marvell · written c. 1650; published 1681 · English

“But at my back I always hear / Time’s wingèd chariot hurrying near.”

But at my back I always hear
Time’s wingèd chariot hurrying near;
And yonder all before us lie
Deserts of vast eternity.
Thy beauty shall no more be found,
Nor, in thy marble vault, shall sound
My echoing song; then worms shall try
That long preserved virginity,
And your quaint honour turn to dust,
And into ashes all my lust:
The grave’s a fine and private place,
But none, I think, do there embrace.

Marvell’s speaker urges his mistress not to wait, because time is short, and in the grave there is no embracing. Eliot’s note cites the poem. Twice he begins a line with Marvell’s “But at my back,” and each time puts another sound where the chariot was: at lines 185–186 “The rattle of the bones,” at lines 196–198 “The sound of horns and motors,” which brings Sweeney to Mrs Porter. Eliot’s essay “Andrew Marvell,” in the Times Literary Supplement of 31 March 1921, while he was at work on the poem, praised in Marvell “a tough reasonableness beneath the slight lyric grace.”

References Marvell, “To His Coy Mistress”, lines 21–32; Eliot, “Andrew Marvell” (1921).

Parsifal

Paul Verlaine · 1886 · French

“And, oh, those children’s voices singing in the dome!”: the last line of a sonnet on Wagner’s Parsifal.

Parsifal a vaincu les Filles, leur gentil
Babil et la luxure amusante — et sa pente
Vers la Chair de garçon vierge que cela tente
D’aimer les seins légers et ce gentil babil;

Il a vaincu la Femme belle, au cœur subtil,
Étalant ses bras frais et sa gorge excitante;
Il a vaincu l’Enfer et rentre sous la tente
Avec un lourd trophée à son bras puéril,

Avec la lance qui perça le Flanc suprême!
Il a guéri le roi, le voici roi lui-même,
Et prêtre du très saint Trésor essentiel.

En robe d’or il adore, gloire et symbole,
Le vase pur où resplendit le Sang réel.
— Et, ô ces voix d’enfants chantant dans la coupole!

Parsifal has overcome the Maidens, their pretty chatter and amusing lust, and his own leaning toward the Flesh, the leaning of a virgin boy whom all that tempts to love light breasts and that pretty chatter; he has overcome the beautiful Woman with the subtle heart, spreading her cool arms and her exciting bosom; he has overcome Hell and returns to the tent with a heavy trophy on his boyish arm, with the lance that pierced the supreme Side! He has healed the king, and here he is king himself, and priest of the most holy essential Treasure. In a robe of gold he adores, glory and symbol, the pure vessel where the real Blood shines. And, oh, those children’s voices singing in the dome!

Verlaine’s sonnet on Wagner’s last opera, written for the Revue wagnérienne, a Paris journal devoted to Wagner, and printed there in January 1886, gives the plot in thirteen lines: the flower-maidens, the temptress Kundry, the recovered spear, the healed king. The fourteenth line is the boys’ choir in the dome of the Grail temple. Eliot’s note cites the sonnet. In the poem the line comes straight after the ballad about Mrs Porter, which Eliot’s note says was “reported to me from Sydney, Australia.” In Wagner, as in the romances Jessie Weston discusses in From Ritual to Romance, the book Eliot names in the note at the head of his notes, the knight heals the wounded king; the poem’s Fisher King is still fishing at line 425, “with the arid plain behind me.”

References Verlaine, “Parsifal”; Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II.

Fragment 104a (Hesperus)

Sappho · c. 600 BC · Greek

Two lines to the evening star, which brings the sheep, the goat and the child home.

Ἔσπερε πάντα φέρων ὄσα φαίνολις ἐσκέδασ’ Αὔως,
φέρεις ὄιν, φέρεις αἶγα, φέρεις ἄπυ μάτερι παῖδα.

Evening star, bringing back everything that shining dawn scattered: you bring the sheep, you bring the goat, you bring the child back to its mother.

Two lines that survive in quotation: Demetrius, an ancient Greek critic, gives them in his treatise On Style (section 141) for the charm of their repeated “you bring,” all but the second half of the first line, which is preserved in the Etymologicum Magnum, a Byzantine dictionary. Now the chattering swans with hoarse voices make the pools ring; Tereus’s girl sings under the poplar’s shade, so that you would think the stirrings of love were spoken by her musical mouth, and not that a sister complained of a barbarous husband. Dans le Restaurant

T. S. Eliot · 1918 · French

The French original of “Death by Water.”

Phlébas, le Phénicien, pendant quinze jours noyé,
Oubliait les cris des mouettes et la houle de Cornouaille,
Et les profits et les pertes, et la cargaison d’étain:
Un courant de sous-mer l’emporta très loin,
Le repassant aux étapes de sa vie antérieure.
Figurez-vous donc, c’était un sort pénible;
Cependant, ce fut jadis un bel homme, de haute taille.

Phlebas the Phoenician, drowned a fortnight, forgot the cries of the gulls and the swell off Cornwall, and the profits and the losses, and the cargo of tin: an undersea current carried him very far away, taking him back through the stages of his former life. Just imagine, it was a hard fate; and yet he was once a handsome man, and tall.

Eliot published four poems in French; this is one of them, written in 1917, printed in the Little Review in September 1918 and then in his two collections of 1920, Ara Vos Prec and Poems. A shabby waiter tells a customer about sheltering from a shower with a small girl when he was seven; the customer, disgusted, gives him ten sous to go and wash. Then, without transition, come seven lines on the drowned Phoenician Phlebas. Eliot turned those seven lines into the ten of Part IV, dropping the tin and Cornwall and adding “Gentile or Jew” and the address to the man at the wheel. His “Cornouaille”, without an s, is strictly the district of Brittany; the cargo of tin points to Cornwall, and that is how the line is read. In the draft these lines closed a long narrative of a shipwreck; Pound cut the narrative and insisted that Phlebas stay.

From Ritual to Romance

Jessie L. Weston · 1920 · English

The book Eliot names in the note at the head of his notes as the source of the title and the plan: the Fisher King, the waste land, the Chapel Perilous.

Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance. Following the anthropologist J. G. Folklorists of James Frazer’s generation, Alice Gomme among them, connected it with the belief that a bridge was once made to stand by burying a victim in its foundations; Iona and Peter Opie, in The Oxford Dictionary of Nursery Rhymes, treat the connection with caution. Eliot’s crowd crossed the real London Bridge at line 62. Here the rhyme opens the run of quotations at lines 427–430, the “fragments” of line 431; the others are in Italian, Latin and French, with three English words, “O swallow swallow,” set in the Latin line.

That Shakespearian Rag

Gene Buck and Herman Ruby (words), Dave Stamper (music) · 1912 · English

“O O O O that Shakespeherian Rag— / It’s so elegant / So intelligent.”

That Shakespearian rag,
Most intelligent, very elegant,
That old classical drag,
Has the proper stuff, the line “Lay on Macduff” . . .

Published in 1912, and usually said to have been sung in that year’s Ziegfeld Follies, the Broadway revue, on the strength of a trade advertisement that B. R. McElderry cited when he identified the song as Eliot’s source in 1957. In 2017 the music critic Matthew Guerrieri searched the files of Variety, the show-business trade paper, and found no sign that it was sung in the show; the first performance he could find was in vaudeville, at the Columbia Theatre, New York, on 5 January 1913. The lyric has Shakespeare’s characters dancing the ragtime steps of the day. Eliot’s “Shakespeherian,” with its extra syllable, is generally read as deliberate, giving the word the rag’s syncopation; the poem also reverses the song’s “intelligent … elegant.” The cover spells the title “Shakesperian”, the chorus inside “Shakespearian”. No recording from the period is known.
