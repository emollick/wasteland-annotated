I. The Burial of the Dead

  1. Virginia Woolf, who heard Eliot read the poem at dinner in June 1922, wrote in her diary that “he sang it & chanted it rhythmed it.” Start with his own voice: the listening room has his recordings. Then read the seventy-six lines silently, with his stresses in mind.

    References Woolf, Diary, vol. 2, 23 June 1922.

  2. Switch on Voices and the section splits into ten: the poem’s own voice, Marie, a voice in German, a prophet, a sailor and a shepherd from Wagner, a girl with hyacinths, someone who could not speak, a clairvoyante, and a walker in the City. The typescript of this section was headed “He Do the Police in Different Voices”, a phrase from Dickens. For each voice, ask whether it is quoting someone else.

    References Facsimile (1971), the typescript of Part I; Dickens, Our Mutual Friend, bk. I, ch. 16.

  3. Madame Sosostris turns up six cards, fails to find a seventh, and reports one vision. Turn them over one at a time on the table and read the note on each. The seventh card, the one she cannot find, is the Hanged Man. Water comes into her reading twice: the drowned sailor, and “Fear death by water.”

  4. Lines 60–68 follow a route: over London Bridge, “up the hill and down King William Street”, and past St Mary Woolnoth as its clock strikes nine. It is about half a mile, the way into the City that thousands of clerks walked every morning from London Bridge station. Lloyds Bank, where Eliot worked, stood at the end of it, and his note on line 68 calls the dead sound on the final stroke “a phenomenon which I have often noticed”. Follow it on the map, then read the lines again with the walk in mind.

    References Eliot, “What Dante Means to Me” (1950); T. S. Eliot Foundation, “T. S. Eliot at Lloyds Bank”.

  5. Read the first twelve lines of the General Prologue to Chaucer’s Canterbury Tales (they are in the library), then Eliot’s first seven. Both begin with April, with rain, and with roots. Decide what has changed between Chaucer’s April and Eliot’s.

    References Chaucer, General Prologue, lines 1–12.

  6. Part I names water, or its absence, in several places: spring rain and snow in the first seven lines, a shower over the Starnbergersee, the dry stone with no sound of water, the handful of dust, the drowned sailor, and “Fear death by water.” The Water lens tints the lines that name water and the lines that name its absence.

  7. Eliot’s headnote credits Jessie L. Weston’s book on the Grail legend with “not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem”. The plan shows in Part I in the dead land and the dry stone with “no sound of water”, in the Tarot cards Weston connected with the Grail, in the warning “Fear death by water”, and in the corpse in Stetson’s garden, one of the “references to vegetation ceremonies” that the same note says a reader of The Golden Bough, by the anthropologist J. G. Frazer, will recognise. The pathway called The Grail and the waste land follows the plan from the title page on.

  8. The section did not begin with April. In the typescript it began with fifty-four lines about a night out in Boston, which were cut. The Drafts lens marks where the cut fell and describes the cancelled lines, which are still in copyright and cannot be printed here. Read the description and then line 1, and decide what the change did.

    References Facsimile (1971), the typescript of Part I; Edmund Wilson (1971); Litz (1973).

  9. Five lines of Wagner (31–34 and 42), a sentence of German at line 12 and a line of French at line 76 are left untranslated. Switch on Languages to see what they say, then switch it off and read the section as a reader with no German or French would have read it in 1922, hearing those lines without understanding them.

  10. Part I asks questions it does not answer: what roots clutch and what branches grow out of this stony rubbish (19–20); where are you lingering (34, in Wagner’s German); has the corpse in the garden begun to sprout (72). One way in is to read the section with those questions open.

  1. Fiona Shaw performed the whole poem alone on a bare stage, directed by Deborah Warner, one actor speaking for the woman in the chair and the woman in the pub. The listening room has a minute of Shaw performing the poem, from a BBC broadcast of 2015; the film of the staging circulates only in unofficial copies, which are not played there. The whole performance, filmed again for the Faber app in 2011, is in the app. For the pub scene, hear Alec Guinness’s reading of 1975, issued by the record label Argo and linked from the listening room.

  2. Read Enobarbus’s speech from Antony and Cleopatra (it is in the library), then lines 77–110 with it beside you, and see what each of Eliot’s changes does to Shakespeare’s picture: the barge comes indoors and down in scale. Then find in the room’s details Dido, the queen of Carthage in Virgil’s Aeneid, at line 92, and at line 98 Milton’s Eden as Satan first sees it.

    References Shakespeare, Antony and Cleopatra II.ii; Virgil, Aeneid I 726; Milton, Paradise Lost IV 140.

  3. With Voices on, lines 111–138 divide: everything in quotation marks is hers, spoken; everything without is his and, by the usual reading, unspoken. Read only his lines, in order.

    References Unger (1966).

  4. “That Shakespearian Rag” was a real song of 1912. No recording of the day has been found; the sheet music is in the listening room. Read its chorus, then lines 128–130 with Eliot’s extra syllable in “Shakespeherian”: it gives the word the song’s syncopation. The line comes among the man’s answers, which the poem leaves unspoken.

    References Buck, Ruby and Stamper, “That Shakespearian Rag” (1912); Guerrieri (2017).

  5. Eliot’s note sends you to Ovid’s story of Tereus, Procne and Philomela; John Lyly’s nightingale song from his play Campaspe (1584) is the plainest example of the Elizabethan convention, the nightingale’s “jug jug”, that line 103 uses.     References Ovid, Metamorphoses VI 424–674; Lyly, Campaspe V.i.

  6. The poem gives three hours by the clock; two of them are here, ten and four (lines 135–136), and the third is nine, at line 68. The section also has “tonight”, “tomorrow” and closing time, called five times. The Clock lens marks every time of day the poem names.

  7. Eliot’s wife read this section in typescript. Beside the nervous dialogue she wrote “WONDERFUL”; she supplied two lines of the pub scene; and she asked for a line about “ivory men” to be taken out. The Drafts lens says which lines they are.

    References Facsimile (1971), the typescript of Part II; Ricks and McCue, textual notes on lines 120–139, 153 and 164; Litz (1973).

  8. Eliot’s note on line 218 says “all the women are one woman”. The pathway called The loves that fail sets the poem’s couples side by side, in order, from the hyacinth garden to the heart that “would have responded” at line 421, so that you can judge it; four of its stops are in this section.

  9. In the room one person talks and the other thinks. Two of his unspoken answers point to the dead. “Rats’ alley” was a name soldiers gave to trenches on the Western Front, as Peter Chasseaud’s book of trench names records; and the eyes that were pearls are from Ariel’s song in The Tempest, sung for a father thought drowned.

    References Shakespeare, The Tempest I.ii; Chasseaud (2006).

III. The Fire Sermon

  1. The section names, in order, a canal behind a gashouse, the Cannon Street Hotel, the Strand, Queen Victoria Street and Lower Thames Street with St Magnus Martyr, Greenwich Reach and the Isle of Dogs, and in the speeches of the three Thames-daughters Highbury, Richmond and Kew, Moorgate and Margate. The map has the course of the Thames through London with these places on it; Margate, sixty miles east, is marked at its edge.

  2. Spenser’s Prothalamion (1596) is a poem for a double wedding on the Thames, and its refrain, “Sweete Themmes runne softly, till I end my Song”, closes each stanza. Read its first stanza, then lines 173–184. Eliot takes the refrain at 176 and 183 and varies it at 184; Spenser’s nymphs gather flowers by the river, Eliot’s “are departed”, and his river “bears no” bottles, papers or cigarette ends.

    References Spenser, Prothalamion, line 18.

  3. The Voices lens marks each speaker and each quoted voice: the “I” who fishes by the canal, the Mrs. Porter song, Verlaine’s line, the nightingale, Mr. Eugenides (reported, not quoted), Tiresias, the typist, the “I” of Lower Thames Street, the three Thames-daughters, Augustine and the Buddha. Tiresias speaks only in this section; Eliot’s note calls him “the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest”.

  4. Eliot’s note sends the reader to the Buddha’s Fire Sermon in Henry Clarke Warren’s Buddhism in Translations (1896) and calls it as important as the Sermon on the Mount. It is short. In it the Buddha says of the eye, the ear, the nose, the tongue, the body and the mind that each “is on fire”, with the fire of passion, of hatred and of infatuation. The section’s title is the sermon’s, and “burning”, five times at 308–311, is the poem’s word for the sermon’s “on fire”.

    References Warren, Buddhism in Translations, § 73, pp. 351–353.

  5. In the typescript the typist scene was longer, in regular quatrains, and Pound cut it heavily, writing in the margin that the verse was “not interesting enough as verse” for so much of it. The drafts page describes his marks, and the seventy or so lines of couplets in Pope’s manner, about a fashionable woman called Fresca, that opened the section until he struck them out.

    References Facsimile (1971), the typescript of Part III; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 222–230.

  6. Act III of Wagner’s Götterdämmerung opens with the three Rhine-daughters swimming and singing of their stolen gold; “Weialala leia, wallala leialala” is their refrain, and the Thames-daughters take it at 277–278 and 290–291. Listen to the scene (a recording is in the listening room), then read lines 266–306. Eliot’s note says his three daughters “speak in turn” from 292 to 306.

    References Wagner, Götterdämmerung III.i.

  7. “On Margate Sands. / I can connect / Nothing with nothing.” Eliot spent four weeks at Margate, from 15 October to 12 November 1921, on three months’ sick leave from Lloyds Bank. In a letter from there to his friend Sydney Schiff he said he had drafted “some fifty lines” of this part, sitting in a shelter on the front. The Nayland Rock shelter on the seafront, listed in 2009 as the likely place, still stands. The map marks Margate.

    References Letters, vol. 1, Eliot to Sydney Schiff, 4 November 1921; Letters, vol. 1, chronology, October–November 1921; Historic England, list entry 1393490.

  8. The Water lens marks fire in a third colour. In this section it falls on the last four lines only, where “burning”, from the Buddha’s sermon of the title, follows Augustine’s “To Carthage then I came”. Eliot’s note says the placing of these two together “is not an accident”.

    References Warren, Buddhism in Translations, § 73, pp. 351–353; Augustine, Confessions III.1.

  9. Four old poems share lines with a modern scene in this section. Spenser’s nymphs are set beside the Thames they have left. Marvell’s winged chariot is set beside the rat. Actaeon coming to Diana, in John Day’s Parliament of Bees, is set beside Sweeney coming to Mrs. Porter. Goldsmith’s song of a woman who “stoops to folly” is set beside the gramophone. Take them in order and see what each pairing does. The pathway called The past beside the present follows such pairings from Chaucer’s April to “These fragments I have shored against my ruins”, and ends with what Eliot wrote in 1923 about Joyce’s Ulysses: that a “continuous parallel between contemporaneity and antiquity” was a method others must follow.

  10. Eliot’s note at 218 quotes nineteen lines of Ovid (Metamorphoses III, 320–338). Tiresias, who had lived seven years as a woman, is asked to settle Jupiter and Juno’s dispute over which sex has the greater pleasure in love. Juno blinds him for his answer and Jupiter gives him foresight. The library gives lines 324–331, on the serpents he struck and the change of sex that followed, in Latin and English. Read them, then the typist scene, which Tiresias says he has “foresuffered”.

    References Ovid, Metamorphoses III 320–338.

  11. The typist “smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone” (255–256). Eliot’s note gives Goldsmith’s song, in which the woman who “stoops to folly” has nothing left but to die; Eliot’s typist, instead, puts a record on. Critics have read the scene as contempt for her, and as the nearest the poem comes to pity; those who read it as pity point to Tiresias’s “foresuffered all” (243).

    References Goldsmith, The Vicar of Wakefield, ch. 24.

  1. The ten lines are Eliot’s translation of the last stanza of his own French poem “Dans le Restaurant”, published in 1918. Read the French (it is in the library), then the English, and note what was dropped (the tin, Cornwall, the offhand “Figurez-vous donc”) and what was added (“Gentile or Jew”, and the whole address to the man at the wheel).

    References The Little Review, September 1918, pp. 12–13; Poems (1920), “Dans le Restaurant”.

  2. In the typescript this section was a sea voyage of some ninety lines, from the Dry Salvages, rocks off Cape Ann in Massachusetts, to the fishing banks, ending in ice. Pound cut it to the last ten lines and then had to talk Eliot out of cutting those too: “Phlebas is an integral part of the poem . . . Must stay in.”

    References Facsimile (1971), the typescript of Part IV, and p. 129; Pound, Letters, ed. Paige, no. 183; Perloff (1981).

  3. The critic Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the drowned Phoenician Sailor as a recollection of the drowned god of the fertility cults, and held that in the poem “the way into life may be by death itself”. The pathway called Metamorphosis follows the poem’s changed bodies, Philomel turned nightingale, Tiresias seven years a woman, and the drowned man, and has a stop here.

  4. “Full fathom five thy father lies.” Read Ariel’s song (it is in the library), then Phlebas. Shakespeare’s drowned man is not in fact drowned, and the song has him suffer “a sea-change / Into something rich and strange”. Eliot’s Phlebas is a fortnight dead, and the current picks his bones in whispers.

Notes on The Waste Land

Eliot’s notes as printed in the 1922 book, with his own line numbers. The margin on this page counts every line of the poem; the 1922 printing’s numbers run one behind from line 346, so that Eliot’s “line 433” is this margin’s 434, and his numbers do not always agree with the margin. On the numbering.

Not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem were suggested by Miss Jessie L. Weston’s book on the Grail legend: From Ritual to Romance (Macmillan). Indeed, so deeply am I indebted, Miss Weston’s book will elucidate the difficulties of the poem much better than my notes can do; and I recommend it (apart from the great interest of the book itself) to any who think such elucidation of the poem worth the trouble. To another work of anthropology I am indebted in general, one which has influenced our generation profoundly; I mean The Golden Bough; I have used especially the two volumes Atthis Adonis Osiris. Anyone who is acquainted with these works will immediately recognise in the poem certain references to vegetation ceremonies.
