II. The Burial of the Dead

  1. Virginia Woolf, who heard Eliot read the poem at dinner in June 1922, wrote in her diary that "he sang it & chanted it rhythmed it." Start with his own voice: the listening room has his recordings, and the first section is where the chant is plainest. Then read the seventy-six lines silently and notice where his stresses fell.

  2. Switch on Voices and the section splits into at least nine: the poem's own voice, Marie, a stranger in a café, a prophet, a sailor in Wagner, a girl with hyacinths, someone who could not speak, a clairvoyante, a commuter. The typescript's title was "He Do the Police in Different Voices." Ask, of each, whether it is quoting someone else.

  3. Madame Sosostris deals seven cards and one vision. Turn them over one at a time on the table and read the note on each. Then ask what she leaves out: no Hanged Man, no water except the kind that drowns.

  4. London Bridge, King William Street, St Mary Woolnoth, the stroke of nine. It is a route of about six hundred yards, the one Eliot walked to Lloyds Bank. Follow it on the map, then read lines 60–68 as a man who has just walked it in fog.

  5. Read the first twelve lines of the General Prologue, then Eliot's first seven. Same month, same rain, same roots. Decide what has changed between 1387 and 1922, and whether it is the weather or the people.

  6. Spring rain, forgetful snow, a shower of rain, dry stone, no sound of water, a handful of dust, a drowned sailor, "Fear death by water." The section's weather is its argument. The Water lens tints every line by what it is made of.

  7. Dante's crowd at the gate of Hell, Limbo's sighs, Webster's dirge, the corpse in the garden, Frazer's buried god. A short walk through the section's underworld, with the source passages open.

  8. The section did not begin with April. It began with fifty-four lines of a night out in Boston. The drafts view shows where the cut fell, and what the poem gained by starting exhausted.

  9. Five lines of Wagner, one café sentence and a line of Baudelaire are left untranslated. Switch on Tongues to see the translations, then switch it off and read the section as someone in 1922 without German would have read it: as sound, and as a wall.

  10. When were you "neither living nor dead"? What corpse have you planted in a garden, and have you checked on it? Whose shadow at evening rises to meet you? The section asks them; it does not answer. Neither need you, yet.

IIII. A Game of Chess

  1. Fiona Shaw's performance is the one to watch for this section: the woman in the chair and the woman in the pub are the same voice at two social altitudes. Alec Guinness does the pub with a Cockney the Eliots' maid would have recognised.

  2. Read Enobarbus's speech, then lines 77–110 with it beside you. Every gilded thing in the room is a thing from the river; every change Eliot makes is a demotion. Then find the other two queens hidden in the furniture, Dido and Eve.

  3. With Voices on, everything in quotation marks is hers and everything without is his, unspoken. His answers are all quotations. Read only his lines, in order, and you have a man made entirely of other people's sentences.

  4. "That Shakespearian Rag" was a real song of 1912; no recording of the day has turned up, but the sheet music is in the listening room. Play it, then read lines 128–130 with Eliot's extra syllable in "Shakespeherian." The syncopation is the joke, and the joke is what he hums instead of speaking.

  5. Ovid's story of Tereus, Procne and Philomela, and Lyly's "jug jug" song, are the two texts under lines 99–103. Read them, then trace the nightingale through the rest of the poem: lines 203–206, and the swallow at 429.

  6. Hot water at ten, a closed car at four, to-night, tomorrow, HURRY UP PLEASE ITS TIME, good night. The section is a timetable. The clock shows every hour the poem names; most of them are here.

  7. Eliot's wife read this section in typescript, wrote "WONDERFUL" beside the pub scene, supplied one of its best lines, and asked for one line about "ivory men" to be removed. The drafts view says which.

  8. A lady on a throne, a raped girl turned bird, a woman with bad nerves, Lil with her teeth out, her friend, Ophelia. Eliot's note says "all the women are one woman." Walk through them and decide whether you believe him.

  9. Two people in a room; one talks, one thinks. Who is not there? Whose bones were lost in rats' alley? Whose eyes were the pearls? The section's subject is an absence, and the quotations are its shape.

IIIIII. The Fire Sermon

  1. The section names, in order, a canal behind a gashouse, the Cannon Street Hotel, the Strand, Queen Victoria Street and Lower Thames Street with St Magnus Martyr, Greenwich Reach and the Isle of Dogs, and in the daughters' speeches Highbury, Richmond and Kew, Moorgate and Margate. The map has the real course of the Thames, with each of these on it.

  2. Spenser's Prothalamion (1596) is a poem for a double wedding on the Thames, and its refrain, "Sweete Themmes runne softly, till I end my Song", closes each stanza. Read its first stanza, then lines 173–184. Eliot takes the refrain at 176 and 183 and varies it at 184; Spenser's nymphs gather flowers by the river, Eliot's "are departed", and his river "bears no" bottles, papers or cigarette ends.

  3. The Voices lens marks each speaker and each quoted voice: the "I" who fishes by the canal, the Mrs. Porter song, Verlaine's line, the nightingale, Mr. Eugenides (reported, not quoted), Tiresias, the typist, the "I" of Lower Thames Street, the three Thames-daughters, Augustine and the Buddha. Tiresias speaks only in this section; Eliot's note calls him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest".

  4. Eliot's note sends the reader to the Buddha's Fire Sermon in Henry Clarke Warren's Buddhism in Translations (1896) and calls it as important as the Sermon on the Mount. It is short. In it the Buddha says of the eye, the ear, the tongue, the body and the mind that each "is on fire", with the fire of passion, of hatred and of infatuation. The section's title is the sermon's, and "burning", five times at 308–311, is the poem's word for the sermon's "on fire".

  5. In the typescript the typist scene was longer, in regular quatrains, and Pound cut it heavily, writing in the margin that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it. The drafts page describes his marks, and the seventy or so lines of couplets in Pope's manner, about a fashionable woman called Fresca, that opened the section until he struck them out.

  6. Act III of Wagner's Götterdämmerung opens with the three Rhine-daughters swimming and singing of their stolen gold; "Weialala leia, wallala leialala" is their refrain, and the Thames-daughters take it at 277–278 and 290–291. Listen to the scene, then read lines 266–306. Eliot's note says his three daughters "speak in turn" from 292 to 306.

  7. "On Margate Sands. / I can connect / Nothing with nothing." Eliot spent three weeks at Margate, from late October to mid-November 1921, on three months' sick leave from Lloyds Bank. In a letter from there, to Sydney Schiff, he said he had drafted "some fifty lines" of this part, sitting in a shelter on the front. The Nayland Rock shelter on the seafront, listed in 2009 as the likely place, still stands. The map has it.

  8. The Water lens marks fire in a third colour. In this section it falls on the last four lines only, where "burning", from the Buddha's sermon of the title, follows Augustine's "To Carthage then I came". Eliot's note says the placing of these two together "is not an accident".

  9. The Birds pathway stops at each bird in the poem in order: the nightingale (here at 203–206, and in Part II), the gulls of Part IV, the hermit-thrush and the cock of Part V, and the swallow at 429, with recordings where they exist.

  10. Eliot's note at 218 quotes nineteen lines of Ovid (Metamorphoses III, 320–338). Tiresias, who had lived seven years as a woman, is asked to settle Jupiter and Juno's dispute over which sex has the greater pleasure in love. Juno blinds him for his answer and Jupiter gives him foresight. The library gives lines 324–331, on the serpents he struck and the change of sex that followed, in Latin and English. Read them, then the typist scene, which Tiresias says he has "foresuffered".

  11. The typist "smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone" (255–256). Eliot's note gives Goldsmith's song, in which the woman who "stoops to folly" has nothing left but to die; Eliot's typist, instead, puts a record on. Critics have read the scene as contempt for her, and as the nearest the poem comes to pity; those who read it as pity point to Tiresias's "foresuffered all" (243).

IVIV. Death by Water

  1. The ten lines are Eliot's translation of the last stanza of his own French poem of 1918. Read the French, then the English, and note what was dropped (the tin, Cornwall) and what was added ("Gentile or Jew").

  2. In the typescript the section was a ninety-three-line voyage out of Gloucester, Massachusetts, ending on an iceberg. Pound cut it to ten and then had to talk Eliot out of cutting those too: "Phlebas is an integral part of the poem . . . Must stay in."

  3. "Fear death by water" (55), the pearls that were his eyes (48, 125), Ophelia (172), the king's wreck (191), and then Phlebas. The Water pathway collects them.

  4. "Full fathom five thy father lies." Read the song, then Phlebas. Shakespeare's drowned man suffers "a sea-change / Into something rich and strange." Eliot's has his bones picked in whispers. Decide which is the kinder.

  5. The section is short enough to compare readers: Eliot's flat delivery, Guinness's elegy, Irons's, Shaw's. Listen to all the versions you can find, back to back. It takes a few minutes and it tells you what tone the lines will bear.

  6. "Consider Phlebas, who was once handsome and tall as you." The last line addresses whoever turns a wheel and looks to windward: sailors, drivers, anyone steering anything. Consider him. That is the whole instruction.

Phlebas the Phoenician, a fortnight dead,

VV. What the Thunder Said

  1. Eliot recorded the whole poem in 1933 and again in 1946; both are in the listening room, with Part V as he read it in Washington in 1947. Listen from line 400, where the thunder speaks, to the end.

  2. The Water lens tints every line that is about water or the want of it. From line 331 to line 394 there is no water. At line 395 a gust brings rain, and at 397 the leaves are still waiting for it; at 419 a boat answers "the hand expert with sail and oar" on a calm sea; at 425 the arid plain is behind the fisherman.

  3. "Drip drop drip drop drop drop drop." Eliot's note names the bird and quotes Chapman's field guide on its song; "water-dripping" is not Chapman's phrase. A recording is in the listening room. Play it, then read line 358.

  4. Eliot gives two sources for "Who is the third who walks always beside you?": in his headnote, the journey to Emmaus of Luke 24; in his note on the line, Shackleton's account in South of crossing South Georgia in May 1916, when each of three men felt a fourth walking with them. Both are in the library.

  5. The last eight lines are in English, Italian, Latin, French, English, English, Sanskrit, Sanskrit; Eliot's note on line 428 adds Provençal. The Tongues lens gives the translations.

  6. "These fragments I have shored against my ruins." The Fragments view takes the last lines one at a time and gives each its source.

  7. The library has the passage of the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad that Eliot's note cites: the thunder says "Da," and gods, men and demons each hear a different word in it. Eliot changes the order of the three words and follows each with a passage of his own.

  8. The section names Jerusalem, Athens, Alexandria, Vienna and London in two lines, then the Ganges and the Himalaya, then London Bridge and Aquitaine. The world map shows the cities, the river and the mountain, and two places from Eliot's life: Quebec, where his note says he heard the hermit-thrush, and Cape Ann, where he sailed as a boy.

  9. "I knew nothing," "Nothing again nothing," "Do you know nothing?," "Nothing with nothing," "expect / Nothing." The pathway follows the word through the poem. It is not said in Part V; the last stop, at line 402, is the thunder's question, "what have we given?"

  10. Pound's note at the head of the typescript of Part V reads "OK from here on I think," and he cut almost nothing. Eliot wrote the section at Lausanne between late November 1921 and the New Year, and told Bertrand Russell in October 1923 that it was "the only part which justifies the whole, at all."

  11. The Voices lens gives lines 424 to 426 to the Fisher King, lines 427 to 432 to the fragments, and the last two lines to the Upanishad. The fragments are, in order, a nursery rhyme, Dante, a Latin poet and Tennyson, Nerval, the poet himself, Kyd's Hieronimo.

  12. "Shantih shantih shantih." Eliot's note calls it the formal ending of an Upanishad; in 1922 he called "The Peace which passeth understanding" a feeble translation of the word, and from 1925 "our equivalent." Is the line a blessing on the poem, or a man saying the word to himself?

Notes on The Waste Land

Eliot’s notes as printed in the 1922 book, keyed to his own line numbers. The margin numbers on this page count the lines as they are; the 1922 printing’s numbers run one behind from line 346 onward, so that his “line 433” is the poem’s 434th, and his notes do not always agree with the margin. Why.

Not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem were suggested by Miss Jessie L. Weston’s book on the Grail legend: From Ritual to Romance (Macmillan). Indeed, so deeply am I indebted, Miss Weston’s book will elucidate the difficulties of the poem much better than my notes can do; and I recommend it (apart from the great interest of the book itself) to any who think such elucidation of the poem worth the trouble. To another work of anthropology I am indebted in general, one which has influenced our generation profoundly; I mean The Golden Bough; I have used especially the two volumes Atthis Adonis Osiris. Anyone who is acquainted with these works will immediately recognise in the poem certain references to vegetation ceremonies.
