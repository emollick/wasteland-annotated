The listening room

Recordings of the poem, beginning with Eliot’s own, the music it quotes, the song of the bird in Eliot’s note to line 357, and the early printings page by page. Nothing is copied onto this site: each recording plays from the library, archive or broadcaster that holds it, and can be opened there.

Eliot’s own voice

Two readings by the poet, more than a decade apart, and a fragment of a third.

T. S. Eliot1933

Eliot reads the whole poem, 1933

T. S. Eliot

Columbia University, 1933

The earliest recording, cut on aluminium discs at Columbia University by W. Cabell Greet and George W. Hibbitt of Barnard College, in the year Eliot spent in America as Norton Professor at Harvard. Eliot was forty-four; the voice is younger and lighter than in the later recording. The Poetry Archive plays it in five parts on its own site, one page for each: I · II · III · IV · V. The Archive dates it 1933 and says the date was once thought to be 1935, crediting Lacy Rumsey of the ENS de Lyon for the correction; PennSound, which holds the same Columbia series, labels it 1935.

References The Poetry Archive; PennSound; Eliot, The Use of Poetry and the Use of Criticism (1933).

T. S. Eliot1935

The 1933 reading again, at PennSound

T. S. Eliot

Labelled 1935 by PennSound

The same Columbia series in two complete versions of different lengths, about twenty-five and twenty-eight minutes, each divided by section, on PennSound’s own page. PennSound holds it with the permission of the Eliot estate, for study and teaching. It gives the date as 1935, though its own headnote says the Columbia recordings were made in 1933, 1935 and later; the Poetry Archive, which credits the redating to 1933 to Lacy Rumsey, refers readers to Richard Swigg’s Quick, Said the Bird and Jason Camlot’s Phonopoetics for more on the recording.

References PennSound; The Poetry Archive; Swigg (2012); Camlot (2019).

Open at PennSound, University of Pennsylvania

T. S. Eliotlater

Eliot reads the whole poem, the later recording

T. S. Eliot

The Caedmon recording, usually dated 1946; this copy put out online in January 1994

The later recording, the one issued on record. Harper & Row, which bought the Caedmon label in 1987 and became HarperCollins, reissued it under its Harper Audio name. This copy was put out by Harper Audio on 18 January 1994 through the Internet Multicasting Service, which had started an early internet radio station the year before, on its Internet Town Hall channel, as sound files at 8 kHz; HarperCollins consented to that distribution only. The Internet Archive keeps it as a thirty-one-minute MP3. The sound is poor, about the quality of a telephone line. The Poetry Archive dates the recording 1946; the item itself gives no year.

References Harper Audio (1994); Mitgang, New York Times, 27 August 1987; The Poetry Archive.

T. S. Eliot1947

Part V, read at the National Gallery, 1947

T. S. Eliot

23 May 1947, Washington

A reading Eliot gave at the National Gallery of Art in Washington on 23 May 1947, with his own comments. Of The Waste Land he read only Part V; the rest of the recording is Prufrock, La Figlia che Piange, Sweeney Among the Nightingales, the Ariel poems, Landscapes, the last part of Ash-Wednesday and East Coker. The Library of Congress keeps it in its Archive of Recorded Poetry and Literature, and it streams on the Library’s page.

References Library of Congress, recording of 23 May 1947.

Jeremy Irons2017

Jeremy Irons reads the poem, BBC Radio 4, 2017

Jeremy Irons, presented by Martha Kearney with Jeanette Winterson, Rowan Williams and Jackie Kay

Broadcast 1 January 2017

Irons reads the poem on his own in this Radio 4 programme, broadcast on 1 January 2017 and kept by the Internet Archive in a recording made off the air. It was the third of five programmes in which Martha Kearney and her guests introduced his readings of Eliot; the guests here are Jeanette Winterson, Rowan Williams and Jackie Kay. Five years earlier, on 30 March 2012, Irons and Eileen Atkins had shared the poem’s voices between them in another Radio 4 broadcast, introduced by Rowan Williams, Jackie Kay, Matthew Hollis and Sean O’Brien. The BBC’s page for that reading, linked here, has the details and no audio; the copies of it on YouTube are listeners’ uploads and are not played here.

Fiona Shaw2015

Fiona Shaw performs it: a minute from Newsnight

Fiona Shaw, directed by Deborah Warner

Newsnight, January 2015; the staging dates from 1995, the film from 1996

Deborah Warner directed Fiona Shaw in the poem as a piece of theatre, with Shaw alone on a bare stage taking every voice. It opened in Brussels in May 1995 and toured, and Warner’s film of it, made for the BBC, was shown at Cannes in May 1996. In 1997 the staging ran at Wilton’s Music Hall in the East End, which is why that hall is on the site’s map. The clip here is the BBC’s own, of Shaw performing part of the poem on Newsnight in January 2015, fifty years after Eliot’s death. The film itself circulates only in unofficial copies, which are not played here.

References Playbill, 28 October 1996; Festival de Cannes, The Waste Land (1996); Playbill, 8 December 1997; Wilton’s Music Hall, The Waste Land (1997); Newsnight, 19 January 2015.

The music in it

The two operas and the rag that the poem quotes.

Gustav Rödin1928

“Frisch weht der Wind”: the sailor’s song, Bayreuth 1928

Gustav Rödin, young sailor; Bayreuth Festival under Karl Elmendorff

Recorded at the festival in July 1928, on Columbia 78s

Lines 31 to 34 quote the young sailor’s song at the start of Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde, sung unaccompanied from aloft, as if from the mast; Isolde, below, takes it as mockery of her. In the Archive’s track list the song is “Westwärts schweift der Blick”, track 2, and its return “Frisch weht der Wind”, track 5. The surface noise of the 78s is left in.

References Wagner, Tristan und Isolde I.i; Tristan (Bayreuth, 1928), first act.

Hans Beer1928

“Öd’ und leer das Meer”: the shepherd in Tristan, Act III

Hans Beer, shepherd; Rudolf Bockelmann, Kurwenal

The same 1928 Bayreuth set

Line 42 quotes the shepherd’s answer to Kurwenal, Tristan’s servant, at the start of the last act of Tristan und Isolde: he has looked out to sea for Isolde’s ship and there is nothing. The prelude, track 52, and the shepherd’s cor anglais tune, track 53, come before it; the exchange is track 54.

The printings and the manuscript

The printings of 1922 and 1930, page by page; where the manuscript is; and the Frazer volume Eliot’s headnote names.

Boni and Liveright1922

The first edition, 1922: Duke’s copy

Boni and Liveright, New York

December 1922, one of a thousand numbered copies

The book this site follows, scanned page by page by Duke University Libraries. The marginal line numbers are printed every ten lines. On page 42 the line “If there were water” is set as if it were the end of the line before it, carried over for want of room, and the numbering skips it. From there the 1922 numbers run one behind the count used here.

References The Waste Land (1922), p. 42.

Horace Liveright1930

The fourth printing, 1930: Brigham Young University’s copy

Horace Liveright, New York

April 1930, from the 1922 setting of type

The library’s record calls this copy no. 596 of the thousand and cites Gallup A6a, the 1922 edition’s entry in Donald Gallup’s bibliography of Eliot, while also noting a fourth printing. It is not a second scan of the first printing. The title page reads Horace Liveright, the verso “Fourth Printing, April, 1930”, and it has no limitation statement, the note that numbered each copy of the first printing. The pages are the 1922 setting of type, page for page and with the same marginal numbers; “hot water” at line 135 and “Heimat” at line 32, where letters are missing in the Duke copy, print cleanly here. Copies of the first printing exist in two states: in the earlier, line 339 reads “Dead mountain mouth of carious teeth”; in the later the a of “mountain” had dropped out and it reads “mount in”. Duke’s copy is the later state, and this printing still reads “mount in”.

References The Waste Land, fourth printing (1930); The Waste Land (1922); Gallup, A6a.

Open at Internet Archive

The Dial Publishing Company1922

The Dial, November 1922

The Dial Publishing Company, New York

November 1922, pages 473 to 485

The poem as it first reached American readers, without the notes, in the magazine that gave Eliot its two-thousand-dollar award that year. Here the first line’s “cruellest” is spelled “cruelest”, with one l. The poem’s earliest printing of all was in The Criterion, the London magazine Eliot edited, in October 1922; that issue is on HathiTrust, where it can be read in full from the United States.

References The Dial, November 1922, pp. 473–485; The Dial, December 1922, Comment; Rainey, Revisiting The Waste Land, ch. 2; The Criterion, October 1922; HathiTrust, The Criterion, vol. 1.

T. S. Eliot1921

The manuscript, with Pound’s annotations

T. S. Eliot, annotated by Ezra Pound and Vivien Eliot

1921 to 1922

The typescript and manuscript pages, with Pound’s cuts and comments and notes by Eliot’s wife Vivien, which Eliot sent in October 1922 to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector. After Quinn’s death they passed to his sister and then to his niece, who sold them to the New York Public Library’s Berg Collection in 1958; the library announced the purchase in 1968, after Eliot’s death. The library has digitised the pages but shows the images only in the building, so the link here goes to its record, not to the pages. The way to read them is the facsimile edition that Eliot’s widow, Valerie Eliot, published in 1971, reissued by Faber in 2022 with the pages in colour.

References NYPL, The Waste Land typescript; Facsimile (1971), introduction; Ellmann (1971); Keane (2011); Facsimile, Faber colour edition (2022).

Open at New York Public Library, Berg Collection

Sir James George Frazer1906

Frazer’s Adonis, Attis, Osiris

Sir James George Frazer

First edition, 1906

The headnote to Eliot’s notes names “the two volumes Atthis Adonis Osiris”, that is, Adonis, Attis, Osiris, Part IV of the third edition of The Golden Bough (1914). This is the book’s first edition of 1906, in one volume. It gives Frazer’s account of the dying and reviving gods, the gardens of Adonis and the images of the god thrown into the sea, first set out in The Golden Bough of 1890 and enlarged here. The other book the headnote names, Jessie Weston’s From Ritual to Romance, is on Project Gutenberg, and so is the one-volume Golden Bough of 1922.

References Frazer, Adonis, Attis, Osiris, preface, p. v; Frazer, The Golden Bough, part IV (1914); Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922); Weston, From Ritual to Romance.
