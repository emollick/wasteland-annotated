About this edition

The text

The poem is given as it stands in the first book edition, published by Boni & Liveright in New York in December 1922, including Eliot’s notes, which were written for that edition; the poem had appeared in The Criterion (October 1922) and The Dial (November 1922) without them. That text is in the public domain in the United States, where this site is published. In the United Kingdom and the European Union the poem remains in copyright until the end of 2035, seventy years after Eliot’s death.

The 1922 text differs in small ways from the one in later Faber editions. This edition keeps the 1922 readings and notes the later ones where they matter: “Quando fiam ceu chelidon” at line 429 (later “uti,” the word in the Latin poem); “aetherial” at line 415 (later “aethereal”); the note on “Shantih,” which in 1922 calls the English phrase “a feeble translation of the content of this word” and later “our equivalent.” The dedication to Pound was not printed until 1925 and is included here because Eliot wrote it into Pound’s copy in January 1923. Three slips of the 1922 press are set right: “Oed’ und leer das Meer” at line 42 was printed with a plain O; “The hot water at ten” at line 135 lost its a; and “Dead mountain mouth of carious teeth” at line 339 reads “mount in” in most copies of the first printing, the type having shifted as it ran. Typographic quotation marks and a few spellings have been regularised from the transcription on Project Gutenberg, which was checked against a scan of the 1922 printing.

The 434th line called 433

The 1922 printing numbers every tenth line in the margin. On page 42, in Part V, “If there were water” is set flush right as the run-over of “From doors of mudcracked houses” and is not counted, so from line 346 the printed numbers run one behind: 350 stands beside “A spring,” 360 beside “When I count, there are only you and I together” (the poem’s 361st line), and the last line, “Shantih shantih shantih,” is famously “line 433” although the poem has 434. Eliot’s notes mostly follow the printed count, but not always: the note on the hermit-thrush is numbered 357 and the note on the Antarctic expedition 360, which fit the true count, while 366–76, 401, 411, 424 and 433 fit the printed one. On this site the margin counts the lines as they are, and each note is attached to the line Eliot meant.

What was written for this edition

The annotations, the translations of the sources, the descriptions of the voices, the tagging of water and drought, the pathways and the section “ways in” were written for this site. Every claim about the poem’s composition can be traced to one of: the facsimile edition of the drafts (Valerie Eliot, ed., 1971); The Letters of T. S. Eliot, volume 1; The Poems of T. S. Eliot, edited by Christopher Ricks and Jim McCue (2015), whose commentary is the standard reference; Lawrence Rainey’s The Annotated Waste Land (2005); Robert Crawford’s Young Eliot (2015); and Matthew Hollis’s The Waste Land: A Biography of a Poem (2022). The recordings and source texts are linked, not copied, from the libraries and archives that hold them.

Voices from 1922

  • “He sang it & chanted it rhythmed it. It has great beauty & force of phrase: symmetry; & tensity. What connects it together, I’m not so sure.” Virginia Woolf, diary, June 1922, after Eliot read the poem at dinner.
  • “Complimenti, you bitch. I am wracked by the seven jealousies.” Ezra Pound to Eliot, January 1922 (or the December before; the letter is dated in a calendar of his own invention).
  • “Various critics have done me the honour to interpret the poem in terms of criticism of the contemporary world, have considered it, indeed, as an important bit of social criticism. To me it was only the relief of a personal and wholly insignificant grouse against life; it is just a piece of rhythmical grumbling.” Eliot, in a lecture at Harvard, as reported by Theodore Spencer and printed at the head of the facsimile edition.
  • “It gives me very great pleasure that you like The Waste Land, and especially Part V which in my opinion is not only the best part, but the only part that justifies the whole, at all.” Eliot to Bertrand Russell, October 1923.

A timeline

1888
Thomas Stearns Eliot born in St Louis, Missouri, 26 September.
1910–11
A year in Paris; friendship with Jean Verdenal. August 1911 in Munich, where 'Prufrock' is finished.
1911–13
Harvard graduate study: Sanskrit and Pali with C. R. Lanman, the Upanishads, Buddhism, and F. H. Bradley's philosophy.
1914
Eliot arrives in England; meets Ezra Pound in September.
1915
Jean Verdenal killed at the Dardanelles in May. Eliot marries Vivien Haigh-Wood in June. 'The Death of Saint Narcissus' set in type and withdrawn.
1917
Joins Lloyds Bank, Colonial and Foreign Department, Lombard Street. Prufrock and Other Observations published, dedicated to Verdenal.
1918
'Dans le Restaurant' written; its last stanza will become 'Death by Water'.
1919
December: Eliot tells his mother his New Year's resolution is 'to write a long poem I have had on my mind for a long time'.
1921, spring
Parts I and II drafted, under the title 'He Do the Police in Different Voices'.
1921, summer
Eliot's mother, sister and brother visit London for ten weeks. His health breaks down.
1921, October
Lloyds grants three months' sick leave. Eliot goes to Margate, 15 October to 12 November, and writes fifty lines of Part III in a shelter on the front.
1921, November
To Paris, leaving Vivien; then to Lausanne under Dr Roger Vittoz. Parts IV and V written.
1922, January
Back through Paris. Pound edits the typescript, in person and then by post, cutting it roughly in half. 'Complimenti, you bitch.'
1922, June
Eliot reads the poem aloud at the Woolfs'. Virginia Woolf: 'He sang it & chanted it rhythmed it.'
1922, September
Smyrna burns, 13–22 September.
1922, October
First publication, without notes, in the first number of The Criterion, Eliot's own new magazine.
1922, November
American publication in The Dial. The Dial's $2,000 award for 1922 goes to Eliot.
1922, December
Boni & Liveright publish the first book edition in New York, 1,000 copies, with the notes written to fill it out.
1923, January
Eliot inscribes 'For Ezra Pound, il miglior fabbro' in Pound's copy.
1923, September
The Hogarth Press edition, hand-set by Virginia and Leonard Woolf at Richmond, about 460 copies.
1925
Poems 1909–1925 prints the dedication to Pound for the first time. Eliot leaves Lloyds for Faber & Gwyer.
1933
Eliot records the poem at Columbia University, on aluminium discs, the earliest surviving reading (once dated 1935; redated by recent scholarship).
1940s
Eliot records the poem again; this later reading, dated 1946 by the Poetry Archive and issued on record, is the one most often heard.
1956
'The Frontiers of Criticism', Minneapolis: the notes were 'bogus scholarship', and he regrets the 'wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail'.
1965
Eliot dies in London, 4 January.
1968
The New York Public Library announces that it holds the lost typescript, with Pound's and Vivien's pencil on it.
1971
Valerie Eliot's facsimile edition of the drafts.
2015
Christopher Ricks and Jim McCue's annotated edition, The Poems of T. S. Eliot.
2022
Centenary. The 1922 text is in the public domain in the United States; in the United Kingdom it remains in copyright until the end of 2035.

How the site works

The poem page is the whole thing. The row of lenses above the text changes what the page shows beside each line: Eliot’s notes, the sources, the speakers, the languages, the water, the places, the hours, the cuts. The keys 1 to 9 switch lenses; Escape closes anything open. Marked phrases open a card in the margin (or under the line, on a phone). The hairline strip at the edge of the screen is the poem itself, one mark per line, coloured by the lens you are using; click it to move. The violet hour, the dark setting, follows your system’s preference and can be switched with the small dot in the running head.

Two ways of reading are meant to be slower than a page allows. “Line by line” dims everything but one line and brings whatever belongs to it into the margin: the echo, Eliot’s note, a change of speaker, a translation; the arrow keys move, or let it walk on its own at a reading pace. In the voices lens, clicking a speaker follows that voice alone through the poem, so you can hear what the woman in the chair says from beginning to end, or the thunder, or the Thames-daughters.
