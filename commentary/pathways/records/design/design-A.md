Placement in the chooser (twelve pathways once the three kept ones are counted; "about nine" new): keep "A first reading" first; then the three of this design in the order grail, passion, east, because they are the three frames Eliot's own notes claim for the poem (the headnote's Weston and Frazer; the biblical notes on lines 20, 23 and 360; the Upanishad and the Fire Sermon in the notes on 308, 402 and 434), and because read in that order they make a sequence a newcomer can follow: the myth as Weston and Frazer told it, its Christian form, and the Indian texts Eliot set beside it. Then dante, love, seers (the poem's people and voices), then war, wagner, method (the poem's materials and its way of working), then Cities, then The poem in Eliot's life last. If the chooser must be shorter, grail and passion are the two of mine that cannot be dropped; east could be folded into passion under a title such as "Augustine and the Buddha", but I argue below against that.

## 2. The pathways

### grail

**id:** grail
**title:** The Grail and the waste land
**reason:** What the poem takes from Jessie L. Weston’s book on the Grail legend and from Frazer’s dying god: the wounded king and his dry land, the drowned god, the empty chapel, and the rain that comes at the end.

**intro (draft):** Eliot’s headnote to his notes says that the title, the plan and “a good deal of the incidental symbolism” of the poem came from Jessie L. Weston’s <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920), and that a reader who knows Frazer’s <i>The Golden Bough</i>, in particular its two volumes on Adonis, Attis and Osiris, will recognise in the poem “certain references to vegetation ceremonies.” Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve the remains of an old rite of the seasons: a king whose wound or sickness leaves his land waste and its waters dry, and a knight who must come to his castle and ask the right question, so that the king is healed and the waters flow. Frazer collected the rites behind such a story, the god who dies with the year and is buried in the earth or thrown into the water so that he will come back with the spring. This pathway follows the poem’s use of the two books from the dead land of the first lines to the fisher on the shore at the end, and says at each stop what the old story is doing there and where the poem breaks with it. How much of the poem the myth explains has been argued since Cleanth Brooks made it the key to the whole in 1939; Eliot himself, in a lecture of 1956, regretted having sent so many readers off to the Tarot and the Grail. The notes at the stops mark readings as readings. (The headnote is now in eliot-notes.txt, with the 1922 book’s spelling “Atthis Adonis Osiris”; quote it from there.)

**Scholarship it rests on:**
- Weston, From Ritual to Romance (1920): the frame Eliot’s headnote names. Ch. II (the hero’s task: healing the king and lifting the curse from the land), ch. III (the freeing of the waters, which Weston traces to Indra in the <i>Rig-Veda</i>), ch. IV (Tammuz and Adonis; the effigy of the dead god “not buried in the earth, but thrown into the water”), ch. V (where she calls the Waste Land “the very heart of our problem”), ch. VI (the Tarot suits as the Grail talismans, a pack first used, she supposes, “to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land”), ch. IX (the Fisher King; the fish as a life symbol), ch. XIII (the Perilous Chapel). All verified in refs/weston.txt.
- Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), the abridgement, and Frazer, Adonis, Attis, Osiris, the two volumes the headnote names: ch. XXXII (images of Adonis “dressed to resemble corpses… carried out as to burial and then thrown into the sea or into springs”, with a revival celebrated next day), ch. XXXIII (the gardens of Adonis; the dead coming back “in the sprouting corn” and the spring flowers), ch. XXXIX (effigies of Osiris of earth and corn buried at the sowing and found to have sprouted). Verified in refs/frazer.txt. The “Hanged God” of Eliot’s note on 46 is a chapter of <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i> (bk. II, ch. V in the 1906 edition, pp. 185–191: Marsyas hanged on the tree as a double of Attis, Odin on the gallows), not of the abridgement, which has only Artemis “the Hanged One” (ch. XXXV) and the effigy of Attis hung on the pine (ch. XXXIV). The same book has a chapter on Hyacinth (bk. II, ch. VII), “interpreted as the vegetation which blooms and withers”; see Unsettled for the optional stop at 35.
- Brooks (1939): the first full reading of the poem through Weston and Frazer, and the source of several readings the stops attribute to him: the fear of the water that would save (55), the Tarot vulgarised into fortune-telling (43), Part V opening in Gethsemane and moving to the Chapel Perilous, where the cock, the lightning and the rain end the ordeal (386). Each attribution is to be confirmed by the writer with ol.py in modernpoetrytrad0000clea before it is used.
- Ricks and McCue, commentary on the headnote and on lines 46, 189, 312 and 425: the standard record of what Eliot took from the two books, of the 1922 and 1925 states of the notes, and of Eliot’s later remarks (the 1956 lecture “The Frontiers of Criticism”, which may be quoted in a phrase only).

**Stops (13), in line order.** Format: line — the words at the line — what the stop will say (the point, and the reading or fact it rests on) — sources to cite. The marginal card at the line is named where the stop should point at it instead of repeating it.

1. **0** — title page, “The Waste Land” — The title is Weston’s phrase for the land that lies waste with its wounded king, and Eliot’s headnote credits her book with the title, the plan and much of the symbolism, and Frazer’s Adonis volumes with the “vegetation ceremonies”. The stop gives the reader the two-book frame in two sentences (king, land, waters; god, burial, return), says that Weston’s theory of the Grail is not accepted by Arthurian scholars today but is what Eliot used, and points at the card “title” for the headnote’s wording. — Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II; Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. V (the “very heart of our problem” passage); Frazer, Adonis, Attis, Osiris; Ricks and McCue, commentary on the headnote.
2. **1** — “April is the cruellest month” — The return of spring, which the rites in Frazer exist to secure (the dead coming back “in the sprouting corn” and in the spring flowers), is felt here as cruelty, and the voice prefers winter’s “forgetful snow”. The point: the myth’s happy ending is what the poem’s first voice dreads. Brooks (1939) read the opening this way, as the waste land’s people fearing the life that rain brings (check the wording in Brooks before attributing). Method owns Chaucer at this line (card “april”); passion owns Part I’s title. — Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXIII; Brooks (1939).
3. **19** — “What are the roots that clutch” — The poem’s fullest picture of the waste land: dry stone, dead tree, no sound of water. In Weston the oldest form of the legend is the <i>Rig-Veda</i>’s Indra, who slays the giant that has imprisoned the waters and “has inundated the dry land”; this is the land before any freeing. The stop says so, and leaves the biblical voice of the passage (Ezekiel, Ecclesiastes, Isaiah) to passion, which stops at 20 and 25. — Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. III; Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II.
4. **43** — “Madame Sosostris, famous clairvoyante” — Weston’s ch. VI holds that the four suits of the Tarot (Cup, Lance, Sword, Dish) are the Grail’s talismans and that the pack was first used “to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land”. The stop says that the clairvoyante with a cold is that rite reduced to fortune-telling for money (Brooks (1939) made the point; check his wording), and that Eliot’s note on 46 admits he departed from the pack and ties the Man with Three Staves “quite arbitrarily” to the Fisher King. Points at the card “tarot” for the cards themselves. — Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. VI; Brooks (1939); Ricks and McCue, commentary on line 46.
5. **55** — “The Hanged Man. Fear death by water.” — The card she cannot find is the one Eliot’s note on 46 ties to “the Hanged God of Frazer” (Attis, whose effigy hung on the pine-tree: Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXIV) and to the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus (360); the god who must die is missing from the pack. Her warning turns the rite inside out: in Weston and Frazer the god’s image is thrown into the water so that the land may live, and Brooks (1939) read “Fear death by water” as the waste land’s fear of the one thing that would save it. — Eliot’s note on line 46 (on the page); Frazer, Adonis, Attis, Osiris, bk. II, ch. V (“The Hanged God”: Marsyas on the tree, Odin on the gallows; so numbered in the 1906 edition, pp. 185–191, seen in the archive.org scan b31346510; verify the same numbering in the two-volume 1914 edition the headnote names); Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. IV; Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXII; Brooks (1939).
6. **71** — “That corpse you planted last year in your garden” — Frazer’s buried god as a joke between two men who were at Mylae: the priests of Osiris buried effigies of earth and corn at the sowing and dug them up sprouted, and the “gardens of Adonis” were pots of quick-growing seed. “Will it bloom this year?” is the rite’s own question, asked by a man who wants the answer to be no (“keep the Dog far hence”). The card “corpse” gives Frazer’s rites; the stop says what the poem does with them. Dante owns Stetson and the crowd (69); the card “dog” owns Webster. — Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXIX; Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXIII.
7. **189** — “While I was fishing in the dull canal” — The Fisher King’s first appearance, if it is he: a man fishing behind a gasworks, thinking of “the king my brother’s wreck” and “the king my father’s death”. Weston’s ch. IX argues that the fish is an ancient life symbol and that the title Fisher belongs to the keeper of the Grail. The stop says the poem gives this king no wound and no castle, only a canal, a rat and the “White bodies naked on the low damp ground” of the land he keeps, and marks the identification as the usual reading (the retired Water stop had “usually taken as”; confirm in Southam, Guide, note on line 189). The card “fishing-canal” owns <i>The Tempest</i>. — Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. IX; Southam, Guide, note on line 189.
8. **202** — “Et O ces voix d’enfants, chantant dans la coupole!” — The last line of Verlaine’s sonnet on Wagner’s <i>Parsifal</i>, whose tenth line is “Il a guéri le roi”: he has healed the king. This is the one place in the poem where the Grail quest is achieved, and it is achieved in another man’s poem about an opera, in a line that follows Mrs Porter’s soda water and precedes the nightingale’s “Jug jug”. The stop says that and no more; wagner owns the sonnet, the opera and Amfortas. — Verlaine, “Parsifal”; Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. XIII (her closing sentence, that the Grail “woke to new life in the nineteenth century, making its fresh appeal through the genius of Tennyson and Wagner”; she says nothing else of the opera).
9. **312** — “Phlebas the Phoenician, a fortnight dead” — “Death by Water is executed in Part IV”, Eliot’s note on 46 says. In Weston the effigy of the dead god is “thrown into the water”; in Frazer the images of Adonis, dressed as corpses, are carried out as to burial and thrown into the sea, and the next day the god’s revival is celebrated. The stop says the poem gives the drowning and withholds the revival: the current picks Phlebas’s bones, he passes “the stages of his age and youth” backwards, and the section ends in a warning to the reader, not in a rising. Whether this is the death that renews the land or only a drowning is what readers have disagreed about since Brooks (1939); the writer should give Brooks’s view in his words after checking them. Dante owns Phlebas among the dead; the cards “phlebas” and “death-by-water-draft” own the French original and the cut voyage. — Eliot’s note on line 46 (on the page); Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. IV; Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXII; Brooks (1939).
10. **331** — “Here is no water but only rock” — Eliot’s headnote to the notes on Part V names three themes, the second “the approach to the Chapel Perilous (see Miss Weston’s book)”. The stop says that 331–359 is the quester’s road to the chapel told without a quester, through a land whose waters have not been freed, and that the “dry sterile thunder without rain” of 342 is the thunder that will speak at 400 (east owns what it says). Points at the card “no-water”. — Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. XIII; Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. III.
11. **353** — “If there were the sound of water only” — The freeing of the waters imagined as sound and then cancelled (“But there is no water”, 359): the hermit-thrush of Eliot’s note on 357, whose “water-dripping song” Chapman’s handbook praises, sings water into a place that has none. The stop absorbs the Birds stop at 357 and links the listening room’s recordings. — Chapman, Handbook (1895); Eliot’s note on line 357 (on the page); the listening room (listen.html#rec-xc899696-hermit-thrush-quebec, the link the retired Birds stop used).
12. **386** — “In this decayed hole among the mountains” — Weston’s Perilous Chapel: in the romances the knight, sheltering from a storm in a chapel in a waste place, finds a dead body on the altar, a Black Hand puts out the light, voices threaten. The poem’s chapel has no body and no hand, “only the wind’s home”; Brooks (1939) read its emptiness as the ordeal emptied of its terror, so that the cock, the lightning and “a damp gust / Bringing rain” (394–395) end the drought the whole poem has described. The stop says what the chapel lacks and what follows it, and leaves the dry bones, the cock and Peter to passion (391). Points at the card “chapel”. — Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. XIII; Brooks (1939); Eliot’s headnote to the notes on Part V (on the page).
13. **424** — “I sat upon the shore / Fishing, with the arid plain behind me” — Eliot’s note on 425 sends the reader to Weston’s chapter on the Fisher King. The king is still fishing, the plain behind him is still arid, and his question, “Shall I at least set my lands in order?”, is the question of a man who does not expect to be healed. The stop says the poem ends the legend at the moment before the healing, the knight’s question never asked, and that this is the usual reading of the ending (Brooks (1939); Ricks and McCue on 425). Passion owns Hezekiah at 426; wagner owns Amfortas. — Eliot’s note on line 425 (on the page); Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. IX; Brooks (1939); Ricks and McCue, commentary on line 425.

**What this pathway does not stop at, and why:** 35–41 (the hyacinth garden; Frazer’s spring flowers “sprang from their dust” could carry it, but love and seers are both there; see Unsettled); 396–398 (Ganga and the rain over Himavant: east owns them, though Weston’s ch. III is the bridge and the east stop at 396 should say so); 427 (London Bridge: war and method).

### passion

**id:** passion
**title:** The Passion
**reason:** The Bible and the Prayer Book in the poem, from the burial service that names Part I, through the prophets, the Passion and the road to Emmaus, to the blessing that Eliot’s last note puts beside “Shantih”.

**intro (draft):** The poem’s Christianity is in its titles, its prophets and its last section. Part I is named after the Order for the Burial of the Dead in the Book of Common Prayer, the service read at a grave, and the desert of its lines 19 to 30 speaks in the words of Ezekiel, Ecclesiastes and Isaiah, which Eliot’s notes point to. Part V opens, as Cleanth Brooks first set out in 1939, with the Passion told without its names, the arrest by torchlight, the agony in the garden, the death; it goes on to the road to Emmaus, where the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples, a journey Eliot’s headnote to the Part V notes gives as the first of the section’s three themes. Between them the poem passes an exile’s psalm (182), a City church (264) and Augustine’s Carthage (307), and Eliot’s last note translates the Sanskrit “Shantih” with the blessing that ends the Communion service. Eliot was not a Christian when he wrote the poem; he was baptised and confirmed in the Church of England in 1927. This pathway does not read the poem as a conversion. It follows what the poem does with the scriptures it quotes, and says at each stop what the passage takes from them and what it leaves out.

**Scholarship it rests on:**
- King James Bible and Book of Common Prayer (1662): the texts. The verses the stops use were checked in the KJV (Gutenberg #10): Ezekiel 2:1, 6:4, 37:1–14; Ecclesiastes 12:5, 12:7; Genesis 3:19; Isaiah 32:2, 38:1–5; Psalm 137; John 18:3; Luke 22:39–46, 23:27–28, 24:13–31; Matthew 26:74–75, 27:46; Philippians 4:7. The Prayer Book wording (the burial service; Coverdale’s Psalm 137; the Communion blessing) is to be checked against the Wikisource 1662 text, which I could not reach by search (see Unsettled).
- Brooks (1939): Part V opens in Gethsemane and moves to Golgotha, and the poem “associates Christ in Gethsemane with the other hanged gods” (wording seen in a casebook’s reprint of the essay; the writer confirms it in modernpoetrytrad0000clea). Brooks is also the owner of the Chapel Perilous reading grail uses at 386.
- Southam, Guide, notes on lines 20, 23, 25, 182, 264, 307, 322, 360, 368, 391–392, 426 and 434, and Ricks and McCue on the same lines: the commentaries that record the biblical sources and the readings the stops call “usual”; each stop names which to check.
- Grover Smith (1956): the fullest early hunt for the biblical echoes of Part V (the women of Jerusalem at 368; the cock); to be confirmed by scan search before a stop attributes a reading to him. Gordon, Eliot’s Early Years, for the biographical frame in the intro (Eliot’s religious development before 1927); the fact of the 1927 baptism is uncontroversial and also in Crawford, Young Eliot, and Ricks and McCue’s chronology (check which the site prefers).

**Stops (12, and one optional), in line order.**

1. **1** — “April is the cruellest month” (Part I’s title stands above it) — Part I is called “The Burial of the Dead”, the title of the Prayer Book’s service, “The Order for the Burial of the Dead”, whose words at the grave are “earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust” and which begins with “I am the resurrection and the life”. The stop says the title puts the part’s dead (the Sibyl who cannot die, the crowd on the bridge, the corpse in the garden) under a service that promises resurrection, and that the part buries and does not raise; it sends the reader to the library record for the service. No card covers the part title. Grail owns the vegetation cycle at this line; dante owns the dead. — Book of Common Prayer, the Order for the Burial of the Dead; Ricks and McCue, commentary on the title of Part I (check how they key it); Southam, Guide, note on the title of Part I (check).
2. **20** — “Son of man” — Eliot’s note sends the reader to Ezekiel 2:1, where God sets the prophet on his feet to speak to “a rebellious nation”; his note on 23 sends “the cricket no relief” to Ecclesiastes 12:5, the Preacher’s chapter on old age, where “the grasshopper shall be a burden”. The card “son-of-man” gives both; the stop says what the pathway makes of them: the desert speaks in the prophets’ voice, but the one addressed as “Son of man” is told he “cannot say, or guess”, and Ezekiel’s threat that “your images shall be broken” (6:4) is already done, “A heap of broken images”. The Ezekiel 6 comparison is to be marked as a commentators’ one if Southam or Ricks and McCue record it, otherwise as a plain comparison. — King James Bible, Ezekiel 2:1; King James Bible, Ezekiel 6:4; King James Bible, Ecclesiastes 12:5; Southam, Guide, note on line 20 (check).
3. **25** — “There is shadow under this red rock” — Isaiah 32:2 promises a king who will be “as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land”; the rock here offers “something different”, and what it shows is “fear in a handful of dust”. The dust is Genesis 3:19 (“dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return”), Ecclesiastes 12:7 (“Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was”) and the burial service’s “dust to dust”. The stop says the shelter the prophet promised becomes the place where death is shown, and points at the card “red-rock”, which gives the “Saint Narcissus” draft the lines came from. — King James Bible, Isaiah 32:2; King James Bible, Genesis 3:19; King James Bible, Ecclesiastes 12:7; Book of Common Prayer, the Order for the Burial of the Dead; Southam, Guide, note on line 25 (check that Southam gives Isaiah 32:2; the library’s isaiah record already does).
4. **67** (optional; editor to decide) — “Saint Mary Woolnoth kept the hours / With a dead sound on the final stroke of nine” — Several critics hear in the “final stroke of nine” the ninth hour at which Christ died (Matthew 27:46; Mark 15:34); Eliot’s note on 68 says only “A phenomenon which I have often noticed”. The stop would say that a church keeps the hours over Dante’s crowd of the dead, and that the reading of nine as the ninth hour is some readers’, not the note’s. I found the reading in Macrae’s notes (1980), Davidson (1985) and Ellis (2009), not in Southam or Ricks and McCue by scan search; none of the three is in works.txt. Include only if Southam or Ricks and McCue records it; otherwise drop. The card “woolnoth” owns the church; cities owns 66; war owns 60. — King James Bible, Mark 15:34; the reading’s owner, to be found.
5. **182** — “By the waters of Leman I sat down and wept . . .” — Psalm 137 in the Prayer Book’s wording, the psalm of the exiles in Babylon who hang up their harps and are asked for “one of the songs of Sion”: “How shall we sing the Lord’s song in a strange land?” The stop says the poem’s singer, between two lines of Spenser’s wedding-song, is that exile, and that his song is cut short, “I speak not loud or long”. The card “leman” owns the wording and Lake Geneva; eliot owns Lausanne; method owns Spenser at 176. — Book of Common Prayer, Psalm 137; King James Bible, Psalm 137:4.
6. **259** — “O City city, I can sometimes hear” — The poem’s one church interior and its one “splendour” in London: St Magnus the Martyr in Lower Thames Street, rebuilt by Wren after the Great Fire, which Eliot’s note on 264 calls “one of the finest among Wren’s interiors” and defends against the proposed demolition of nineteen City churches. Eliot had written on the threatened churches in his “London Letter” of May 1921 in <i>The Dial</i> (June 1921, pp. 690–691, under the heading “The True Church and the Nineteen Churches”; verified in the archive.org scan sim_dial_1921-06_70): the City churches “give to the business quarter of London a beauty which its hideous banks and commercial houses have not quite defaced”, some “by Christopher Wren himself”, and the loss of “these empty naves, to receive the solitary visitor at noon from the dust and tumult of Lombard Street, will be irreparable and unforgotten”; he cites the same LCC pamphlet as the note on 264. He names All Hallows and St Michael Paternoster Royal there, not St Magnus; and in the same paragraph he quotes, in Italian, the Ugolino line he uses at line 412. The stop says the poem’s Christianity has here a building with a martyr’s name, seen from a public bar among fishmen, and that the poem’s word for what its walls hold is “Inexplicable”. The card “strand” owns the route; cities owns the place. — Eliot’s note on line 264 (on the page); LCC, Nineteen City Churches (1920); Rainey, Annotated Waste Land, note on line 264; Rainey, Annotated Waste Land, “London Letter, May 1921” (reprinted there); the “London Letter” itself as a new work (see Unsettled for its record).
7. **307** — “To Carthage then I came” — Augustine’s <i>Confessions</i>, Book III, in Pusey’s translation, which Eliot’s note quotes: “a cauldron of unholy loves sang all about mine ears”; then, at 309, Book X: “I entangle my steps with these outward beauties; but Thou pluckest me out, O Lord, Thou pluckest me out” (from the chapter on the temptation of the eyes; verified in Gutenberg #3296, Book X; give the chapter after checking Pusey’s numbering, X.34). The stop says the poem sets the convert’s memory of his lust beside the Buddha’s sermon on fire; that Eliot’s note (printed as 312 in the 1922 notes, though it belongs to line 309) calls the pair “eastern and western asceticism”; and that “pluckest” is the one place in the poem where God acts on a speaker. East owns the Fire Sermon and the note’s claim about the collocation; the card “carthage” owns the Latin. — Augustine, Confessions, bk. III, ch. 1; Augustine, Confessions, bk. X, ch. 34 (check); Southam, Guide, note on line 307.
8. **322** — “After the torchlight red on sweaty faces” — The Passion without its names: the arrest by night, when the band comes “with lanterns and torches” (John 18:3); “the frosty silence in the gardens”, Gethsemane, where “being in an agony he prayed more earnestly” (Luke 22:44); “Prison and palace”; and the death, “He who was living is now dead”. Brooks (1939) read the lines so and noted that the poem “associates Christ in Gethsemane with the other hanged gods” (check wording). The stop says the Passion is told in the past, “After… After… After”, by a “we” who are “now dying”, and that the resurrection is not told. The card “torchlight” gives the verses. — King James Bible, John 18:3; King James Bible, Luke 22:39–46; Brooks (1939); Southam, Guide, note on line 322.
9. **360** — “Who is the third who walks always beside you?” — Luke 24:13–31: on the day of the resurrection two disciples walking to Emmaus are joined by a stranger, “But their eyes were holden that they should not know him” (24:16), until he breaks bread. Eliot’s headnote to the Part V notes gives “the journey to Emmaus” as the section’s first theme; his note on 360 gives the Antarctic explorers’ delusion of an extra man; his note on 46 ties “the hooded figure” to the Hanged Man. The stop says the risen Christ is in the poem only as an uncounted companion, “wrapt in a brown mantle, hooded”, whose sex cannot be told, and that the question is never answered; it marks the Hanged Man association as Eliot’s own and leaves Shackleton to the card “third”. Seers owns “eyes were holden” as a fact about sight; passion owns the Emmaus story. — King James Bible, Luke 24:13–31; Shackleton, South, ch. X (only to point at the card).
10. **367** — “What is that sound high in the air / Murmur of maternal lamentation” — Commentators have connected the lamentation with the women who followed Christ to the cross, “Daughters of Jerusalem, weep not for me, but weep for yourselves, and for your children” (Luke 23:28), and Jerusalem is the first of the five cities at 375; Eliot’s note on 367–377 gives Hesse on eastern Europe instead. The stop gives both, marks the Luke reading as commentators’ (the retired Women stop already had it so; find the owner: Grover Smith (1956) is the likely one, Southam, Guide, note on line 368 the other), and leaves Hesse, the hordes and the falling towers to war. — King James Bible, Luke 23:27–28; Grover Smith (1956) (check); Southam, Guide, note on line 368 (check).
11. **391** — “Dry bones can harm no one.” — Ezekiel 37: the valley of dry bones, “Son of man, can these bones live?”, and the bones that live when the prophet prophesies to them; here they “can harm no one” and no one prophesies. Then the cock: readers have heard the cock that crowed at Peter’s denial (Matthew 26:74–75; Luke 22:60–61) and the cock of <i>Hamlet</i> I.i, at whose crowing the ghost fades; its crow is followed by lightning and “a damp gust / Bringing rain”, the only event in the chapel. The stop says Ezekiel’s promise is turned round (bones that can neither live nor harm) and names the readers of the cock (the retired Birds stop said “Readers have heard”; find the owners, likely Grover Smith (1956) and Brooks (1939)); grail owns the Chapel Perilous at 386. — King James Bible, Ezekiel 37:1–14; King James Bible, Matthew 26:74–75; Shakespeare, Hamlet I.i; Brooks (1939); Grover Smith (1956) (check).
12. **426** — “Shall I at least set my lands in order?” — Isaiah 38:1: Hezekiah, “sick unto death”, is told by Isaiah, “Set thine house in order: for thou shalt die, and not live”; he turns his face to the wall, prays and weeps, and is given fifteen more years (38:5). The stop says the poem’s king takes the sentence without the reprieve and makes it smaller, “at least”, “my lands”; grail owns the Fisher King at 424, and the card “fisher-king” already names Hezekiah, so the stop points at it and says what the pathway adds. — King James Bible, Isaiah 38:1–5; King James Bible, 2 Kings 20:1; Southam, Guide, note on line 426 (check).
13. **434** — “Shantih shantih shantih” — Eliot’s note translates the word with “The Peace which passeth understanding”, the words of the blessing that ends the Communion service in the Prayer Book (“The peace of God, which passeth all understanding, keep your hearts and minds in the knowledge and love of God”), from Philippians 4:7. In 1922 he called this “a feeble translation of the content of this word”; in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i> he changed it to “is our equivalent to this word” (verified in the scan poems19091925030616mbp). The stop says the poem’s last word is glossed by its author with the church’s blessing, and that he softened the gloss three years later; east owns the word itself and the Upanishad’s ending. — King James Bible, Philippians 4:7; Book of Common Prayer, the Order of Holy Communion (the blessing; check the 1662 wording); Poems 1909–1925 (the note on Shantih); Ricks and McCue, commentary on line 434.

**What this pathway does not stop at, and why:** 0 (the Sibyl and the dedication: dante and seers); 74 (Webster’s dirge over an unburied body: the card “dog” and dante); 202 (Verlaine’s <i>Parsifal</i>, a Christian legend in an opera: wagner and grail); 386–389 (the Chapel Perilous: grail; passion begins at 391); 428 (Arnaut in the refining fire: dante; the purgatorial reading of the last fire is noted under east).

### east

**id:** east
**title:** The Fire Sermon and the thunder
**reason:** The Buddha and the Upanishads in the poem: the sermon that names Part III, the thunder that speaks Sanskrit in Part V, and the peace the poem ends on.

**intro (draft):** Eliot studied Sanskrit at Harvard for two years from 1911 with Charles Rockwell Lanman, and spent a further year on Indian philosophy with James Haughton Woods; he wrote in 1934 that the study had left him in “a state of enlightened mystification”. Two Indian texts are in the poem by his own account. Part III takes its title from the Buddha’s Fire Sermon, in which everything the senses meet is “on fire” with passion, hatred and infatuation; the sermon is heard at line 308, set beside Augustine as, in Eliot’s note, “eastern and western asceticism”. Part V takes its title and its last lines from the <i>Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad</i>, where the thunder’s syllable “DA” is heard by gods, men and demons as three different commands, and “Shantih” is the formal close of a scripture. This pathway follows the Indian texts from the title of Part III to the last word, says what the poem does with each command, and marks where Eliot changed his sources. Cleo McNelly Kearns’s <i>T. S. Eliot and Indic Traditions</i> (1987) is the study behind it.

**Scholarship it rests on:**
- Warren, Buddhism in Translations (1896), §73 “The Fire-Sermon”, pp. 351–53, translated from the Mahā-Vagga (verified in refs/warren.txt): the text Eliot’s note on 308 names. “All things, O priests, are on fire”; the eye and forms, the ear and sounds, the nose, the tongue, the body, the mind, each “on fire… With the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation”; the disciple who “conceives an aversion” for them “becomes divested of passion” and free.
- Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad V.2, in Müller’s translation (works.txt; verified in the archive.org scan p2upanishads00mluoft, Fifth Adhyāya, Second Brāhmaṇa): the gods hear “Damyata”, Be subdued; the men “Datta”, Give; the Asuras “Dayadhvam”, Be merciful; “The divine voice of thunder repeats the same, Da Da Da”. Eliot’s note on 402 cites Deussen’s German (p. 489) and numbers the passage “5, 1”; the card “da” records the numbering. Deussen’s page I could not verify (see Unsettled).
- Kearns (1987): the standard study of Eliot’s Indic sources, Harvard years and the thunder fable; a scan exists (tseliotindictrad0000kear) for ol.py checks, which the writer should use for every reading this pathway attributes to her.
- Eliot, After Strange Gods (1934): the sentence on two years of Sanskrit under Lanman and a year of Patañjali under Woods that left him in “a state of enlightened mystification” (seen in the 1934 scan; quote the phrase only). Crawford, Young Eliot, for the Harvard years (the scan search did not surface the Lanman pages; the writer should confirm before citing; James E. Miller’s <i>T. S. Eliot: The Making of an American Poet</i> (2005) has the course record if Crawford does not). Southam, Guide, notes on lines 308, 396–397 (confirmed: “Ganga… Ganges”; “Himavant: a holy mountain in the Himalaya range”), 402, 412, 419 and 434; Ricks and McCue on the same.

**Stops (10), in line order.**

1. **173** — “The river’s tent is broken” (Part III’s title stands above it) — Part III is called “The Fire Sermon” after the Buddha’s sermon at Gayā, which Eliot’s note on 308 says “corresponds in importance to the Sermon on the Mount”. The sermon says that all things are on fire: the eye and what it sees, the ear and what it hears, the tongue, the body, the mind, each burning with passion, hatred and infatuation. The stop says the title is given first and the sermon heard last (308), and tells the reader to carry the title through the part: the river and its litter, Sweeney and Mrs Porter, the merchant’s invitation, the typist and the clerk, the Thames-daughters, are what the sermon calls on fire. No card covers the part title. Method owns Spenser at 176; love owns the couplings. — Warren, Buddhism in Translations, §73; Kearns (1987).
2. **308** — “Burning burning burning burning” — The sermon itself, reduced to one word four times, without the priests, the senses or the disciple who “becomes divested of passion”; then Augustine, “O Lord Thou pluckest me out”. Eliot’s note (printed as 312 in the 1922 notes; it belongs to line 309) says the collocation of “these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism, as the culmination of this part of the poem, is not an accident”. The stop says what the sermon teaches (aversion from the senses, freedom from passion), what the poem keeps of it (the burning only), and that “asceticism” is Eliot’s word for what the two texts share; passion owns Augustine at 307, and the card “carthage” gives the Latin. — Warren, Buddhism in Translations, §73; Southam, Guide, note on line 308; Kearns (1987).
3. **327** — “Of thunder of spring over distant mountains” — Part V is called “What the Thunder Said”, and its first thunder is here, in the Passion passage, over distant mountains; the second, at 342, is “dry sterile thunder without rain”. The stop says the thunder is heard twice before it speaks, and that when it speaks, at 400, it will speak in Sanskrit; passion owns 322–330, grail owns 331–359. This stop shows only the poem and needs no sources.
4. **396** — “Ganga was sunken, and the limp leaves / Waited for rain” — The poem goes to India for its rain: the Ganges low, black clouds over Himavant (the Himalaya), the jungle “humped in silence”. The card “ganga” gives the names and their sources. The stop says that the rain the whole poem has wanted gathers in the country of the Upanishads, and adds a fact for the reader to weigh: the first evidence in Weston’s book for the “freeing of the waters” is the <i>Rig-Veda</i>’s Indra, the thunder god who “has inundated the dry land”. The writer states Weston’s chapter and leaves the connection to the reader. — Southam, Guide, note on lines 396–397; Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. III.
5. **400** — “Then spoke the thunder / DA” — The fable of <i>Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad</i> V.2: the three kinds of Prajāpati’s children, gods, men and demons, finish their studentship and ask him to teach them; to each he says the one syllable “Da”, and each hears a different word, the gods “damyata” (be subdued, control yourselves), the men “datta” (give), the demons “dayadhvam” (be merciful, sympathise); “The divine voice of thunder repeats the same, Da Da Da”. Eliot’s note on 402 gives “Datta, dayadhvam, damyata (Give, sympathise, control)”. The stop says the poem puts the men’s word first and the gods’ word last, reversing the Upanishad’s order, so that the thunder’s one syllable becomes three answers in the poem’s own sequence; points at the card “da” for the fable. Mark the significance of the changed order, if any is claimed, as a reading (Kearns (1987) or Ricks and McCue, commentary on line 402; check which records the change). — Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad V.2; Ricks and McCue, commentary on line 402 (check); Kearns (1987) (check).
6. **402** — “Datta: what have we given?” — Give: the men’s word. The poem answers with a question and then “The awful daring of a moment’s surrender / Which an age of prudence can never retract”, a gift not to be found “in our obituaries” or under a solicitor’s seals. The stop says the command is answered with the one gift the poem allows, a surrender rather than a charity, and that “By this, and this only, we have existed” is the poem’s strongest affirmation; love owns the reading of the surrender as a love; the card “datta” gives Webster’s spider. — Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad V.2; Brooks (1939) (check what he makes of Datta before attributing).
7. **412** — “Dayadhvam: I have heard the key” — Sympathise, or in Müller “Be merciful”: the demons’ word. The poem answers with Ugolino’s tower (Eliot’s note gives <i>Inferno</i> XXXIII 46) and with Bradley’s sentence, quoted in the same note, that each soul’s experience is “a circle closed on the outside”. The stop says the command to feel for others is answered by a prison that each confirms by thinking of the key, and that the poem’s only relief is “aetherial rumours” at nightfall; dante owns Ugolino, and the card “dayadhvam” gives Bradley. — Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad V.2; Bradley, Appearance and Reality, p. 346; Kearns (1987) (check).
8. **419** — “Damyata: The boat responded” — Control: the gods’ word, put last. The poem answers with a boat that “responded / Gaily, to the hand expert with sail and oar” on a calm sea, and then turns conditional: “your heart would have responded / Gaily, when invited”. The stop says the only command the poem shows obeyed is obeyed by a boat, and that the heart’s obedience is in the conditional perfect, a thing that did not happen; eliot owns the sailing off Gloucester (card “damyata”). — Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad V.2; Southam, Guide, note on line 419 (check).
9. **433** — “Datta. Dayadhvam. Damyata.” — The three words again, with full stops, as the Upanishad’s thunder repeats “Da Da Da”, after the fragments of 427–432. The stop says the poem ends its heap of European fragments with the three Sanskrit commands set down as plain words, in the poem’s order, not the Upanishad’s, and then the formal ending. — Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad V.2.
10. **434** — “Shantih shantih shantih” — Eliot’s note: “Repeated as here, a formal ending to an Upanishad.” The word means peace and closes the Upanishads as an invocation. The stop says the poem takes the form of a scripture’s ending for its own, that Eliot’s gloss for the word (“The Peace which passeth understanding”) is the Prayer Book’s, and that he changed the gloss in 1925 from “a feeble translation” to “our equivalent”; passion owns the Prayer Book and Philippians at this line, so this stop says the Upanishad’s side and points across. — Ricks and McCue, commentary on line 434; Kearns (1987); Poems 1909–1925 (the note on Shantih).

**What this pathway does not stop at, and why:** 209 (Mr Eugenides of Smyrna: “east” in the poem’s Mediterranean sense, which is love’s and cities’); 428 (Arnaut’s “foco che gli affina”: the poem’s last fire is Dante’s refining one, and the contrast with “Burning” at 308 is a reading many critics make; dante owns the line, and I could not confirm an owner in the site’s scholarship by scan search, so it is not a stop here; see Unsettled); 0 and 1 (nothing Indian is there).

## 5. Unsettled: what I could not check, or want a second opinion on

1. **The number of the Augustine-and-Buddha note.** The site's eliot-notes.txt prints "312. From St. Augustine's Confessions again…", and the site's rule is to cite notes by the site's line; but line 312 is Phlebas, the note belongs to line 309, and the Gutenberg transcription of the same 1922 edition numbers it 309. The east and passion stops write "Eliot's note (printed as 312; it belongs to line 309)". The editor should decide the site's form.
2. **The Book of Common Prayer.** The Wikisource URL in works.txt (Book_of_Common_Prayer_(1662)) returns 404, Wikisource's search did not find the 1662 subpages, and Gutenberg #1662 (which SOURCES-ONLINE.md guessed at) is not the Prayer Book. The Church of England's own page of the 1662 burial service (churchofengland.org/prayer-and-worship/worship-texts-and-resources/book-common-prayer/burial-dead) confirms "earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust; in sure and certain hope of the Resurrection to eternal life" and "In the midst of life we are in death". Not confirmed there by my fetch, and to be checked before use: the service's opening sentence "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25) and the Communion blessing "The peace of God, which passeth all understanding, keep your hearts and minds…". The citations thread should replace the dead URL in works.txt.
3. **Deussen.** Eliot's note cites Deussen's <i>Sechzig Upanishads des Veda</i>, p. 489; I could not find the German book on archive.org (only his <i>Philosophy of the Upanishads</i>), so the stops cite Müller's translation, which works.txt gives as the site's text of the Upanishad, and report Deussen as Eliot's citation. If a writer finds the Deussen scan, p. 489 should be checked and the work added.
4. **Frazer's "Hanged God".** Verified in the 1906 edition (bk. II, ch. V, pp. 185–191); the two-volume 1914 edition Eliot's headnote names was not reachable in a usable scan (the DLI copies gave no text). The chapter number should be checked there.
5. **An optional grail stop at 35 (the hyacinth garden).** Frazer's <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i> has a chapter on Hyacinth (bk. II, ch. VII, "interpreted as the vegetation which blooms and withers"), and the abridgement (ch. XXXIII) says the spring flowers, "the violets and the hyacinths", were thought to spring from the dust of the dead. Grail could stop at 35 to say that the hyacinth is a dying god's flower. I left it out because love and seers are both in the garden already, and because no commentary in works.txt has been checked for the reading; the editor may add it (grail would then have fourteen stops).
6. **The ninth hour at line 68.** The reading of "the final stroke of nine" as the ninth hour of the crucifixion (Matthew 27:46) is in print (Macrae 1980; Davidson 1985; Ellis 2009) but I did not find it in Southam or Ricks and McCue by scan search; the optional passion stop at 67 should be used only if one of them records it.
7. **Attributions to Brooks (1939).** The stops attribute to Brooks: the theme of death-in-life and the fear of the water that would save (1, 55; Rainey's <i>Revisiting</i> quotes the "death-in-life" phrase as Brooks's); "vulgar fortune-telling" (43; confirmed in Jay Martin's 1968 collection, which reprints the essay); Part V opening in Gethsemane and the "other hanged gods" (322; seen in a casebook's reprint); the Chapel Perilous emptied of terror and the cock, lightning and rain (386; seen in the same reprint). Each should be confirmed in the scan modernpoetrytrad0000clea before the note is written, and Brooks's own words used.
8. **Grover Smith (1956).** Named as the likely owner of the Luke 23:28 reading at 367–368 and of the cock as Peter's at 392; the scan search did not confirm either in his 1956 book (his 1983 <i>The Waste Land</i> has the cock passage, but that book is not in works.txt). If neither is in the 1956 book, the stops should say "commentators" and cite Southam or Ricks and McCue instead, after checking.
9. **The London Letter.** A new work: T. S. Eliot, "London Letter," <i>The Dial</i> 70, no. 6 (June 1921), 686–91 (dated May 1921; the section "The True Church and the Nineteen Churches" is on pp. 690–691); open at https://archive.org/details/sim_dial_1921-06_70; reprinted in Rainey, <i>Annotated Waste Land</i>, as "London Letter, May 1921". Before 1930, so it may be quoted. The citations thread should add it to works.txt (suggested short: Eliot, "London Letter," Dial, June 1921).
10. **Crawford on the Sanskrit years.** Young Eliot has the pages on Lanman and Woods (scan snippets confirm "His professor was Charles Rockwell Lanman" and the later letter to Woods asking for news of Lanman), but I could not see the chapter number; the writer must locate it by chapter. The After Strange Gods sentence is confirmed in the 1934 scan (afterstrangegods0000elio_a6q3) and may be quoted only in a phrase.
11. **Kearns (1987).** Listed as the study behind east, but none of her readings has been checked; the scan tseliotindictrad0000kear is searchable with ol.py. Every reading the east stops attribute to her must be confirmed there first.
12. **The changed order of the thunder's words.** The fact (Upanishad: gods damyata, men datta, demons dayadhvam; poem: datta, dayadhvam, damyata) is verified in Müller. Which commentary records the change I could not confirm by scan search; the stop at 400 cites Ricks and McCue and Kearns with "(check)".
13. **The two fires (308 and 428).** The reading that Arnaut's refining fire answers the Buddha's burning is common in criticism (Helen Williams 1968, for instance), but I found no owner among the works in works.txt; it is not a stop in east, and the editor may wish dante's stop at 428 to carry it.
14. **The part titles.** The stops at 1 (passion) and 173 (east) are about the titles of Parts I and III, which poem.txt does not number as lines. Confirm that the poem page shows the part title at the top of those lines so that the guide's scroll lands on it; eliot-notes.txt now carries the titles.
15. **Line 1 with three new pathways** (grail, passion, method) and 434 with two (east, passion): the notes say different things, but the editor may prefer to thin them (see the overlaps table for the alternative at 1).
16. **Whether east should stand alone.** Its ten stops are all in Parts III and V, and the pathway could be folded into passion under a title such as "Augustine and the Buddha". I argue against it: Eliot's own note makes the pairing of east and west "the culmination" of Part III, the whole ending of the poem is the Upanishad's, and the owner asked for the poem's relation to its scriptures; a reader who wants the Indian texts should be able to take them in one pass.
17. **Weston's theory and the site's voice.** The grail intro says Arthurian scholars do not accept Weston's theory today; that is a fact of the field rather than a reading, but it needs a source the site can cite (Ricks and McCue's headnote commentary probably records the point; check), or it should be cut to "what Eliot used".
