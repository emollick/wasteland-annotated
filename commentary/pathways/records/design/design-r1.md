# Thematic pathways through The Waste Land: design, round 1

Designer's draft for the editor, 20 September 2026. Line numbers are the site's (line 0 is the title page; Eliot's note numbers from his 366 are given as the site's line, so "Eliot's note on line 425" is the note he printed as 424). Heads (title, reason, intro) use curly quotes as the site does; the stop columns are working notes for the writers, not finished stop text.

Reading of the brief: the owner wants pathways that carry what the poem means, not what it repeats. Each pathway below is one the scholarship names as a strand of the poem: Eliot's own notes and headnote, the commentaries (Ricks and McCue 2015, Southam 1994, Rainey 2006), and the older readings (Brooks 1939, Grover Smith 1956, Kenner 1959, Gardner 1949). Where a stop rests on a reading rather than a fact, the reading is named with its owner so the writer can mark it as a reading.

Ten thematic pathways. Titles are open to the editor; alternatives are noted under each pathway. The candidate set's nine are all kept (love rather than tiresias, seers as suggested); one is added, change (Metamorphosis), which takes the drowned-and-transformed matter of the retired Water pathway (Ariel's pearls, Ferdinand, Phlebas's bones) and Ovid's changes, which no other pathway could hold without repeating itself.

## 2. The pathways

### grail — The Grail and the waste land

**Title:** The Grail and the waste land. (Alternative: “Weston and Frazer”; the editor may prefer the names since the site already has a “Water” lens.)

**Reason:** The plan Eliot’s headnote credits to Jessie Weston’s book on the Grail: the wounded king, the dry land, the Chapel Perilous and the coming of the waters, followed through the poem.

**Intro (draft):** Eliot’s headnote to his notes says that “not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem were suggested by” Jessie L. Weston’s <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920), a study of the Grail legend, and that anyone acquainted with Frazer’s <i>Golden Bough</i> “will immediately recognise in the poem certain references to vegetation ceremonies.” Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve an ancient fertility rite: a land laid waste by the wound or sickness of its king and restored, its waters freed, when the knight who comes to the castle asks the right question. Frazer supplied the god who dies with the year and is buried, drowned or hanged so that he may return. This pathway follows what Eliot made of the two books, from the title page to the king fishing on the shore at line 424. Cleanth Brooks’s essay of 1939 is the reading that took Eliot’s headnote at its word; later commentators have asked how much of the legend is really in the poem, and the stops say where it is and where it is not.

**Scholarship it rests on:**
- Eliot's headnote to the notes (Weston: title, plan, symbolism; Frazer: "vegetation ceremonies"); Eliot's notes on line 46 (the Tarot, the Hanged Man and Frazer's Hanged God, the Man with Three Staves as the Fisher King), on Part V (the approach to the Chapel Perilous), and on line 425 (Weston's chapter on the Fisher King).
- Weston 1920: chapter II, "The Task of the Hero" (the healing of the Fisher King and the lifting of the curse on the waste land are one task); chapter III, "The Freeing of the Waters"; chapter IV, "Tammuz and Adonis"; chapter VI, "The Symbols" (the four suits of the Tarot as the Grail symbols); chapter IX, "The Fisher King"; chapter XII, "Mithra and Attis" (the cults carried west "through the medium of Syrian merchants"); chapter XIII, "The Perilous Chapel". (Chapter numbers checked against the Gutenberg text, #4090: the Perilous Chapel is XIII, not XII.)
- Frazer, The Golden Bough, abridged edition (1922): chapter XXXII, "The Ritual of Adonis" (images of the god "dressed to resemble corpses, were carried out as to burial and then thrown into the sea or into springs; and in some places his revival was celebrated on the following day"), and chapter XXXIII, "The Gardens of Adonis" (pots of quick-sprouting seed carried out with the images and flung into the water); Adonis, Attis, Osiris (third edition), the chapter "The Hanged God" in the Attis section, which Eliot's note on 46 has in mind.
- Cleanth Brooks, "The Waste Land: Critique of the Myth" (1939): the reading of the poem as the legend, including "Fear death by water" as a warning against the death that could bring life, and the merchant as the descendant of Weston's Syrian merchants.
- Ricks and McCue, commentary on the headnote and on lines 46, 189 and 424; Southam on 46, 189, 424 (the fisher "usually taken" as the Fisher King); Grover Smith 1956 for the plan followed closely; Hugh Kenner, The Invisible Poet (1959), for the doubt about how much of Weston governs the poem.

### passion — The Passion and the Prayer Book

**Title:** The Passion and the Prayer Book. (Alternatives: “Scripture and the Passion”; “The Christian poem” is rejected as a claim the poem does not make.)

**Reason:** The Bible and the Prayer Book in the poem, from the burial service that names Part I to the Passion, the road to Emmaus and the blessing at the end.

**Intro (draft):** Part I takes its title from the Book of Common Prayer’s Order for the Burial of the Dead, and the poem’s last word is glossed in Eliot’s note with words from the Prayer Book’s blessing at the end of Holy Communion, “The Peace which passeth understanding.” Between them the poem uses Ezekiel and Isaiah, the psalm of the exiles by the waters of Babylon, Augustine’s <i>Confessions</i>, and, in Part V, the arrest, trial and death of Christ told without his name, the walk to Emmaus on the day of the resurrection, the women who lamented on the way to the cross, Ezekiel’s dry bones, the cock that crowed for Peter and the prophet’s warning to king Hezekiah. Eliot was not a Christian when he wrote the poem (he was baptised into the Church of England in 1927), and this pathway does not make him one. It follows what the poem does with the Christian texts it uses, which is, at almost every stop, to take the words and leave out the promise.

**Scholarship it rests on:**
- Eliot's notes on lines 20, 23, 307, 308 ("corresponds in importance to the Sermon on the Mount"), the note on the collocation of Augustine and the Buddha (numbered 312 in eliot-notes.txt; 309 in the 1922 book: see Unsettled), the Part V headnote ("the journey to Emmaus"), the notes on 360 and 434.
- The Book of Common Prayer: the Order for the Burial of the Dead (opening sentence "I am the resurrection and the life"; "In the midst of life we are in death"; the committal "earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust"); Psalm 137 in Coverdale's psalter; the blessing at the end of Holy Communion ("The peace of God, which passeth all understanding"), from Philippians 4:7.
- The Bible (King James Version): Ezekiel 2:1 and 37:1–14; Isaiah 32:2 and 38:1–5; Ecclesiastes 12; Luke 22:39–62, 23:27–28 and 24:13–31; John 18:3; Matthew 26:36–75. Augustine, Confessions III.1 and X (Pusey's translation, in the library).
- Ricks and McCue, commentary on the title of Part I and on lines 322–330, 360, 367–368, 391–392, 426 and 434; Southam on the same lines; Rainey's notes on Part V; Grover Smith 1956 and Brooks 1939 on lines 322–330 as the Passion; Helen Gardner 1949 and Lyndall Gordon 1998 on the distance between this poem and the Christian poems that followed (Gordon for the baptism, 29 June 1927).

### east — The Fire Sermon and the thunder

**Title:** The Fire Sermon and the thunder. (Alternative: “The Buddha and the Upanishads”. The id could be buddha, but the pathway is as much Hindu as Buddhist, so east.)

**Reason:** The Buddha’s sermon and the Upanishad’s thunder: what Eliot took from his Sanskrit and Pali studies, and what he changed.

**Intro (draft):** At Harvard between 1911 and 1913 Eliot studied Sanskrit with Charles Lanman and Indian philosophy with James Woods, and read the Upanishads and the Pali scriptures in the originals (Robert Crawford, <i>Young Eliot</i>). Two of those texts enter the poem by name. Part III takes its title from the Buddha’s Fire Sermon, in which everything the senses touch is said to be on fire with passion, hatred and infatuation; Eliot’s note calls it as important as the Sermon on the Mount and sets its “burning” beside Augustine. Part V takes its title and its last words from the fable of the thunder in the <i>Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad</i>, in which gods, men and demons each hear their own instruction in the one syllable DA, and the poem ends with the formula that closes an Upanishad. This pathway takes the two texts in order and says at each stop what Eliot kept and what he changed: the order of the three words, and the answers he wrote for them.

**Scholarship it rests on:**
- Eliot's notes on line 308 (Warren), on the collocation of Augustine and the Buddha, on line 402 (his 401: the three words, "Give, sympathise, control", the fable in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, Deussen p. 489), and on line 434.
- Henry Clarke Warren, Buddhism in Translations (1896), section 73, "The Fire-Sermon", translated from the Mahā-Vagga: "All things, O priests, are on fire... With the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation" (checked in the archive.org text; in the library as buddha-fire-sermon).
- Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 5.2 (Eliot's note numbers it 5, 1): the three kinds of Prajāpati's offspring; DA heard as damyata by the gods, datta by men, dayadhvam by the demons; Deussen, Sechzig Upanishads des Veda (1897), p. 489, cited by page in Eliot's note; the library's paraphrase (brihadaranyaka).
- Crawford, Young Eliot (2015), on Lanman, Woods and the Indic studies; Ricks and McCue, commentary on 308, 401–402, 412, 419 and 434 (including the reports that Eliot said he had thought of becoming a Buddhist about the time of the poem: describe, do not quote); Rainey's notes on 308 and 402; Southam on 308, 402, 412, 419, 434; Brooks 1939 and Grover Smith 1956 on the three answers.

### dante — Dante and the dead

**Title:** Dante and the dead. (Alternative: “Dante’s dead”.)

**Reason:** The poem’s dead, read with Dante’s <i>Inferno</i> and <i>Purgatorio</i> open beside them, from the dedication to Arnaut in the fire.

**Intro (draft):** Dante is in the poem five times by Eliot’s own account: the dedication to Pound is Dante’s praise of Arnaut Daniel in <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI; the crowd on London Bridge is the crowd inside the gate of Hell, and its sighs are Limbo’s (<i>Inferno</i> III and IV); the first Thames-daughter speaks in La Pia’s words (<i>Purgatorio</i> V); the key turning in the door is Ugolino’s (<i>Inferno</i> XXXIII); and at line 428 Arnaut steps back into the fire. Eliot said in 1950 that he had meant the London Bridge lines to be recognised. This pathway takes those places in order and, between them, the poem’s other dead: the Sibyl who cannot die, the one who came back from the garden “neither / Living nor dead”, Stetson stopped in the street, the bones in the garret, Tiresias who “walked among the lowest of the dead”. At each stop it asks where in Dante’s ordering of the dead the poem has put its people, and what the placing says about them.

**Scholarship it rests on:**
- The dedication; Eliot's notes on lines 63, 64, 293, 412 and 428 (his 427).
- Dante: Inferno III 34–69 (the vestibule; "che mai non fur vivi", III 64; "vidi e conobbi l'ombra di colui / che fece per viltade il gran rifiuto", III 58–60); Inferno IV 25–27 and 41–42 (Limbo, "sanza speme vivemo in disio"); Inferno XV (Brunetto Latini recognised); Inferno XX 40–45 (Tiresias among the diviners); Inferno XXXIII 1–78 (Ugolino); Purgatorio V 130–136 (La Pia); Purgatorio XXVI 115–148 (Guinizelli, Arnaut, "miglior fabbro", "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina"). All in the library except Inferno XV and XX.
- Eliot's essay "Dante" (1929, in Selected Essays), which may be quoted, for his account of Dante's visual clarity and of Purgatorio XXVI; "What Dante Means to Me" (1950), which may only be described, for his statement that the London Bridge lines were meant to be recognised and that Dante was the most persistent influence on his verse.
- Ricks and McCue, commentary on the dedication and on 39–40, 62–65, 69, 245–246, 293–294, 412–415, 428; Southam on the same; Kenner 1959 (the Inferno as the model for the poem's London); Brooks 1939 (the poem's people as neither living nor dead); Homer, Odyssey XI, and Sophocles, Oedipus the King, for Tiresias at 245.

### method — The past beside the present

**Title:** The past beside the present. (Alternative: “The mythical method”, which is Eliot’s phrase but was said of Joyce.)

**Reason:** Eliot’s “continuous parallel between contemporaneity and antiquity”: the places where an old poem and a modern scene are made to share the same lines.

**Intro (draft):** In November 1923, reviewing Joyce’s <i>Ulysses</i> in <i>The Dial</i>, Eliot wrote that “in manipulating a continuous parallel between contemporaneity and antiquity, Mr Joyce is pursuing a method which others must pursue after him,” and that the method was “simply a way of controlling, of ordering, of giving a shape and a significance to the immense panorama of futility and anarchy which is contemporary history.” He was writing a year after his own poem and about someone else’s book, but readers have taken the sentences as the nearest thing he gave to an account of what <i>The Waste Land</i> does. This pathway stops where the parallel is plainest: where a line of Chaucer, Shakespeare, Spenser, Marvell, John Day, Goldsmith or Froude is set in the same sentence as a modern scene, so that the reader sees both at once and measures the distance. The stops say what each pairing does, and do not assume that the past comes off better.

**Scholarship it rests on:**
- T. S. Eliot, "Ulysses, Order, and Myth", The Dial, November 1923 (before 1930, so quotable; the wording above is checked against the Wikisource text of The Dial, vol. 75; the same paragraph has "Instead of narrative method, we may now use the mythical method").
- Eliot's notes on lines 60, 77, 92, 98, 176, 196, 197, 253, 279 and 432 (his 431, Kyd).
- Brooks 1939 (the parallels, and the argument that the poem's past is as sterile as its present); Kenner 1959 (the poem made of quotation); Grover Smith 1956 (the sources traced); Ricks and McCue's commentary on each line; Rainey 2006, introduction, on the allusions; the library texts chaucer, baudelaire-sept-vieillards, shakespeare-antony, shakespearian-rag, spenser-prothalamion, marvell-coy-mistress, day-parliament-of-bees, goldsmith-vicar, froude-elizabeth, kyd-spanish-tragedy.

### love — The lovers

**Title:** The lovers. (Alternatives: “Tiresias and the lovers”; the brief’s “the loves that fail” is a reading and is kept out of the title.)

**Reason:** The poem’s couples and what goes wrong between them, from the hyacinth garden to the typist and the clerk, with Tiresias, who has been both.

**Intro (draft):** Eliot’s note on Tiresias says that “all the women are one woman, and the two sexes meet in Tiresias,” and that what Tiresias sees “is the substance of the poem.” What he sees, in the one scene he tells, is a typist and a clerk. This pathway takes the poem’s couples in order: the two who came back from the hyacinth garden and could not speak; the woman with bad nerves and the man who does not answer; Lil and Albert, home from the army; the nymphs and the heirs of city directors who left no addresses; Mr Eugenides and his weekend at the Metropole; the typist and the young man carbuncular; the three Thames-daughters and what was done to them at Richmond, Moorgate and Margate; and at the end the thunder’s “moment’s surrender” and the heart that “would have responded”. Part III is named for the Buddha’s sermon on the fires of the senses, and the couples stand under it; but the pathway does not decide for the reader whether the poem despises its lovers or pities them. It says at each stop what the passage gives them and what it withholds.

### seers — Prophecy and sight

**Title:** Prophecy and sight. (Alternatives: “Seers and the blind”; “Prophets”.)

**Reason:** The prophets, fortune-tellers and blind seers who speak in the poem, and the eyes that fail to see.

**Intro (draft):** The poem’s epigraph is about a prophetess, the Sibyl of Cumae, seen hanging in a jar; the voice at line 20 is addressed as Ezekiel was, “Son of man”; the fortune-teller has a cold; the chief witness, Tiresias, is blind; and on the road to Emmaus the disciples’ eyes “were holden”. This pathway follows the poem’s seers and the poem’s eyes together: who is given sight and at what cost, and what the people who have eyes do with them (they fix them before their feet; they fail in the garden; they are pressed, lidless, waiting for a knock). Eliot’s note says that what Tiresias sees “is the substance of the poem”; the pathway asks at each stop what is seen, what is foreseen, and what is not seen.

- Eliot's notes on lines 20 (Ezekiel), 46 (the cards that "appear later"), 138 (Middleton), 218 (Tiresias, with the Ovid), 360 (Shackleton) and the Part V headnote (Emmaus).

### change — Metamorphosis

**Title:** Metamorphosis. (Alternatives: “Sea-change”, Ariel’s word, which is narrower; “The changes”.) This is the one pathway added to the candidate set; section 4 says what it absorbs.

**Reason:** Ovid’s changed bodies and Ariel’s sea-change: Philomel, Tiresias, the drowned man’s eyes, and whether anyone in the poem is changed.

**Intro (draft):** Two books of changed bodies lie under the poem. Ovid’s <i>Metamorphoses</i> gives it Philomel, raped and turned into a nightingale (“The change of Philomel”, line 99), Tiresias, who was a woman for seven years, Actaeon, Hyacinthus and the Sibyl; Ariel’s song in <i>The Tempest</i> gives it a drowned king whose eyes become pearls and whose body suffers “a sea-change / Into something rich and strange”. The poem quotes the pearls twice and the sea-change never. This pathway follows the changes: what Ovid’s and Shakespeare’s bodies are turned into, what the poem’s own people are turned into (Phlebas into picked bones, the typist into an “automatic hand”), and the change asked for in the poem’s last Latin line, “when shall I be as the swallow”. The reading behind it, that the poem wants a transformation it does not show, is Cleanth Brooks’s; the stops mark it as his and set the texts beside the lines.

### war — After the war

**Title:** After the war. (Alternatives: “The war”; “1922”.)

**Reason:** The poem three years after the Armistice: rats’ alley, Albert demobbed, the song of the Australian troops, the falling towers.

**Intro (draft):** The poem was written in 1921 and published in October 1922, three years after the Armistice, by a man who had spent the war in London and whose work at Lloyds Bank was the settlement of pre-war debts with Germany. It does not name the war. It has the trenches in “rats’ alley”, a husband four years in the army and “demobbed”, a song the Australian troops sang, a pub whose hours were cut by wartime order, a Bradford millionaire in a silk hat, the “hooded hordes” of an eastern Europe that Eliot’s note says is in decay, the capitals of fallen empires, and London Bridge falling down. This pathway takes them in order. It does not make the poem a war poem of Owen’s or Sassoon’s kind; it says at each stop what of the war and its aftermath the passage carries, and names the reader who first said so where the claim is a reading.

### wagner — Wagner

**Title:** Wagner.

**Reason:** The three passages of Wagner the poem quotes and the opera it names, with the drowned king of the Starnbergersee.

**Intro (draft):** Wagner is quoted in the poem three times, in German: the sailor’s song from the masthead that opens <i>Tristan und Isolde</i> (lines 31–34), the shepherd’s report in its third act that the sea is empty (42), and the Rhine-daughters’ refrain from <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, twice, on the Thames (277–278, 290–291). A fourth opera, <i>Parsifal</i>, Wagner’s Grail opera, is present through the last line of Verlaine’s sonnet on it (202); and Jessie Weston’s book, which Eliot’s headnote credits with the poem’s plan, records in its preface the discussions at the Bayreuth Festival of 1911 that gave her some of her evidence, and ends by saying that the Grail story woke to new life in the nineteenth century “through the genius of Tennyson and Wagner.” This pathway takes the Wagner in order and says what each opera is doing at its place in the poem. It begins at the Starnbergersee, where Wagner’s patron Ludwig II of Bavaria was found drowned in 1886, and ends with the Fisher King of line 424 beside Wagner’s wounded Grail king Amfortas; both are readings, Herbert Knust’s (1967), and the stops mark them as such.

## 3. Overlaps and decisions

Rule followed: two pathways may stop at one line when each has its own thing to say there; where they would say the same thing, one owns the line and the other passes or stops a line or two away. ## 5. The five replacement "ways in" items

Same fields as ways.txt (kind: path; title; path; body). Five parts, five pathways: grail, love, method, change, passion. The other five new pathways (east, dante, seers, war, wagner) have no way-in item; the editor may want a second path item in some parts (dante in Part I, war in Part II, east in Part III or V, seers in Part III, wagner in Part I), and I can draft them.

**Part I** (replaces "Meet the dead")
kind: path
title: Weston's plan, from the title on
path: grail
body: Eliot's headnote credits Jessie L. Weston's book on the Grail legend with "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem". The Grail pathway starts on the title page and stops at the five places in Part I where the plan shows: the dead land and the dry stone with "no sound of water", the Tarot cards that Weston connected with the Grail, the warning "Fear death by water", and the corpse in Stetson's garden that readers of Frazer were meant to recognise.

**Part II** (replaces "The women, one after another")
kind: path
title: The couples in the room and the pub
path: love
body: A woman who asks to be spoken to and a man who does not answer; Lil, told to get herself some teeth before Albert comes home; Ophelia's good night at closing time. The Lovers pathway takes the poem's couples in order, from the hyacinth garden to the thunder's "moment's surrender", and stops three times in this section. Eliot's note on line 218 says that "all the women are one woman"; the pathway sets the couples side by side so that the reader can judge it.

**Part III** (replaces "Every bird")
kind: path
title: Spenser's nymphs and the litter
path: method
body: Most of the Past-beside-the-present pathway is in this section: Spenser's wedding river and what the Thames now carries, Marvell's chariot and the rat, Day's Actaeon and Sweeney, Goldsmith's ruined woman and the gramophone, Elizabeth's barge and the barges of oil and tar. Take them in order and see what each pairing does. The pathway begins with Chaucer's April and ends at "These fragments I have shored against my ruins", with Eliot's 1923 sentences on the "mythical method" beside it.

**Part IV** (replaces "The drowned before Phlebas")
kind: path
title: The sea-change that does not come
path: change
body: Ariel's song, which the poem quotes at lines 48 and 125, promises that the drowned king's body will suffer "a sea-change / Into something rich and strange". Phlebas gets a current that picks his bones. The Metamorphosis pathway follows the poem's changed bodies, Philomel's, Tiresias's, the drowned man's, and reaches this section at line 315; Cleanth Brooks's reading, that the poem asks for a transformation it will not show, is set out there and marked as his.

**Part V** (replaces "Every 'nothing'")
kind: path
title: The Passion, without the name
path: passion
body: Lines 322 to 330 tell the arrest, the trial and the death of Christ without naming him, and the section goes on to the road to Emmaus, the women's lamentation, Ezekiel's dry bones, the cock that crowed for Peter and Isaiah's warning to a dying king. The Passion and the Prayer Book pathway takes them in order, and ends where Eliot's note on the last line does, with words from the Prayer Book's blessing at the end of Holy Communion.

## 6. Unsettled

Things I could not check from here, or would want the editor's decision on, in rough order of importance.

1. **The number of Eliot's note on the collocation.** eliot-notes.txt prints "312. From St. Augustine's Confessions again. The collocation of these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism..." In the 1922 Boni and Liveright book and the standard editions the note is numbered 309 (the line "O Lord Thou pluckest me out"), and the brief itself calls it "Eliot's note on 309". Either the site's file has a slip or the Notes lens attaches it to 312 on purpose. I have written "the note on the collocation" in the stops and left the number to the editor; passion 307 and east 308 both need it.
2. **Cleanth Brooks (1939).** Several stops rest on readings I attribute to Brooks from memory of the essay: the Syrian merchant (grail 209), the foot-washing and Parsifal (wagner 202), "Fear death by water" as the death that brings life (grail 55), Elizabeth and Leicester as no better than the present (method 279), the empty chapel as a terror of nothing (grail 386), the poem's people as neither living nor dead (dante 39). I could not reach the essay's text online (Modern Poetry and the Tradition, 1939; reprinted in Jay Martin's collection, 1968). The writers must verify each before attributing it, and re-attribute to Grover Smith 1956 or Southam where Brooks turns out not to say it.
3. **Two stops at one line.** The design has two pathways at lines 0 (three), 43, 209, 360, 412, 424 and 434. If the guide software cannot show two pathways' stops at the same line, the fallbacks are: seers 43 to 46; love 209 to 207; seers 360 to 366; dante 412 stays and east's Bradley moves into its 402 note; wagner 424 to 425; passion 434 folded into 426.
4. **Metamorphosis as a tenth pathway.** It is the one addition to the candidate set. Its case: the poem's transformations (Philomel, Tiresias, the pearls, Phlebas's bones, the swallow) are a strand the commentaries follow, and without it the drowned matter of the retired Water pathway has nowhere to go but grail, which is already thirteen stops. Its risk: it is the most reading-led of the ten, and it shares lines with love, seers and grail. If the editor wants nine, fold 48, 125, 191 and 315 into grail and 99, 203, 243 and 429 into love, and drop 257.
5. **Wagner at eight stops.** It meets the minimum only by taking 8 (Ludwig) and 306 ("la la"). Knust 1967 is verified to exist but I have not read it; Southam's note on line 8 should be checked for Ludwig before the stop is written. If the editor prefers, wagner folds into grail (202, 424) and love (31, 42) and the Rhine-daughters stay with the Part III listen item.
6. **Checks for particular stops.** dante 69: whether Southam or Ricks and McCue cite Inferno III 58–59 for "There I saw one I knew". seers 39: what Ricks and McCue say of "the heart of light". war 12: whether anyone reads the nationality line as post-war. war 231: the identification of the Bradford millionaire with a customer of Lloyds (Sir James Roberts is proposed on the web; find it in Ricks and McCue or Hollis or leave it out). passion 263: Eliot's "London Letter" in The Dial for June 1921 on the City churches. war 369: where Hesse's essay was first translated (Sydney Schiff, as Stephen Hudson, 1922; The Dial or The Criterion). wagner intro: the date Eliot first heard Tristan (Crawford) and Verdenal's letters on Wagner (Letters, vol. 1, 1912). east: the report that Eliot considered becoming a Buddhist about the time of the poem (Stephen Spender's memoir; cite through Ricks and McCue or Rainey, and describe).
7. **Verified here, for the writers' comfort.** Weston's chapter numbers and titles are from the Gutenberg text (#4090): III "The Freeing of the Waters", IV "Tammuz and Adonis", VI "The Symbols", IX "The Fisher King", XII "Mithra and Attis" (the Syrian merchants), XIII "The Perilous Chapel"; the site's library and glosses say "Chapel Perilous" and Eliot's headnote does too, but Weston's chapter is "The Perilous Chapel". Frazer's abridged Golden Bough (Gutenberg #3623): XXXII "The Ritual of Adonis" and XXXIII "The Gardens of Adonis", quoted above; "The Hanged God" is a chapter of the third edition's Adonis, Attis, Osiris (Attis part), not of the abridgement. Warren's Fire Sermon is section 73 of Buddhism in Translations, from the Mahā-Vagga, and the wording "All things, O priests, are on fire" and "With the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation" is checked against the archive.org text. The 1923 Dial sentences are checked against Wikisource. Weston's preface records her discussions with Professor von Schroeder at the Bayreuth Festival of 1911, and her last sentence names "the genius of Tennyson and Wagner" as the legend's revivers.
8. **Titles and order.** Alternatives are listed under each pathway; the chooser's order is a proposal. The reasons are one sentence each and the intros one paragraph, as the standard asks, but all are drafts and the writers will have the last word on tone.
9. **Second way-in items.** Five pathways have no way in. If the lists can take two path items, I suggest Part I "Dante's crowd on the bridge" (dante), Part II "Albert comes home" (war), Part III "The sermon over the section" (east), Part III or I "The seer with a cold" (seers), Part I "Tristan on either side of the garden" (wagner); I can draft bodies on request.
