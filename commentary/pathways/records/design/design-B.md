# Design B: dante, love, seers

Three thematic pathways for the annotated *Waste Land*, designed 20 September 2026 from DESIGN-BRIEF-B.md under STANDARD.md. Line numbers are the site's (line 0 is the title page). For each stop the design gives the point, the reading or fact it rests on, and the sources the writer should cite; the writers check the details and write the notes. Readings are marked as readings with their owners; where a stop rests on something I could not check today it is listed in section 5.

## 1. The three pathways

| id | title | reason |
|---|---|---|
| dante | Dante and the dead | Take this pathway to follow the poem's dead through the cantos of Dante it keeps quoting, from the crowd on London Bridge to Arnaut in the refining fire. |
| love | The loves that fail | Take this pathway for the poem's lovers and couples, from the hyacinth garden to the typist's divan, and for what Tiresias, Augustine and the thunder make of them. |
| seers | Prophets and seers | Take this pathway for the poem's prophets, fortune-tellers and blind seers, from the Sibyl in the epigraph to Isaiah's warning near the end, and for what each of them is allowed to see. |

Placement in the chooser (A first reading before the thematic pathways, Cities and The poem in Eliot's life after them): grail first, since Eliot's headnote makes Weston the plan of the poem; then passion and east, the poem's two religious frames; then dante, love and seers together, in that order. The brief's numbering puts seers ninth; I would move it up to sixth, beside love, because Tiresias joins them (love owns the scene he watches, seers owns his blindness and his sight) and a reader who has just taken one will want the other. War, wagner and method follow.

Ids checked against the ones in use (first, water, dead, women, cities, birds, nothing, eyes, eliot): dante, love and seers are free.

## 2. The pathways

### dante — Dante and the dead

**id:** dante
**title:** Dante and the dead
**reason:** Take this pathway to follow the poem's dead through the cantos of Dante it keeps quoting, from the crowd on London Bridge to Arnaut in the refining fire.

**intro (draft):** Eliot’s notes send the reader to Dante five times: to the <i>Inferno</i> for the crowd on London Bridge (his notes on lines 63 and 64) and for Ugolino’s tower (412), and to the <i>Purgatorio</i> for La Pia (293) and for Arnaut Daniel in the refining fire (428); the dedication to Pound, “il miglior fabbro”, is from the same canto as Arnaut. The poem’s dead are more than these passages: the Sibyl who cannot die, the corpse planted in Stetson’s garden, the bones in the garret, Phlebas a fortnight drowned, Tiresias who “walked among the lowest of the dead”. This pathway takes the dead in the order of the poem and asks at each stop what the passage does with Dante’s way of seeing them, in which the dead speak of their lives in the past tense, ask to be remembered, and are recognised by the living who meet them. Eliot’s essay on Dante in <i>The Sacred Wood</i> (1920) is quoted where it bears on a line.

**Is it really in the poem?** Five of Eliot's notes go to Dante, more than to any other author except Shakespeare; the dedication is Dante; and Eliot said in 1950 that he gave the Dante references in his notes so that a reader who recognised them would know he was meant to ("What Dante Means to Me", described, not quoted). The pathway adds no allusion of its own; where it sets a passage beside a canto Eliot did not name (lines 39, 69, 245, 312) it says so and names who made the comparison.

**Scholarship it rests on:**
1. Eliot's notes on lines 63, 64, 293, 412 and 428 (the site's numbers; the last two are the notes he numbered 411 and 427), and the dedication (Purgatorio XXVI 117; the site's dedication card): they fix which cantos the poem points to.
2. Eliot, "Dante", in <i>The Sacred Wood</i> (1920): what Eliot made in 1920 of the Brunetto Latini episode (Inferno XV), the voyage of Ulysses (XXVI) and Arnaut Daniel; before 1930, so quotable. His <i>Dante</i> (1929; in <i>Selected Essays</i>, 1932) is also before 1930 and open on archive.org (section 5): it lists Brunetto, Ulysses and Ugolino among the episodes of the Inferno that "first convinced" him, quotes La Pia and Arnaut with his own translations, and says of the purgatorial flame that it is "deliberately and consciously accepted by the penitent" and that "In their suffering is hope". "What Dante Means to Me" (1950): Dante as the deepest and most lasting influence on his verse, and the reason he gave the Dante references in the notes; described only.
3. Grover Smith (1956): the London Bridge passage as framed by Inferno III and IV; Stetson as "the modern representative" of the neutral "who made the great refusal" (Inferno III 59–60).
4. Southam, Guide, and Ricks and McCue, commentary, on lines 63–64, 69, 245, 293, 312, 412 and 428: the standard notes; Southam's note on 293 translates La Pia's line.
5. The Paris Review interview (1959), in which Eliot said the long draft of Part IV was inspired by the Ulysses canto of the Inferno (described, not quoted); Facsimile (1971), the typescript of Part IV; Pound, Letters, ed. Paige, nos. 181–183, for Pound's insistence on keeping Phlebas.

**Stops (11):**

1. **Line 0** — “For Ezra Pound / il miglior fabbro”; the Sibyl in the epigraph.
   Says: the dedication is Purgatorio XXVI 117, Guido Guinizelli's words for Arnaut Daniel among the lustful in the refining fire; the poem's last words from Dante (428) are from the same canto, so the Purgatorio's fire stands at both ends of the poem. The Sibyl of the epigraph, who wants only to die, was in Virgil the guide who took Aeneas among the dead (Aeneid VI) as Virgil took Dante; the stop says only that the poem opens as a visit among the dead, and leaves her prophecy to the seers pathway. Point to the dedication card.
   Cite: Dante, Purgatorio XXVI 115–117 and 140–148; Petronius, Satyricon 48; Virgil, Aeneid VI; Poems 1909–1925 (first printing of the dedication); Ricks and McCue, commentary on the dedication; Southam, Guide, note on the dedication.

2. **Line 40** — “Living nor dead, and I knew nothing,”.
   Says: the first "I" of the poem describes the condition of the crowd the poem will meet at line 62. Seven lines after the passage Eliot's note on 63 quotes, Dante calls that crowd "questi sciaurati, che mai non fur vivi", these wretches who never were alive (Inferno III 64), the neutrals who lived "sanza 'nfamia e sanza lodo" (III 36). No note of Eliot's makes the link; Craig Raine (2006) sets the two passages side by side, and Ann Brady (1978) made the comparison before him (neither book is in works.txt: section 5). The stop presents the two lines together, names Raine as the owner of the reading, and lets the reader judge. Love owns the garden and the girl (35); seers owns the failed eyes (39); this stop, at the line that begins "Living nor dead", owns only that condition.
   Cite: Dante, Inferno III 34–36 and 64; Raine (2006) (new work); Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 39–41 (for whatever they record; writer to check).

3. **Line 62** — “A crowd flowed over London Bridge, so many, / I had not thought death had undone so many.”
   Says: Eliot's notes give Inferno III 55–57 and IV 25–27. The first is the train of the neutrals just inside the gate of Hell, "so long a train of people that I should never have believed death had undone so many"; the second is Limbo, where the unbaptised virtuous sigh without torment and the sighs make the eternal air tremble. Neither is a place of punishment: the London crowd is condemned to nothing worse than nothingness, and the two cantos together are the poem's judgement on it (Grover Smith reads the passage as framed by the two cantos). Absorbs the retired Dead stop at 62. War and cities own "Unreal City" (60); this stop owns Dante.
   Cite: Dante, Inferno III 34–36, 55–57; IV 25–27; Grover Smith (1956); Southam, Guide, note on line 63; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 62–65; Eliot, "What Dante Means to Me" (1950), described.

4. **Line 69** — “There I saw one I knew, and stopped him, crying: ‘Stetson!’”
   Says: to recognise an acquaintance among the dead and stop him is Dante's recurring scene; in Inferno XV Dante is caught by the hem by a burnt figure and says "Siete voi qui, ser Brunetto?" (XV 23–30). Eliot in The Sacred Wood (1920) wrote that "The emotion of the passage resides in Brunetto's excellence in damnation—so admirable a soul, and so perverse", and in <i>Dante</i> (1929) named the Brunetto and Ulysses episodes as the parts of the Inferno "which first convinced me". Grover Smith (1956) reads Stetson as the modern representative of the neutral "who made the great refusal" (Inferno III 59–60); A. D. Moody (1974) compared the greeting with Dante's to Brunetto. The corpse belongs to grail (Frazer) and Mylae to war; this stop is about the meeting and the greeting.
   Cite: Dante, Inferno XV 22–33 and III 58–60; The Sacred Wood ("Dante"); Grover Smith (1956); Ricks and McCue, commentary on line 69; Moody (1974) if the writer uses him (new work, section 5).

5. **Line 193** — “White bodies naked on the low damp ground / And bones cast in a little low dry garret”.
   Says: the poem's dead are not buried. Part I is named for the burial service and no burial happens in it; the corpse is dug up by the Dog (74–75, Cornelia's dirge for "unburied men" in Webster), the dead men in rats' alley "lost their bones" (116), the bones here are rattled by a rat "year to year", Phlebas's bones are picked by a current (316), and the chapel's "Dry bones can harm no one" (391). The fisher has just mourned "the king my father's death" in Ferdinand's words from The Tempest (Eliot's note on 192), and Ferdinand's father is not dead: the stop says the poem's dead are of uncertain status, neither laid to rest nor gone, and points forward to 312 and 391. Absorbs the retired Dead stop at 194. Passion owns the burial service and Ezekiel's bones; war owns rats' alley.
   Cite: Webster, The White Devil V.iv; Shakespeare, The Tempest I.ii; Book of Common Prayer, the Order for the Burial of the Dead (for the title only); Southam, Guide, note on lines 191–195; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 193–195.

6. **Line 245** — “I who have sat by Thebes below the wall / And walked among the lowest of the dead.”
   Says: Tiresias's two credentials are two other poems. Thebes is Sophocles' city, where Tiresias told Oedipus what he could not see; the walk among the dead is Odyssey XI, where Odysseus summons the shades, and Circe has told him that Tiresias alone among the dead keeps his understanding, "the other ghosts flit about aimlessly" (Butler's translation, Odyssey X). Dante too set Tiresias among the dead, with the diviners of Inferno XX (40–45), and told his change of sex there; Eliot's note goes to Ovid, not to Dante, and the stop says so. Seers owns 218 (blindness and foresight); this stop owns the descent. Absorbs the retired Dead stop at 245.
   Cite: Homer, Odyssey X 490–495 and XI (new work: Butler's translation, 1900, section 5); Dante, Inferno XX 40–45; Southam, Guide, note on line 245; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 245–246; Sophocles, Oedipus the King, only if the writer wants the Thebes reference to be specific (new work).

7. **Line 293** — “Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me.”
   Says: Eliot's note gives Purgatorio V 133. La Pia, among those who died by violence and repented late, asks Dante to remember her when he is back in the world, and gives her birth and her death in one line, "Siena mi fé, disfecemi Maremma"; the next lines name, without naming, the husband who married her with his ring and had her killed (V 130–136). "Undid me" translates "disfecemi", and it is the same verb, disfare, that gives "undone" at line 63 ("disfatta", Inferno III 57): the poem's two undoings are both Dante's. The point of the stop: Dante's dead ask to be remembered; the girl from Highbury asks nothing and, at 305, expects "Nothing". Eliot's <i>Dante</i> (1929) quotes La Pia's lines and translates them, "Siena made me, Maremma unmade me". Love owns 292 (the seduction); method should pass here (section 3).
   Cite: Dante, Purgatorio V 130–136; Inferno III 57; Southam, Guide, note on line 293; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 293–295.

8. **Line 312** — “Phlebas the Phoenician, a fortnight dead”.
   Says: Eliot said in 1959 that the long draft of Part IV, a sea voyage ending in a wreck, was inspired by the Ulysses canto of the Inferno; there Ulysses' ship, in sight of the mountain of Purgatory, is spun three times by a whirlwind and the sea closes over it (Inferno XXVI 136–142), and "Entering the whirlpool" keeps that ending. Pound cut the voyage and would not let Phlebas go ("I DO advise keeping Phlebas", in his reply, late January 1922, to Eliot's suggestion of omitting him). The epitaph "Consider Phlebas" addresses the living from the side of the dead, as Dante's dead do. Grail owns Phlebas as Frazer's drowned god; this stop owns the wreck and the epitaph. Absorbs the retired Dead stop at 312.
   Cite: Paris Review interview (1959), described; Dante, Inferno XXVI 136–142; Facsimile (1971), the typescript of Part IV; Pound, Letters, ed. Paige, nos. 181–183 (the writer to name the letter); Letters, vol. 1, Eliot to Pound, late January 1922; Southam, Guide, note on Part IV; Ricks and McCue, commentary on the Part IV headnote.

9. **Line 328** — “He who was living is now dead / We who were living are now dying”.
   Says: here the poem's dead become "we". The crowd was "undone" by death at 63 and seen from outside; now the speaker counts himself among the dying, and speaks of the living in the past tense, as Dante's dead speak of their lives ("Siena mi fé"). Keep it short and mark it as the pathway's reading. Passion owns 322 (the Passion; "He who was living" as Christ); this stop owns line 329. Absorbs the retired Dead stop at 328.
   Cite: Southam, Guide, note on lines 322–330; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 328–330.

10. **Line 412** — “Dayadhvam: I have heard the key / Turn in the door once and turn once only”.
   Says: Eliot's note on line 412 gives Inferno XXXIII 46: Count Ugolino, shut in a tower at Pisa with his sons and grandsons, hears the door below "chiavar", nailed up or locked, and they starve. The thunder has said "sympathise", and the poem answers with Dante's most terrible image of a closed door, paired in the same note with Bradley's "circle closed on the outside". The stop tells the story briefly (the gloss card gives the word), and says what the pairing does: sympathy is asked for at the one place where none can reach. East owns the Sanskrit word and the fable; this stop owns Ugolino. Absorbs the retired Dead stop at 412.
   Cite: Dante, Inferno XXXIII 1–78 (the key at 46–47); Bradley, Appearance and Reality (the passage in Eliot's note); Southam, Guide, note on line 412; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 412–415; Grover Smith (1956).

11. **Line 428** — “Poi s’ascose nel foco che gli affina”.
   Says: Eliot's note on line 428 gives the four lines: Arnaut Daniel, in Provençal, asks Dante to remember his pain in due time, "then hid himself in the fire that refines them" (Purgatorio XXVI 145–148). Of the fragments shored at the end, this is the only one with a way out: the fire is purgatorial, entered willingly, and ends. Eliot had already taken "Ara Vos Prec" as the title of his London volume of 1920; in The Sacred Wood (1920) the line is the last of a list of episodes in which "the errors of Arnaut are corrected", and in <i>Dante</i> (1929) he quotes the whole passage, translates the last line "Then dived he back into that fire which refines them", and says the flame of purgatory differs from hell's in being "deliberately and consciously accepted by the penitent" (both before 1930, quotable). The dedication (line 0) is from the same canto. Passion should pass here and make its point about purging fire at 308 (section 3).
   Cite: Dante, Purgatorio XXVI 140–148; The Sacred Wood ("Dante"); Eliot, Dante (1929) (new work, section 5); Gallup (for Ara Vos Prec, 1920); Southam, Guide, note on line 428 (which also records that section III of Ash-Wednesday first appeared in Commerce, autumn 1929, as "Som de l'escalina"); Ricks and McCue, commentary on line 428.


### love — The loves that fail

**id:** love
**title:** The loves that fail
**reason:** Take this pathway for the poem's lovers and couples, from the hyacinth garden to the typist's divan, and for what Tiresias, Augustine and the thunder make of them.

**intro (draft):** Eliot’s note on line 218 says that in the poem “all the women are one woman, and the two sexes meet in Tiresias”, the blind seer who has been both. This pathway follows the poem’s couples and seductions in order: the hyacinth garden, where one of the two could not speak; the woman in the chair and the man who does not answer her; Lil and the demobbed Albert; the nymphs of Spenser’s wedding song and the summer nights that leave no addresses; the typist and the clerk, watched by Tiresias; Elizabeth and Leicester on the river; the three Thames-daughters; and, at the end of Part III, the Buddha and Augustine, whom Eliot’s note calls “two representatives of eastern and western asceticism”. Jessie Weston’s book, which Eliot’s headnote credits with the poem’s plan, makes the waste land a country whose king has lost his virility and whose fields fail with him. The pathway ends with the thunder’s three words and with the one love in the poem that is spoken of as possible, in the conditional, at line 421.

**Is it really in the poem?** Part III is called The Fire Sermon, and Eliot's note on line 309 (the note he numbered 312) says its ending sets two ascetics together on purpose; his note on 218 makes Tiresias, the man who has been a woman, the poem's witness and all the women one. Weston's thesis (From Ritual to Romance, ch. II) is that the king's "loss of virility" brings "a suspension of the reproductive processes of Nature": the land is waste because generation has failed. Brooks (1939) built his reading on that, and the couples are simply there in the text, one after another.

**Scholarship it rests on:**
1. Eliot's notes on lines 218 (Tiresias and the women), 307 and 309 (Augustine, the Fire Sermon, "asceticism"), 402 (Datta) and 408 (Webster's remarrying widow): the frame the poet himself gave the couples.
2. Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II (the king's virility and the land's fertility) and ch. IX (the Fisher King); Warren, Buddhism in Translations, §73 (the Fire Sermon: "the fire of passion... the fire of hatred... the fire of infatuation"); Augustine, Confessions, bk. III (the cauldron of unholy loves at Carthage) and bk. X ("Thou pluckest me out").
3. Brooks (1939): the poem read through Weston's sterility; Tiresias as the seer who in Oedipus Rex knew the plague on Thebes came from a sexual sin. Kenner (1959): the Tiresias note as an offer of "a nameable point of view". Grover Smith (1956): the hyacinth garden as a Grail scene, "a place of water and flowers", with the girl as Grail-bearer.
4. Facsimile (1971): Vivien Eliot's "wonderful" beside the nerves passage in the typescript of Part II; the cut line "The ivory men make company between us"; Eliot's remark that the pub passage was "pure Ellen Kellond" (p. 127). Southam, Guide, notes on lines 35, 49, 77–92, 99, 111, 137, 139, 176, 199–202, 218, 279, 292–306, 307, 402, 419; Ricks and McCue, commentary on the same lines; Rainey, Annotated Waste Land, note on line 214.
5. Biographical readings, used as readings and marked: Gordon, Eliot's Early Years and The Hyacinth Girl (Emily Hale and the garden; the marriage behind Part II).

**Stops (14):**

1. **Line 35** — “You gave me hyacinths first a year ago; / They called me the hyacinth girl.”
   Says: the poem's first love, and its failure. A girl remembers being given hyacinths; someone else remembers the return from the garden, arms full, hair wet, and could not speak, "neither / Living nor dead", knew nothing. The scene is framed by Wagner: before it the sailor's love-song from the mast of Tristan's ship (31–34), after it the shepherd's "empty the sea", Tristan dying while he waits for Isolde (42). Grover Smith reads the garden as the Grail scene of Weston's book, the girl as Grail-bearer and the man as the quester who fails to speak; Gordon reads the girl as Emily Hale; both are readings and are marked. Point to the hyacinth-girl and Tristan cards. Dante owns "Living nor dead" (40); seers owns "my eyes failed" (39).
   Cite: Wagner, Tristan und Isolde, Acts I and III; Grover Smith (1956); Gordon, The Hyacinth Girl; Southam, Guide, note on line 35; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 35–42. Absorbs the retired Women 35, Nothing 40 and Water 42.

2. **Line 49** — “Here is Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks, / The lady of situations.”
   Says: the second card dealt is a woman: "beautiful lady" and deadly nightshade, a poison and a cosmetic; "Lady of the Rocks" usually traced to Leonardo's Virgin of the Rocks (Southam); "lady of situations", a woman of intrigues. Eliot's note on 218 says all the women are one woman; the card is dealt before any of them appears, so the reader meets the type before the women. Grail owns the Tarot as Weston's symbols (43); this stop owns the woman on the card.
   Cite: Southam, Guide, note on line 49; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 49–50; Grover Smith (1956). Absorbs the retired Women 49.

3. **Line 77** — “The Chair she sat in, like a burnished throne”.
   Says: the room is built out of two queens' loves. The chair and the perfumed air are Cleopatra's barge in Enobarbus's speech (Eliot's note on 77: Antony and Cleopatra II.ii); the "laquearia" and the burning lamps are Dido's banquet for Aeneas (note on 92: Aeneid I 726), Dido who killed herself when he sailed. Both loves ended in death; the woman in the chair sits among their furniture with a man who will not speak. Method owns the setting of past beside present at 77; this stop owns what the two queens say about love. Point to the burnished-throne card.
   Cite: Shakespeare, Antony and Cleopatra II.ii; Virgil, Aeneid I 726 and bk. IV (Dido's death); Southam, Guide, notes on lines 77 and 92; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 77–96. Absorbs the retired Women 77.

4. **Line 99** — “The change of Philomel, by the barbarous king / So rudely forced”.
   Says: the picture over the mantel is a rape. In Ovid, Tereus, king of Thrace, rapes his wife's sister Philomela, cuts out her tongue, and is told by a weaving; the sisters are turned into birds. Her "inviolable voice" fills the desert and "still the world pursues", but what it hears is "Jug Jug" with "dirty ears". The first act of love the poem shows is this one; the nightingale returns at 203–206 ("Tereu", the rapist's name) and the swallow, her sister Procne, at 429. Point to the philomel card.
   Cite: Ovid, Metamorphoses VI (Tereus, Procne and Philomela); Southam, Guide, note on line 99; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 99–103 and 203–206. Absorbs the retired Women 99, Birds 99 and 203 (the swallow at 429 goes to method or wagner: section 3).

5. **Line 111** — “My nerves are bad tonight. Yes, bad. Stay with me.”
   Says: a marriage in which one speaks and the other does not answer aloud; her words are in quotation marks and his are not (the nerves card gives the usual reading). "Speak to me. Why do you never speak"; what he thinks of is rats' alley and "Those are pearls that were his eyes", and Eliot's note on 126 sends that line back to the hyacinth garden (37) and the drowned man (48): the failed love of Part I is what he remembers. In the typescript Vivien Eliot wrote "wonderful" beside the passage, and a line, "The ivory men make company between us", was cut at her request and restored by Eliot from memory in 1960 (Southam's note on 137). Gordon's reading of the passage as the Eliots' marriage is marked as hers.
   Cite: Facsimile (1971), the typescript of Part II; Southam, Guide, notes on lines 111 and 137; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 111–138; Gordon, Eliot's Early Years. Absorbs the retired Women 111, Nothing 120, 122 and 126, Water 125.

6. **Line 139** — “When Lil’s husband got demobbed, I said—”
   Says: a marriage told in a pub. Albert, four years in the army, "wants a good time"; Lil has had five children, "nearly died of young George", took pills "to bring it off", and at thirty-one has lost her teeth and her looks; "What you get married for if you dont want children?" is the poem's plainest statement of Weston's theme, a marriage in which generation has become the thing to be prevented. Eliot said the passage was "pure Ellen Kellond", the Eliots' maid, who told the story (Facsimile, p. 127). The pub's goodnights turn into Ophelia's (172), a girl driven mad by a lover and drowned. War owns the demob; this stop owns the marriage and the children.
   Cite: Facsimile (1971), p. 127; Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II; Southam, Guide, note on line 139; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 139–172; Shakespeare, Hamlet IV.v. Absorbs the retired Women 139 and 172, Water 172.

7. **Line 173** — “The river’s tent is broken: the last fingers of leaf / Clutch and sink into the wet bank.”
   Says: Eliot's note on 176 gives Spenser's Prothalamion, a "spousall verse" in which nymphs gather flowers on the Thames for two brides; the refrain, "Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song", is sung here over a river without litter because the summer is over and "The nymphs are departed" with "their friends, the loitering heirs of city directors", who "have left no addresses": a wedding song for affairs that end without a trace. Method owns 176 (the past set beside the present); this stop owns the contrast between marriage and the summer nights.
   Cite: Spenser, Prothalamion; Southam, Guide, note on line 176; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 173–184. Absorbs the retired Water 176 (the wedding poem; grail keeps the river).

8. **Line 199** — “O the moon shone bright on Mrs. Porter”.
   Says: the song is a brothel ballad which Eliot's note says was reported to him from Sydney; it has been identified as a song of the Australian troops (Southam). Sweeney is brought to Mrs Porter "in the spring" as Actaeon was brought to Diana bathing (Eliot's note on 196), the hunter who saw the goddess naked and was torn by his own hounds; then a line of French, the last of Verlaine's sonnet on Parsifal, the knight who resisted the Flower-maidens and Kundry and whose feet were washed before he entered the Grail hall (the verlaine card): Helen Gardner (1972) suggested that the washing of the feet is what leads from the soda water to Verlaine, a reading marked as hers. The poem's one chaste lover enters as a single line among the brothel song. Method owns 196 (Actaeon and Sweeney); wagner owns 202 (Verlaine's Parsifal); this stop owns Mrs Porter.
   Cite: Verlaine, "Parsifal"; Southam, Guide, notes on lines 199 and 202; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 196–202; Gardner (1972) (new work, section 5). Absorbs the retired Women 199.

9. **Line 215** — “At the violet hour, when the eyes and back / Turn upward from the desk”.
   Says: the poem's central scene of love, watched by the one who has been both sexes. The typist home at teatime, the clerk "with one bold stare", "Exploring hands encounter no defence", "makes a welcome of indifference", "Well now that's done: and I'm glad it's over". Eliot's note on 218 says that what Tiresias sees "is the substance of the poem": this is what he sees. Before it, Mr Eugenides's invitation to "a weekend at the Metropole" (209–214; Rainey's note gives the Brighton hotel) is another loveless proposition; after it, Goldsmith's Olivia sings that a lovely woman who stoops to folly can only die (Eliot's note on 253), and the typist puts on a record. Brooks (1939) noted the irony that Tiresias, who in Oedipus Rex knew that the plague on Thebes came from a sexual sin, here watches a scene in which nothing is felt as sin. Seers owns 218 (blindness and foresight); method owns 253 (Goldsmith); this stop owns the encounter.
   Cite: Southam, Guide, notes on lines 215–256; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 215–256; Brooks (1939); Rainey, Annotated Waste Land, note on line 214; Goldsmith, The Vicar of Wakefield, ch. 24. Absorbs the retired Women 222 and Eyes 232.

10. **Line 279** — “Elizabeth and Leicester / Beating oars”.
   Says: the queen and Lord Robert Dudley on a barge on the Thames in 1561, "talking nonsense" about marriage in the Spanish ambassador's hearing (Eliot's note quotes Froude's translation of De Quadra's letter). It was a courtship all Europe watched and it never became a marriage; the queen did not marry, and Dudley's wife had died the year before in circumstances that made the match impossible (the writer takes the facts from Froude and Southam). Between the Thames-daughters' two songs, a royal love that came to nothing beside the working river. Method owns 266 (the Rhine-daughters on the Thames) and the barge beside oil and tar; this stop owns the courtship.
   Cite: Froude, Reign of Elizabeth (vol. I, ch. iv, as Eliot's note gives it); Southam, Guide, note on line 279; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 279–289. Absorbs the retired Women 279.

11. **Line 292** — “Trams and dusty trees. / Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me.”
   Says: three girls speak in turn (Eliot's note on 266), each a seduction told in a few lines: Richmond, on the floor of a canoe; Moorgate, "After the event / He wept. He promised 'a new start.' / I made no comment. What should I resent?"; Margate, "I can connect / Nothing with nothing", "My people humble people who expect / Nothing". The poem's loves are here stripped of story and reproach. Wagner's Rhine-daughters, whose refrain they sing, lament a theft; these lament nothing. Dante owns La Pia (293); wagner and method own the Rhine-daughters (266); this stop owns the three stories.
   Cite: Wagner, Götterdämmerung III.i; Southam, Guide, notes on lines 292–306; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 292–306. Absorbs the retired Women 292, Nothing 302 and 305.

12. **Line 307** — “To Carthage then I came / Burning burning burning burning”.
   Says: the part called The Fire Sermon ends by setting two ascetics together: Augustine arriving at Carthage "where a cauldron of unholy loves sang all about mine ears" and praying "Thou pluckest me out" (Eliot's notes on 307 and 309; Confessions III and X), and the Buddha's sermon, in which all things are on fire "with the fire of passion... with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation" (Warren, §73). Eliot's note on line 309 says the collocation "as the culmination of this part of the poem, is not an accident". For this pathway it is the part's verdict on its loves: what burns in them is what the two ascetics renounced. Passion owns Augustine (307) and east owns the Buddha (308); this stop owns Eliot's word "asceticism" and its application to the couples of Part III.
   Cite: Augustine, Confessions, bk. III and bk. X; Warren, Buddhism in Translations, §73; Southam, Guide, headnote to Part III (the Fire Sermon "against the fires of lust, anger, envy") and note on line 307; Brooks (1939).

13. **Line 402** — “Datta: what have we given? / My friend, blood shaking my heart / The awful daring of a moment’s surrender”.
   Says: the thunder's first word is "give", and the only thing the poem says was given is a surrender, "Which an age of prudence can never retract / By this, and this only, we have existed". Critics have read the surrender back to the hyacinth garden, the one moment in the poem when a gift was made and the giver could not speak (the writer names the critic: section 5). What it is not recorded in follows: obituaries, memories, wills, with Webster's widow who will remarry before the worm has pierced the winding-sheet (Eliot's note on 408). East owns the Upanishad and the fable; this stop owns the surrender.
   Cite: Webster, The White Devil V.vi; Brooks (1939); Southam, Guide, note on line 402; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 402–410.

14. **Line 419** — “Damyata: The boat responded / Gaily, to the hand expert with sail and oar”.
   Says: the third word, "control", is answered first by a boat under a hand that knows what it is doing, and then by a heart that "would have responded / Gaily, when invited, beating obedient / To controlling hands". It is the poem's only love in the conditional, addressed to "you", and it comes last: after the couples who could not speak or would not answer, a heart that would have answered, had it been asked. The damyata card gives Eliot's sailing off Gloucester. East owns the word; this stop owns "would have".
   Cite: Southam, Guide, note on line 419; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 419–423. Absorbs the retired Water 419.


### seers — Prophets and seers

**id:** seers
**title:** Prophets and seers
**reason:** Take this pathway for the poem's prophets, fortune-tellers and blind seers, from the Sibyl in the epigraph to Isaiah's warning near the end, and for what each of them is allowed to see.

**intro (draft):** The poem begins with a prophetess who wants to die and ends with a prophet’s warning to a dying king, turned into a question. Between them come Ezekiel’s “Son of man” and a voice that promises to show fear in a handful of dust; a fortune-teller with a cold whose cards come true; the blind Tiresias, of whom Eliot’s note says that what he sees “is the substance of the poem”; the disciples on the road to Emmaus, whose eyes “were holden”; and the seer of Hermann Hesse’s sentence in Eliot’s note on line 367, who hears the songs of a Europe going to ruin “with tears”. This pathway follows prophecy and sight through the poem, and sets the eyes that fail (in the hyacinth garden, on London Bridge, on the road to Emmaus) beside the seers who see.

**Is it really in the poem?** Eliot's note on 218 calls Tiresias "the most important personage in the poem" and what he sees its "substance"; his note on 46 says the fortune-teller's cards "appear later" and that "Death by Water is executed in Part IV"; his note on 20 names Ezekiel and his note on 367 quotes a sentence about "der Heilige und Seher"; the epigraph is a prophetess. Kenner (1959) and Brooks (1939) both take the Tiresias note as the key to the poem's point of view. The pathway's one addition is to read the poem's failures of sight (39, 65, 138, 360) against its seers, and each of those lines has its own source in Eliot's notes or the library.

**Scholarship it rests on:**
1. Eliot's notes on lines 20, 46, 126, 138, 218, 360 and 367, and the epigraph (Petronius, Satyricon 48).
2. Ovid, Metamorphoses III 316–338 (Tiresias, in Eliot's note) and XIV 130–153 (the Sibyl's bargain with Apollo); Homer, Odyssey X–XI (Tiresias keeps his understanding among the dead); Sophocles' Oedipus, as Brooks (1939) uses it.
3. King James Bible: Ezekiel 2:1–3 and 6:4–6; Ecclesiastes 12; Luke 24:13–31; Isaiah 38:1–8 and 2 Kings 20:1–11.
4. Kenner (1959): the note on Tiresias "offers to supply the poem with a nameable point of view"; Brooks (1939): the irony that Tiresias, who in Oedipus Rex saw the cause of the plague on Thebes, is made the commentator. Southam, Guide, and Ricks and McCue, commentary, on the epigraph and on lines 19–30, 43–59, 65, 138, 218, 360–366, 367–385, 400–402, 426.
5. Eliot, "Thomas Middleton" (1927), on the chess scene ("I've given thee blind mate twice"; "The irony could not be improved upon"); Hesse, Blick ins Chaos; Shackleton, South; Huxley, Crome Yellow; the Vagrancy Act 1824.

**Stops (12):**

1. **Line 0** — the epigraph: “Nam Sibyllam quidem Cumis ego ipse oculis meis vidi”.
   Says: the first seer in the poem is a prophetess who wants only to die. Trimalchio says he saw her "with my own eyes" hanging in a jar at Cumae; in Ovid the Sibyl tells Aeneas that Apollo gave her as many years as the grains of sand in her hand and that she forgot to ask for youth (Metamorphoses XIV 130–153); in Virgil she is the prophetess who leads Aeneas among the dead (Aeneid VI). The boys who ask her what she wants get the one prophecy she has left, "I want to die". Point to the epigraph card. Grail owns the title and headnote at line 0; dante owns the dedication.
   Cite: Petronius, Satyricon 48; Ovid, Metamorphoses XIV 130–153; Virgil, Aeneid VI; Southam, Guide, note on the epigraph; Ricks and McCue, commentary on the epigraph. Absorbs the retired Eyes 0 and Women 0 (Dead 0 with dante).

2. **Line 19** — “What are the roots that clutch, what branches grow / Out of this stony rubbish? Son of man,”.
   Says: the voice changes to a prophet's. "Son of man" is God's address to Ezekiel (Eliot's note on 20: Ezekiel 2:1), the prophet of visions, sent to "a rebellious nation"; "the cricket no relief" is the Preacher's chapter on old age and death (note on 23: Ecclesiastes 12:5); the "heap of broken images" has been read with Ezekiel's "your images shall be broken" (6:4–6; the writer checks Southam and Ricks and McCue for the reading). The point of the stop: the poem's own voice takes the prophet's office, "I will show you", and what it shows is not a vision but "fear in a handful of dust". Grail owns the dry land at 19; passion owns Ezekiel and Isaiah as the poem's scripture (20, 25); this stop owns the mode of speech.
   Cite: King James Bible, Ezekiel 2:1–3 and 6:4–6; Ecclesiastes 12:1–7; Southam, Guide, notes on lines 20–30; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 19–30. Absorbs the retired Dead 23 and Birds 23 (the cricket), with passion.

3. **Line 39** — “Speak, and my eyes failed, I was neither”.
   Says: the first eyes in the poem after the Sibyl's fail, and they fail before light, not darkness: "Looking into the heart of light, the silence". Then Wagner's shepherd, set to watch the sea for Isolde's ship, reports it "desolate and empty" (42; the oed-und-leer card). Two failures of sight in four lines: one who looks and cannot see, one who looks and there is nothing to see. The stop points forward to Emmaus (360), where eyes are "holden". Love owns the garden (35); dante owns "Living nor dead" (40).
   Cite: Wagner, Tristan und Isolde, Act III; Southam, Guide, note on line 42; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 39–42. Absorbs the retired Eyes 39.

4. **Line 43** — “Madame Sosostris, famous clairvoyante”.
   Says: a professional seer with a bad cold and "a wicked pack of cards", "the wisest woman in Europe" (from Huxley's Crome Yellow, as the sosostris card says), careful of the law ("One must be so careful these days": telling fortunes for money could be prosecuted under the Vagrancy Act). Her reading comes true: Eliot's note on 46 says the Sailor and the Merchant "appear later", so do the crowds, and "Death by Water is executed in Part IV"; the card she does not find, the Hanged Man, is by the same note the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus; and there is a card she is "forbidden to see" (54). The stop: the poem's fortune-teller is a fraud whose every prophecy the poem fulfils, with one blank in her sight. Grail owns the Tarot as Weston's symbols; this stop owns the seer and the fulfilment.
   Cite: Huxley, Crome Yellow; Vagrancy Act 1824; Southam, Guide, notes on lines 43–59; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 43–59; Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. VI (pointer only). Absorbs the retired Women 43 and Eyes 52.

5. **Line 48** — “(Those are pearls that were his eyes. Look!)”.
   Says: the one command to see in Part I. The words are Ariel's, sung to Ferdinand of a father drowned, whose eyes have become pearls, "something rich and strange" (The Tempest I.ii; the pearls card): the drowned man's eyes are what the sea makes of sight. The line returns at 125 as the only thing the silent man in Part II can say he remembers, and Eliot's note on 126 sends it back here and to the garden (37).
   Cite: Shakespeare, The Tempest I.ii; Southam, Guide, notes on lines 48 and 125; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 48 and 125. Absorbs the retired Eyes 48 and Water 47 (Water 125 with love).

6. **Line 65** — “And each man fixed his eyes before his feet.”
   Says: the crowd that Dante's lines have just called dead does not look up; Eliot's note on 64 gives Limbo's sighs (Inferno IV 25–27) for the sighs of the line before. The stop sets the downcast eyes against the "Look!" of 48 and against the one who sees the crowd for what it is ("I had not thought"): the seen do not see. Helen Williams (1968) read the line as the crowd avoiding sight (a reading, marked as hers; not in works.txt, section 5). Dante owns 62; war and cities own 60.
   Cite: Dante, Inferno IV 25–27; Southam, Guide, note on line 65; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 64–65. Absorbs the retired Eyes 65.

7. **Line 138** — “Pressing lidless eyes and waiting for a knock upon the door.”
   Says: Eliot's note gives the chess game in Middleton's Women Beware Women (II.ii): Livia keeps a widow at chess while, above, the Duke seduces the widow's daughter-in-law Bianca, and the moves are called with double meanings. Eliot in 1927 quoted the exchange, "I've given thee blind mate twice" among it, and wrote "The irony could not be improved upon". The watcher at the board sees nothing of what matters, and "lidless" eyes cannot close. In the same room a Cupidon "hid his eyes behind his wing" (81). Love owns the couple (111); this stop owns the watching.
   Cite: Middleton, Women Beware Women II.ii; Eliot, "Thomas Middleton" (1927); Southam, Guide, note on line 138; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 137–138. Absorbs the retired Eyes 81 and 138.

8. **Line 218** — “I Tiresias, though blind, throbbing between two lives,”.
   Says: the poem's seer proper. In Ovid (Eliot's note quotes the passage) Juno blinded him for judging that women have the greater pleasure in love and Jupiter gave him foresight in compensation; in Sophocles he told Oedipus the cause of the plague on Thebes, which the king with eyes could not see, and Brooks (1939) draws the irony of making him the commentator here; in Homer he alone keeps his understanding among the dead (dante owns 245). "Perceived the scene, and foretold the rest" (229): his prophecy is of what has always happened, "foresuffered all" (243). Eliot's note calls him "a mere spectator" and "the most important personage in the poem", and Kenner (1959) read the note as the offer of "a nameable point of view" for a poem that otherwise has none. Love owns the typist's scene (215).
   Cite: Ovid, Metamorphoses III 316–338; Brooks (1939); Kenner (1959); Southam, Guide, note on line 218; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 218–219, 228–229 and 243–246. Absorbs the retired Eyes 215 and 218.

9. **Line 360** — “Who is the third who walks always beside you?”
   Says: Eliot's note gives an Antarctic party's delusion of one more member than could be counted (Shackleton's, he thinks; the third card gives the passage of South), and his headnote to Part V gives the journey to Emmaus, where two disciples walk with the risen Christ and "their eyes were holden that they should not know him" (Luke 24:16) until, at supper, "their eyes were opened, and they knew him; and he vanished out of their sight" (24:31). The stop: counting and looking give different answers ("When I count... But when I look"); the figure is hooded and its sex unknown; and by Eliot's note on 46 it is the Hanged Man the fortune-teller could not find. Sight fails here as it failed in the garden. Passion owns Emmaus as resurrection; this stop owns the holden eyes.
   Cite: King James Bible, Luke 24:13–31; Shackleton, South; Southam, Guide, note on line 360; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 360–366. Absorbs the retired Eyes 360.

10. **Line 367** — “What is that sound high in the air / Murmur of maternal lamentation”.
   Says: Eliot's note on 367 quotes Hesse: half of Europe is on the way to chaos, singing drunkenly as Dmitri Karamazov sang, and "der Heilige und Seher hört sie mit Tränen", the saint and the seer hear these songs with tears. Lines 367–385 are what such a seer hears and sees, cast first as questions ("What is that sound", "Who are those hooded hordes", "What is the city") and then as a vision without question marks: the woman fiddling on her hair, bats with baby faces, towers upside down, voices out of empty cisterns (the bats card gives the earlier fragment). War owns the decay of eastern Europe; passion owns the lamenting women (Luke 23:27); this stop owns the seer of Hesse's sentence and the visionary mode.
   Cite: Hesse, Blick ins Chaos; Southam, Guide, note on lines 367–377; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 367–385. Absorbs the retired Women 368 and 378 and Birds 380.

11. **Line 400** — “Then spoke the thunder / DA”.
   Says: the last voice that speaks with authority is not a person but thunder, and it speaks one syllable. In the Upanishad (the da card gives the fable) Prajapati says "Da" to gods, men and demons in turn, and each hears in it the word it needs, control, give, be compassionate; the thunder, the divine voice, repeats "da, da, da". The stop: an oracle whose meaning is in the hearer, and the poem takes all three. East owns the text, Deussen and the order of the three words; this stop owns the thunder as a prophecy that has to be interpreted.
   Cite: Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad V.2; Southam, Guide, note on line 402; Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 400–402.

12. **Line 426** — “Shall I at least set my lands in order?”
   Says: the last prophet's words in the poem are Isaiah's to Hezekiah, "Set thine house in order: for thou shalt die, and not live" (Isaiah 38:1; 2 Kings 20:1), a prophet's warning to a sick king; the fisher on the shore (grail owns 424) turns it into a question. In Isaiah the king prays, is given fifteen years and a sign; the poem gives the question and then London Bridge falling. Passion should pass here (section 3).
   Cite: King James Bible, Isaiah 38:1–8; 2 Kings 20:1–11; Southam, Guide, note on line 426; Ricks and McCue, commentary on line 426.

## 5. Unsettled

Things I could not check today or would want a second opinion on.

1. **dante 40 ("Living nor dead" beside Inferno III 64).** The comparison is made by Craig Raine (2006) and Ann P. Brady (1978), neither in works.txt, and by no one in the standard commentaries that I could find. Editor to decide whether the stop stays; without it dante still has ten stops.
2. **dante 245 and Inferno XX.** That Dante puts Tiresias among the diviners (Inferno XX 40–45) is a fact about Dante; I found no commentator on The Waste Land who brings it in. The stop presents it as Dante's Tiresias, not Eliot's; drop the sentence if that seems too much.
3. **dante 293: "disfecemi" and "disfatta".** That both come from disfare is a fact of the Italian, not a reading in the commentaries; the writer should state it as the former.
4. **dante 328** is the thinnest stop; it could go, leaving dante with ten.
5. **dante 312: Pound's letter.** The reply "I DO advise keeping Phlebas" is quoted in Southam (note on Part IV), the facsimile and Litz (1972); the writer should give its number in Paige (the exchange is nos. 181–183) and its date (late January 1922) from the volume itself.
6. **love 199: the foot-washing.** The link from the soda water to Parsifal's washed feet is Helen Gardner's, in her 1972 Adamson lecture (not in works.txt). The retired Women stop says the ballad "has been identified as a song of the Australian troops"; the writer should find who identified it (Southam's note on 199 is the place to look).
7. **love 215: the Metropole.** Rainey's note on 214 gives the Brighton hotel; the reading of the invitation as a homosexual proposition is in the scholarship (Cooper 1987; Wilks) but I did not confirm the wording in Southam or Rainey. Cite whichever has it, or say "usually read as a proposition".
8. **love 402: who ties Datta to the hyacinth garden.** The reading is in the Cox and Hinchliffe casebook (1968) in an essay I could not identify from the snippet ("regrets again — the girl in the hyacinth-garden — 'the awful daring of a moment's surrender'"), and in Schwarz (1988). The writer identifies the critic from the casebook, or rests on Brooks (1939) alone.
9. **love 215 and seers 218: Brooks on Oedipus.** The sentence "The fact that Tiresias is made the commentator serves a further irony. In Oedipus Rex, it is Tiresias who recognizes..." appears in five collections that reprint Brooks's essay; I did not see it in Modern Poetry and the Tradition itself. Likewise Brooks's "the Fisher King, who has been rendered impotent" (via the Norton Critical Edition, p. 186). The writer confirms in the book.
10. **love 111: Vivien's annotation.** Litz (1972) quotes it as "Yes & wonderful wonderful"; the writer gives the exact words and the facsimile page.
11. **seers 19: Ezekiel 6:4–6** for "broken images" needs a commentary behind it (Southam's or Ricks and McCue's note on 22) or it is dropped.
12. **seers 65:** the reading of the downcast eyes as avoidance is Helen Williams's (1968), not in works.txt; the stop can rest on the poem's own contrast with "Look!" and pass over her.
13. **seers 400:** "da, da, da" as the thunder's repetition is in the Upanishad (V.2.3); the writer verifies the wording in Müller or Deussen.
14. **Heads.** The love intro is long and lists the couples; the dante intro ends on a list of three things Dante's dead do. The editor may cut either.
15. **Eliot's 1929 Dante and 1927 Middleton essay** are before 1930 and quotable; the quotations above are from open scans (archive.org, Digital Library of India), and the writers should take them from a printed Selected Essays if one is at hand.
