## The designer's section

### wagner

**Title:** Wagner.

**Reason:** The three passages of Wagner the poem quotes and the opera it names, with the drowned king of the Starnbergersee.

**Intro (draft):** Wagner is quoted in the poem three times, in German: the sailor’s song from the masthead that opens <i>Tristan und Isolde</i> (lines 31–34), the shepherd’s report in its third act that the sea is empty (42), and the Rhine-daughters’ refrain from <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, twice, on the Thames (277–278, 290–291). A fourth opera, <i>Parsifal</i>, Wagner’s Grail opera, is present through the last line of Verlaine’s sonnet on it (202); and Jessie Weston’s book, which Eliot’s headnote credits with the poem’s plan, records in its preface the discussions at the Bayreuth Festival of 1911 that gave her some of her evidence, and ends by saying that the Grail story woke to new life in the nineteenth century “through the genius of Tennyson and Wagner.” This pathway takes the Wagner in order and says what each opera is doing at its place in the poem. It begins at the Starnbergersee, where Wagner’s patron Ludwig II of Bavaria was found drowned in 1886, and ends with the Fisher King of line 424 beside Wagner’s wounded Grail king Amfortas; both are readings, Herbert Knust’s (1967), and the stops mark them as such.

**Scholarship it rests on:**
- Eliot's notes on lines 31, 42, 202 and 266.
- Wagner, Tristan und Isolde (1865), Act I scene 1 (the sailor's song) and Act III scene 1 (the shepherd, "Öd und leer das Meer"); Götterdämmerung (1876), Act III scene 1 (the three Rhine-daughters and their refrain); Parsifal (1882), Act III (Kundry washes Parsifal's feet; Amfortas healed by the spear; the boys' voices from the dome in the Grail scene). The library has tristan and gotterdammerung; the cards "The sailor's song from the masthead", "The shepherd's answer", "The children's voices in the dome" and "The Rhine-daughters on the Thames" give the scenes.
- Verlaine, "Parsifal" (1886; library verlaine-parsifal). Weston 1920, preface (the Bayreuth Festival of 1911, Professor von Schroeder) and the last sentence of chapter XIV ("through the genius of Tennyson and Wagner"), both checked in the Gutenberg text; chapter IX for the Fisher King.
- Herbert Knust, Wagner, the King, and "The Waste Land" (Pennsylvania State University Studies, 1967): Ludwig II, the Starnbergersee and the Wagnerian reading of the poem's kings (verified to exist; not read: see Unsettled). Stoddard Martin, Wagner to "The Waste Land" (1982). Ricks and McCue, commentary on 8, 31–34, 42, 202, 266–278, 306, 424; Southam on 8, 31, 42, 202, 266; Crawford 2015 for Eliot's early hearing of Tristan and for Jean Verdenal's letters of 1912 on Wagner (The Letters of T. S. Eliot, vol. 1; Verdenal's letters are not Eliot's and may be quoted once checked).

## 6. Unsettled

Things I could not check from here, or would want the editor's decision on, in rough order of importance.

1. **The number of Eliot's note on the collocation.** eliot-notes.txt prints "312. From St. Augustine's Confessions again. The collocation of these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism..." In the 1922 Boni and Liveright book and the standard editions the note is numbered 309 (the line "O Lord Thou pluckest me out"), and the brief itself calls it "Eliot's note on 309". Either the site's file has a slip or the Notes lens attaches it to 312 on purpose. I have written "the note on the collocation" in the stops and left the number to the editor; passion 307 and east 308 both need it.
2. **Cleanth Brooks (1939).** Several stops rest on readings I attribute to Brooks from memory of the essay: the Syrian merchant (grail 209), the foot-washing and Parsifal (wagner 202), "Fear death by water" as the death that brings life (grail 55), Elizabeth and Leicester as no better than the present (method 279), the empty chapel as a terror of nothing (grail 386), the poem's people as neither living nor dead (dante 39). I could not reach the essay's text online (Modern Poetry and the Tradition, 1939; reprinted in Jay Martin's collection, 1968). The writers must verify each before attributing it, and re-attribute to Grover Smith 1956 or Southam where Brooks turns out not to say it.
3. **Two stops at one line.** The design has two pathways at lines 0 (three), 43, 209, 360, 412, 424 and 434. If the guide software cannot show two pathways' stops at the same line, the fallbacks are: seers 43 to 46; love 209 to 207; seers 360 to 366; dante 412 stays and east's Bradley moves into its 402 note; wagner 424 to 425; passion 434 folded into 426.
4. **Metamorphosis as a tenth pathway.** It is the one addition to the candidate set. Its case: the poem's transformations (Philomel, Tiresias, the pearls, Phlebas's bones, the swallow) are a strand the commentaries follow, and without it the drowned matter of the retired Water pathway has nowhere to go but grail, which is already thirteen stops. Its risk: it is the most reading-led of the ten, and it shares lines with love, seers and grail. If the editor wants nine, fold 48, 125, 191 and 315 into grail and 99, 203, 243 and 429 into love, and drop 257.
5. **Wagner at eight stops.** It meets the minimum only by taking 8 (Ludwig) and 306 ("la la"). Knust 1967 is verified to exist but I have not read it; Southam's note on line 8 should be checked for Ludwig before the stop is written. If the editor prefers, wagner folds into grail (202, 424) and love (31, 42) and the Rhine-daughters stay with the Part III listen item.
6. **Checks for particular stops.** dante 69: whether Southam or Ricks and McCue cite Inferno III 58–59 for "There I saw one I knew". seers 39: what Ricks and McCue say of "the heart of light". war 12: whether anyone reads the nationality line as post-war. war 231: the identification of the Bradford millionaire with a customer of Lloyds (Sir James Roberts is proposed on the web; find it in Ricks and McCue or Hollis or leave it out). passion 263: Eliot's "London Letter" in The Dial for June 1921 on the City churches. war 369: where Hesse's essay was first translated (Sydney Schiff, as Stephen Hudson, 1922; The Dial or The Criterion). wagner intro: the date Eliot first heard Tristan (Crawford) and Verdenal's letters on Wagner (Letters, vol. 1, 1912). east: the report that Eliot considered becoming a Buddhist about the time of the poem (Stephen Spender's memoir; cite through Ricks and McCue or Rainey, and describe).
7. **Verified here, for the writers' comfort.** Weston's chapter numbers and titles are from the Gutenberg text (#4090): III "The Freeing of the Waters", IV "Tammuz and Adonis", VI "The Symbols", IX "The Fisher King", XII "Mithra and Attis" (the Syrian merchants), XIII "The Perilous Chapel"; the site's library and glosses say "Chapel Perilous" and Eliot's headnote does too, but Weston's chapter is "The Perilous Chapel". Frazer's abridged Golden Bough (Gutenberg #3623): XXXII "The Ritual of Adonis" and XXXIII "The Gardens of Adonis", quoted above; "The Hanged God" is a chapter of the third edition's Adonis, Attis, Osiris (Attis part), not of the abridgement. Warren's Fire Sermon is section 73 of Buddhism in Translations, from the Mahā-Vagga, and the wording "All things, O priests, are on fire" and "With the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation" is checked against the archive.org text. The 1923 Dial sentences are checked against Wikisource. Weston's preface records her discussions with Professor von Schroeder at the Bayreuth Festival of 1911, and her last sentence names "the genius of Tennyson and Wagner" as the legend's revivers.
8. **Titles and order.** Alternatives are listed under each pathway; the chooser's order is a proposal. The reasons are one sentence each and the intros one paragraph, as the standard asks, but all are drafts and the writers will have the last word on tone.
9. **Second way-in items.** Five pathways have no way in. If the lists can take two path items, I suggest Part I "Dante's crowd on the bridge" (dante), Part II "Albert comes home" (war), Part III "The sermon over the section" (east), Part III or I "The seer with a cold" (seers), Part I "Tristan on either side of the garden" (wagner); I can draft bodies on request.
