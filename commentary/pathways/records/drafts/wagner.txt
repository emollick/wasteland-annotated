title: Wagner
intro: The Wagner in the poem, opera by opera. He is quoted three times, in German: the sailor’s song that opens <i>Tristan und Isolde</i> (lines 31–34), the shepherd’s report from its last act that the sea is empty (42), and the Rhine-daughters’ cry from the last act of <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, set twice on the Thames (277–278, 290–291) and worn down to “la la” at 306. A fourth opera, <i>Parsifal</i>, Wagner’s Grail opera, comes in at line 202 through Verlaine’s sonnet on it. Jessie Weston’s book on the Grail acknowledges in its preface talks at the Bayreuth Festival of 1911 that gave her evidence, and its argument ends by naming Tennyson and Wagner as the legend’s revivers. Jean Verdenal, Eliot’s friend in Paris, wrote to him in 1912 that <i>Tristan</i> at a first hearing “leaves you prostrate with ecstasy.” The pathway begins at the lake where Wagner’s patron drowned and ends with the Fisher King beside Wagner’s wounded Amfortas.
why: What Wagner’s operas are doing in the poem, quotation by quotation, and where to hear the music.
sources: Weston, From Ritual to Romance, preface
Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. XIII
Letters, vol. 1, Jean Verdenal to Eliot, 5 February 1912

line: 8
text: The lake in which Ludwig II of Bavaria, Wagner's patron, drowned on 13 June 1886, after being put under restraint as mad. He had brought Wagner to Munich in 1864; <i>Tristan und Isolde</i>, quoted at line 31, was first performed there the next year. Countess Marie Larisch, whose memoir is behind lines 8–18, counted herself one of his family and tells of his death. Herbert Knust (1967) argued that her book, with its drowned king, shapes much of the poem; Southam's <i>Guide</i> records the argument.

line: 31
text: A song of setting out, in another language, cut in straight after "fear in a handful of dust". In the opera the young sailor sings it unseen, from the mast, as the ship carries Isolde to her marriage with the King of Cornwall; Isolde, below, an Irish princess, takes it as mockery of her. <i>Tristan</i> ends with both lovers dead, and the poem sets its first words just before the hyacinth garden. The song, in the 1928 Bayreuth recording, is in the <a href="listen.html#rec-tristan-1928-bayreuth-act1-sailor">listening room</a>.

text: The shepherd's answer to Kurwenal's question whether any ship is yet on the sea. With it the two quotations from <i>Tristan</i> stand on either side of the hyacinth garden, a song of setting out before it and an empty sea after. Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the two together, the empty sea seeming at first "simply an extension of the last figure", the lines just before it. The love pathway takes the couple; the exchange itself, from the same 1928 Bayreuth set, is in the <a href="listen.html#rec-tristan-1928-bayreuth-act3-shepherd">listening room</a>.

line: 202
text: Verlaine's sonnet is on Wagner's <i>Parsifal</i>, the Grail legend as the poem's first readers knew it from the stage, with its healed king and its boys' voices in the dome. In the last act the penitent Kundry washes Parsifal's feet before he is led in to heal the king. Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the soda water of line 201 as reminding the poet "ironically of another sort of foot-washing". So Wagner's Grail hall follows straight on a soldiers' song, which the war pathway has.

line: 266
text: Eliot's note names the scene that opens the last act of <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, where the three Rhine-daughters swim in a ring and sing of the gold they once kept. The river's depths were bright while it lay there, and dark since it was stolen. That is what the parallel brings to the Thames, a river with nothing bright in it, sweating "Oil and tar". They ask Siegfried for the ring made from the gold, in vain. The refrain itself comes at 277.
sources: Wagner, Götterdämmerung, Act III scene 1

path: wagner
line: 277
text: The refrain itself, the Rhine-daughters' cry set as the poem's own line. Of the poem's three passages from Wagner it is the one with nothing to translate, since the syllables are sound, not words, the voice Wagner gave the river; the poem uses them twice as a refrain before its three daughters speak in turn. Hear the three sing it, in the 1964 Solti recording in the <a href="listen.html#rec-gotterdammerung-solti-1964-rhinemaidens">listening room</a>, and then read lines 266 to 291 aloud.
sources: Wagner, Götterdämmerung, Act III scene 1

text: After the three daughters have spoken in turn, the last of them on Margate Sands, the refrain does not come back whole; two syllables stand for it, the last two of "leialala". Nothing of Wagner follows, and what is left of the Rhine-daughters' cry after Margate is "la la". The love pathway takes the three speeches.

line: 424
text: Eliot's note points to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King, the maimed king of the Grail castle; the grail pathway gives him. Set beside him the king Eliot's generation knew from the stage, Wagner's Amfortas in <i>Parsifal</i>, wounded by the holy spear and healed in the third act, the act of Verlaine's sonnet at 202, when Parsifal touches the wound with it. Wagner's king is healed; the poem's is left fishing, the plain arid behind him. Weston's own argument ends by naming Tennyson and Wagner as the legend's revivers.
