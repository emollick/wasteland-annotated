title: The Bible and the Prayer Book
intro: The Bible and the Book of Common Prayer in the poem. Part I is named after the Prayer Book’s service for a burial, and the desert of lines 19 to 30 speaks in words from Ezekiel, Ecclesiastes and Isaiah. Part V opens, as Cleanth Brooks set out in 1939, with the Passion told without its names, and goes on to the road to Emmaus, where the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples. Between them the poem passes an exile’s psalm at line 182, a City church at 264 and Augustine’s Carthage at 307, and Eliot’s last note glosses “Shantih” with the blessing that ends the Communion service. Eliot was baptised into the Church of England in June 1927; this pathway does not read the poem as a conversion. It follows what the poem does with the scriptures it quotes, and says at each stop what a passage takes from them and what it leaves out.
why: What the poem takes from the Bible, the Prayer Book and the Church, from the burial service that names Part I, through the prophets, the Passion and the road to Emmaus, to the blessing Eliot’s last note puts beside “Shantih”.
sources: Book of Common Prayer, the Order for the Burial of the Dead

text: Part I takes its title from the Prayer Book's service for the dead, "The Order for the Burial of the Dead", which begins with Christ's words "I am the resurrection and the life" and commits the body to the ground "earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust; in sure and certain hope of the Resurrection to eternal life". The title puts the part's dead, the crowd on London Bridge, the corpse in the garden, under a service that promises resurrection. The part buries them and raises none.

text: Eliot's note sends "Son of man" to Ezekiel 2:1, where God sets the prophet on his feet to speak to "a rebellious nation"; his note on line 23 sends the cricket to Ecclesiastes 12, the chapter on old age and the grave. The desert speaks in the prophets' words, but this son of man is told he "cannot say, or guess", and the "heap of broken images" is what Ezekiel's God threatened, "your images shall be broken" (6:4), already done. line: 25
text: Isaiah 32:2 promises a king in whose reign a man shall be "as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land"; Helen Gardner (1949) set the verse beside this rock, and Southam's note gives it. What the shadow shows here is "fear in a handful of dust", and the dust is scripture's and the burial service's: "dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return" (Genesis 3:19, and Ecclesiastes 12:7), "dust to dust". The shelter the prophet promised is where death is shown.

text: A church keeps the hours over Dante's crowd of the dead; the card at this line gives the building. Southam's note on line 68 records the reading of the "final stroke of nine" as the ninth hour at which Christ died (Matthew 27:46, Mark 15:34), and notes that the Gospels' ninth hour is the ninth of daylight, about three in the afternoon, not nine in the morning. Eliot's note says only "A phenomenon which I have often noticed". The reading is some readers', not the note's.

line: 182
text: Psalm 137 in the Prayer Book's wording: the exiles in Babylon hang up their harps when their captors ask for "one of the songs of Sion", and answer "How shall we sing the Lord's song: in a strange land?" The poem's singer, set down between two lines of Spenser's wedding-song for the Thames, is that exile, and his song is cut short: "I speak not loud or long". The Prayer Book's psalms are Miles Coverdale's, of the 1530s; the pathway meets him again at line 264.

line: 259
text: St Magnus the Martyr was among nineteen City churches proposed for demolition. In his London Letter to <i>The Dial</i> of May 1921 Eliot wrote that the City churches give the business quarter "a beauty which its hideous banks and commercial houses have not quite defaced", and his note on line 264 cites the County Council's report on the scheme. The report records the tablet to Miles Coverdale, once rector here and reburied here, whose psalms the Prayer Book keeps. The poem's one church interior holds a splendour it calls "Inexplicable".

line: 307
text: Augustine's <i>Confessions</i>, Book III, in Pusey's translation, which Eliot's note quotes. At line 309, Book X, from the chapter on the temptation of the eyes: Augustine, still entangling his steps "with these outward beauties", says "but Thou pluckest me out, O Lord, Thou pluckest me out". The convert's memory of his lust is set beside the Buddha's sermon on fire, a pairing Eliot's note on line 309 calls "eastern and western asceticism" and "not an accident". "O Lord Thou pluckest me out" are the poem's only words addressed to God.

line: 322
text: The Passion without its names. The torchlight is the arrest by night, when Judas comes to the garden "with lanterns and torches and weapons" (John 18:3); "the frosty silence in the gardens" is Gethsemane, where "being in an agony he prayed more earnestly" (Luke 22:44); then the death, "He who was living is now dead". Cleanth Brooks (1939) wrote that the gardens' frosty silence "associates Christ in Gethsemane with the other hanged gods". The resurrection is not told, and the "we" who speak "are now dying".

line: 360
text: Luke 24: on the day of the resurrection two disciples walking to Emmaus are joined by a stranger they do not know, until he breaks bread and vanishes. Eliot's headnote to the notes on Part V gives "the journey to Emmaus" as the section's first theme, and his note on line 46 associates the Hanged Man with "the hooded figure". The risen Christ is in the poem only as an uncounted companion, "wrapt in a brown mantle, hooded", whose sex the speaker cannot tell, and the question is never answered.

line: 367
text: Hugh Kenner (1959) heard in the "Murmur of maternal lamentation" the women who followed Christ to the cross, "Daughters of Jerusalem, weep not for me, but weep for yourselves, and for your children" (Luke 23:28); Jerusalem is the first of the five cities named at line 375. Eliot's note on lines 367–377 gives something else, Hermann Hesse on eastern Europe singing drunkenly on its way to chaos. The lamentation is heard "high in the air", with no mourner in sight, as the Passion was told without its names.

line: 391
text: Ezekiel 37: the prophet is set down in a valley full of bones and asked "Son of man, can these bones live?"; when he prophesies to them they live and stand up, "an exceeding great army". Here no one prophesies, and the bones can neither live nor harm. Then the cock. Cleanth Brooks (1939) read it as the bird of folk-lore "whose voice chases away the powers of evil"; in <i>Hamlet</i> I.i the ghost "faded on the crowing of the cock". Its crow is followed by lightning and rain.

line: 426
text: Isaiah 38:1: King Hezekiah, "sick unto death", is told by the prophet, "Set thine house in order: for thou shalt die, and not live"; he turns his face to the wall, prays and weeps, and is given fifteen more years. The card at line 424 names him beside Weston's Fisher King, and Hugh Kenner (1959) and Southam's note record the verse. The poem's king takes the sentence without the reprieve and makes it smaller, "at least", "my lands", a question where Isaiah gave a command.

line: 434
text: Eliot's note translates the word with "The Peace which passeth understanding", the words of the blessing that ends the Prayer Book's Communion service, "The peace of God, which passeth all understanding, keep your hearts and minds", from Philippians 4:7. The poem's last word is glossed by its author with the Church's blessing. In 1922 the note called this "a feeble translation of the content of this word"; the words stood in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>, and when that book was reset in 1932 they became "is our equivalent to this word".
