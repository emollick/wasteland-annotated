id: method
title: The past beside the present
intro: Old poems set in the same lines as modern scenes. In November 1923, reviewing Joyce’s <i>Ulysses</i> in <i>The Dial</i>, Eliot wrote that “in manipulating a continuous parallel between contemporaneity and antiquity, Mr Joyce is pursuing a method which others must pursue after him”, and that this was “simply a way of controlling, of ordering, of giving a shape and a significance to the immense panorama of futility and anarchy which is contemporary history.” The sentences were about another man’s book, but F. O. Matthiessen was applying them to Eliot’s own work by 1935. This pathway stops where the parallel is plainest, where Chaucer, Baudelaire, Shakespeare, Spenser, Marvell, John Day or Goldsmith is set in the same sentence as a modern scene, so that the reader sees both at once. Cleanth Brooks (1939) warned that the poem’s parallels are often contrasts and its contrasts parallels, and the stops do not assume that the past comes off better.
why: The places where an old poem and a modern scene are made to share the same lines, from Chaucer’s April to the fragments at the end.
sources: Eliot, "Ulysses, Order, and Myth" (1923)
Matthiessen (1935)
Brooks (1939)

text: Chaucer's April brings the sweet showers that end the drought of March and set folk longing for pilgrimages (<i>General Prologue</i> 1–12); the card gives the lines. Eliot's first sentence answers them without quoting them. It keeps the month and the rain reaching the roots, and adds the word "cruellest". This is the pathway's first instance and its rule. The old poem is not named but heard, and a reader who knows it sees two Aprils at once. The Bible pathway takes the part's title.

line: 60
text: Eliot's note gives Baudelaire's two lines and the card the poem, "Les Sept Vieillards", in which a spectre accosts the passer-by in broad daylight. Eliot lays that Paris over the City of London at nine in the morning; Baudelaire's dirty yellow fog (his line 9) becomes "the brown fog of a winter dawn". In 1950 Eliot said that Baudelaire had shown him the sordid modern city as matter for poetry, and the realistic and the phantasmagoric fused. The Dante pathway takes the crowd.

line: 77
text: Enobarbus's speech in <i>Antony and Cleopatra</i> describes Cleopatra's barge on the Cydnus; the card gives it. Eliot keeps its shape and changes each thing in it. The barge that "Burn'd on the water" is a chair that "Glowed on the marble", and the perfumed sails become her "strange synthetic perfumes". A queen's arrival by water becomes a woman waiting in a room whose grandeur is all borrowed, since Dido's ceiling and Milton's Eden are there too (Eliot's notes on 92 and 98). The loves pathway takes her at 111.

line: 128
text: The card gives the song, "That Shakespearian Rag" of 1912, whose chorus calls it "most intelligent, very elegant". Here the method runs the other way. The popular present had already set Shakespeare to ragtime, and what follows the question "Are you alive, or not?" is not an answer but the song. Eliot's spelling adds a syllable the title does not have, and his "so elegant / So intelligent" turns the chorus round. The past reaches this room by way of a dance tune.

path: method
line: 176
text: Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> is a "spousall verse", with nymphs gathering flowers for the brides and each stanza ending on the line Eliot borrows. Here the device is the negative. The nymphs "are departed", and the river "bears no" bottles, papers or cigarette ends, a list of what is not there. Cleanth Brooks (1939) held that the poem works by "surface contrasts which in reality constitute parallelisms" as well as the reverse; Spenser himself names Elizabeth and, as "that great lord", Leicester (so Southam), the pair in the barge at 279.
sources: Spenser, Prothalamion 1–36 and 132–162

line: 185
text: Marvell's lover, arguing his mistress into bed, hears time's winged chariot at his back, and before them lie the deserts of vast eternity ("To His Coy Mistress" 21–24); the card gives it. This is the method at its smallest, one line kept and one thing changed. Marvell hears time coming; the speaker, fishing in the canal a few lines on, hears "The rattle of the bones", a chuckle, and a rat. At 196 the same line brings horns and motors, and Eliot's note on line 197 names Marvell.
sources: Marvell, "To His Coy Mistress" 21–24

line: 196
text: Eliot's note quotes John Day's <i>Parliament of Bees</i>, in which a noise of horns and hunting brings Actaeon to Diana in the spring; in Ovid the hunter who sees the goddess bathing becomes a stag and is torn by his own hounds. Here the substitution is complete. "Horns and motors" stand for horns and hunting, Sweeney, from Eliot's own poems, for the hunter, Mrs Porter's soda water for the goddess's spring, and "in the spring" is kept word for word. The war pathway takes the soldiers' song at 199.
sources: Day, The Parliament of Bees, Character 3

text: Goldsmith's song is Olivia Primrose's in <i>The Vicar of Wakefield</i>; its second stanza says the one art left her "is to die". Eliot keeps the song's first line and runs it on into the typist's room, where she "smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone." The two remedies stand side by side without comment. Cleanth Brooks (1939) found the queen of line 279 and the typist "alike as well as different"; the loves pathway takes her at 249.

text: The line is the poet's own, set among borrowed ones, which the Fragments view and the card give one by one. In the draft it read "spelt into my ruins"; "shored against" was the revision. Set it beside the 1923 sentence on Joyce, which called the method "a way of controlling, of ordering, of giving a shape and a significance" to contemporary history. The essay says order; the line says propping up. F. O. Matthiessen was applying the sentence to Eliot's own work by 1935.
