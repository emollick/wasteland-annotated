id: seers
title: Prophecy and sight
intro: Prophecy in the poem, and the sight it depends on. The poem is full of people who claim to see what others cannot, and it shows what each of them is allowed to see. Its epigraph is spoken by a man who saw a prophetess with his own eyes, and all she had left to say was that she wanted to die. Eliot’s note on line 218 calls the blind Tiresias the poem’s most important personage and what he sees its substance; but his foresight is of things that have always happened, and the thunder near the end says one syllable, which each hearer must construe for himself. Between the seers come the eyes that fail, from the hyacinth garden to the road to Emmaus. The stops say at each what is seen, by whom, and on what authority.
why: The poem’s prophets, fortune-tellers and blind seers, from the Sibyl in the epigraph to Isaiah’s warning near the end, with the eyes that fail between them.

text: The poem's first seer is a prophetess with one prophecy left, and it concerns herself. Trimalchio saw the Sibyl of Cumae hanging in a jar, and when boys asked what she wanted she answered that she wanted to die (the epigraph card gives his words). Ovid tells how it came about: Apollo offered her whatever she chose, she pointed to a heap of dust and asked for as many years as it had grains, and forgot to ask for youth. She will dwindle until only her voice is left.
sources: Petronius, Satyricon 48

text: Here the poem takes on the voice of prophecy. "Son of man" is how God addresses Ezekiel (the card at line 20 gives it), and the speaker who borrows it addresses someone who knows only "A heap of broken images"; Southam sets the phrase beside Ezekiel's judgement on idols, "your images shall be broken" (6:4). The voice promises what a prophet is given, something shown ("I will show you", twice), and what it shows is not a vision but "fear in a handful of dust".

text: The first eyes in the poem proper are these, and they fail before light, not darkness: "Looking into the heart of light, the silence." Then Wagner's shepherd, set to watch for Isolde's ship and to pipe a merry tune if he sees it, reports the sea desolate and empty (line 42; the card gives the scene). In four lines sight fails twice: one who looks and cannot see, one who looks and finds nothing to see. Both failures return on the road to Emmaus (line 360).

line: 43
text: The poem's professional seer has a cold and, the card says, a name usually traced to Huxley's <i>Crome Yellow</i>. Eliot's note on line 46 says her reading comes true: the Sailor and the Merchant "appear later", and "Death by Water is executed in Part IV". Her sight has two blanks: a card she is "forbidden to see", and the Hanged Man she cannot find. Her last line is a fortune-teller's caution: under the Vagrancy Act of 1824 anyone "pretending or professing to tell fortunes" was a rogue and vagabond.

text: The one command to look in Part I is Eliot's own: the word is not in Ariel's song, which the card gives. Ariel sings it to Ferdinand, who believes his father drowned, and the pearls are what the sea has made of a dead man's eyes, "something rich and strange". The line comes back at 125 as the one thing the man in Part II says he remembers, and Eliot's note on line 126 sends it here and to the garden of line 37, where eyes failed.

text: The crowd that Dante's line has just called dead (the card gives his passage) comes over the bridge looking down, and no one in it looks up at the one who has just seen what it is. Against the command to look at line 48, the crowd is looked at and does not look. Southam sets the passage beside Ezekiel's valley of dry bones, where the prophet is told to "Prophesy unto the wind" so that the slain may live (37:9); here no one prophesies to the dead.

text: Eliot's note sends the chess to Middleton's <i>Women Beware Women</i>, where Livia keeps a widow at the board while, above them, the Duke seduces the widow's daughter-in-law Bianca; the card gives the scene. Eliot quoted the exchange in 1927, down to "I've given thee blind mate twice", and wrote that "The irony could not be improved upon". In the play the widow answers, "My eyes begin to fail." The watcher at the board sees nothing of what matters, and eyes that are "lidless" cannot even close.

line: 218
text: In Ovid, whose lines Eliot's note quotes, Juno struck Tiresias blind for siding with Jupiter, and Jupiter, unable to undo it, gave him foreknowledge. Brooks (1939) drew the irony that the commentator here is the seer who in Sophocles told Oedipus the cause of the plague on Thebes. His foresight is of what has always happened: he "foretold the rest" and has "foresuffered all". Kenner (1959) read Eliot's note, which calls him "the most important personage in the poem", as an offer to supply "a nameable point of view".
sources: Ovid, Metamorphoses III 316–338

line: 360
text: Eliot's headnote to Part V names the journey to Emmaus, where two disciples walk with the risen Christ and "their eyes were holden that they should not know him" (Luke 24:16) until, at supper, "their eyes were opened" and he vanishes (24:31). His note on this line gives the Antarctic delusion of one member more than could be counted (the card gives Shackleton). Counting and looking give different answers here, and the figure is hooded and its sex unknown. As in the garden at line 39, sight fails.

text: Eliot's note on these lines quotes the close of Hesse's essay on <i>The Brothers Karamazov</i>: half of Europe is on the way to chaos, singing drunkenly as Dmitri Karamazov sang, and while the citizen laughs, "der Heilige und Seher hört sie mit Tränen", the saint and the seer hear them with tears. Read lines 367 to 385 as what such a seer hears and sees: questions first, "What is that sound", "Who are those hooded hordes", from line 378 plain statement, a woman fiddling on her hair, towers upside down.

path: seers
line: 400
text: The poem's last voice of prophecy is not a person's but thunder's, and it says one syllable. In the Upanishad (the card gives the fable) Prajapati answers gods, men and demons with one syllable, Da, and each hears in it the word it needs; then, in Max Müller's translation, "The divine voice of thunder repeats the same, Da Da Da". After the Sibyl, the clairvoyante and Tiresias, prophecy has come down to an oracle whose meaning lies with the hearer, and the poem takes all three meanings.
sources: Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, V.2

text: The last prophet's words in the poem are Isaiah's to King Hezekiah, sick and told that he will die: "Set thine house in order: for thou shalt die, and not live" (Isaiah 38:1; the same words in 2 Kings 20:1). The fisher on the shore, whom the card takes with Weston's Fisher King, turns the prophet's command into a question about his lands, and the poem gives no prophet to answer it; the next line is London Bridge falling.
sources: King James Bible, Isaiah 38:1
King James Bible, 2 Kings 20:1
Southam, Guide, note on line 425
