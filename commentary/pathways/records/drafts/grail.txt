title: The Grail and the waste land
intro: The Grail legend as Eliot took it from Weston and Frazer. Eliot’s headnote says that the title, the plan and “a good deal of the incidental symbolism” came from Jessie L. Weston’s <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920), and that readers of Frazer’s <i>The Golden Bough</i> will recognise “certain references to vegetation ceremonies”. Weston argued that the Grail romances keep the remains of an old rite, in which a king’s wound leaves his land waste and its waters dry until a knight asks the right question, so that the king is healed and the waters flow. Frazer supplies the rite behind the romance, the god who dies with the year and is buried or drowned so that he may return with the spring. Cleanth Brooks made the legend the key to the whole poem in 1939; Eliot himself, in a lecture of 1956, regretted having sent readers on a “wild goose chase” after the Tarot and the Grail.
why: Weston’s Grail legend and Frazer’s dying god in the poem, from the dead land of the first lines to the fisher on the shore.
sources: Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II
Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXIX
Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXII
Brooks (1939)
Eliot, "The Frontiers of Criticism" (1956)

text: Frazer's rites exist to bring the year back. The dead, he writes, "came back in the sprouting corn" and were imagined to return in the spring flowers, "the violets and the hyacinths", which "sprang from their dust". The poem's first voice feels that return as cruelty and prefers the "forgetful snow". Cleanth Brooks, who in 1939 read the poem through Weston and Frazer, wrote that its people "are afraid to live in reality", so that "April, the month of rebirth, is not the most joyful season but the cruelest".

text: These lines are the poem's fullest picture of the waste land, dry stone and dead tree and "no sound of water", before any quest has begun. Weston traces the Grail hero's oldest task, the freeing of the waters, to the <i>Rig-Veda</i>, whose hymns praise Indra for killing the giant that had "imprisoned" the rivers: "Indra has filled the rivers, he has inundated the dry land." This is such a land before the giant is killed; no rain reaches it until the "damp gust / Bringing rain" of line 394.

text: The card she cannot find is the dying god's. Eliot's note on line 46 ties the Hanged Man to "the Hanged God of Frazer", a chapter of <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i> on Attis, whose effigy hung on a pine and whose human representative, Frazer conjectures, was once hanged there. Weston's dead god is "not buried in the earth, but thrown into the water", and Brooks (1939) read "Fear death by water" as spoken by one who does not know "that the way into life may be by death itself".

text: The card at this line gives Frazer's rites. In Frazer the priests of Osiris buried corn-stuffed effigies of the god at the sowing and dug them up with the corn "found to have sprouted from the body of Osiris", a sprouting hailed as the cause of the crops; "Will it bloom this year?" is the rite's own question. The poem gives it to a man who would keep the corpse in the ground, safe from the frost and from the Dog that would dig it up again.

text: The card at this line has Ferdinand and the Fisher King together; Brooks (1939) read the fisher, for "the reader who knows the Weston references", as the Fisher King. In Weston the king's sickness keeps his land waste, and his castle stands by water, "on or near the sea, or on the banks of an important river". The poem gives its king no wound and no castle, only a canal, a rat, and the "White bodies naked on the low damp ground" of the land he keeps.

line: 312
text: "Death by Water is executed in Part IV", Eliot's note on line 46 says. In Frazer the women of Alexandria carried the image of the dead Adonis "to the sea-shore and committed it to the waves", and "sang that the lost one would come back again". The poem keeps the drowning and drops the return; a current picks Phlebas's bones, and the section ends in a warning, not in a rising. Brooks (1939) nevertheless suggested that the section "gives an instance of the conquest of death and time".

text: Eliot's headnote to his notes on Part V names three themes, the second "the approach to the Chapel Perilous (see Miss Weston's book)". In Weston the knight rides to the chapel through a storm; lines 331 to 359 are that road without a knight or a storm, through mountains "of rock without water" where the "dry sterile thunder without rain" is the thunder that will speak at line 400. This is the land whose waters, in the oldest form of the legend, the hero comes to free.

text: The freeing of the waters, in Weston the oldest form of the hero's task, is imagined here as sound only, and cancelled at line 359. The hermit-thrush of Eliot's note on line 357 supplies the sound; the note quotes Chapman's handbook and adds that the bird's "water-dripping song" is justly celebrated. Eliot told Ford Madox Ford in 1923 that the poem had "about 30 good lines" and that they were "the 29 lines of the water-dripping song in the last part". text: The card at line 389 gives Weston's Perilous Chapel, an adventure she says is "fraught with extreme peril to life". The poem's chapel has no body, no hand and no voice, "only the wind's home", and "Dry bones can harm no one". Brooks (1939) called it "perhaps also the Perilous Chapel"; Southam's note says it is. What follows is what the land has lacked since line 24, a cock, a flash of lightning and "a damp gust / Bringing rain".

text: Eliot's note on line 425 sends the reader to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King, and the card at this line gives her argument. In Weston the quest is achieved when a knight comes to the castle and asks the question, and the king is healed or, in the oldest version, "the waters flowed again thro' their channel". Here no knight has come. The king is still fishing, the plain behind him is still arid, and the one question asked is his own, about his lands and not his healing.
sources: Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. IX
Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. II
