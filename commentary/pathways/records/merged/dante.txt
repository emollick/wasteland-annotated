title: Dante and the dead
intro: The poem’s dead, and what they keep of Dante’s. Five of Eliot’s notes send the reader to Dante, more than to any other writer: to the <i>Inferno</i> for the crowd on London Bridge (the notes on lines 63 and 64) and for Count Ugolino, starved in his tower (412), and to the <i>Purgatorio</i> for La Pia, a murdered wife (293), and for the troubadour Arnaut Daniel in the refining fire (428), the canto from which the dedication to Pound is also taken. Dante’s dead, met and known by name, tell the living who they were and ask to be remembered. This pathway stops at the poem’s dead in order and asks what each passage keeps of that. Eliot’s essay on Dante in <i>The Sacred Wood</i> (1920) and his <i>Dante</i> of 1929 are quoted where they bear on a line, and his lecture of 1950 on what Dante meant to him is drawn on at line 62.
why: The poem’s dead taken in order and set beside Dante’s, from the crowd on London Bridge to the poet Arnaut Daniel in the fire of Purgatory.
sources: The Sacred Wood, "Dante"
Eliot, Dante (1929)
Eliot, "What Dante Means to Me" (1950)

text: The dedication, "il miglior fabbro", the better craftsman, is Dante's; the card gives the line (<i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI 117). In the fire that purges the lustful, the poet Guido Guinizelli points Dante to the troubadour Arnaut Daniel with these words, asks Dante to say a paternoster for him in heaven, and vanishes into the fire like a fish going through water to the bottom (XXVI 127–135). Arnaut's own plea, at the end of the canto, gives the poem its last words from Dante, at line 428, where this pathway ends.

text: Eliot's notes send this crowd to two places at the edge of Dante's Hell: the neutrals in the vestibule inside the gate, whom heaven casts out and deep Hell will not receive (<i>Inferno</i> III 40–42), and Limbo, the first circle, where the unbaptised sigh in "grief without torments" (IV 28). The crowd here is undone and sighing, "so many", and no one in it named until Stetson at line 69. The card at line 63 has what Eliot said of this crowd in 1950.

text: To know a face among the dead and stop it is Dante's scene. In <i>Inferno</i> XV a burnt figure catches Dante by the hem; it is Brunetto Latini, his old teacher, and Dante asks "Are you here, ser Brunetto?" (XV 23–30, 82–85). In <i>The Sacred Wood</i> (1920) Eliot placed the passage's emotion in "Brunetto's excellence in damnation". The comparison with Stetson is a critic's, A. C. Charity's (1974); when Ethel Stephenson, writing for lay readers in the 1940s, put it to Eliot, he said it had not occurred to him.

text: Dante's dead have their places; few of the poem's are buried. Part I is named for the burial service, and its one buried body, the corpse, may be dug up again (71–75, Cornelia's dirge in Webster's <i>The White Devil</i> over her murdered son, for "the friendless bodies of unburied men"). The bones here are rattled "year to year"; a current picks Phlebas's (316). The man fishing at line 189 has mourned "the king my father's death" in words from Ferdinand's in <i>The Tempest</i>; Ferdinand's father, thought drowned, is alive.

text: Tiresias, the blind seer, gives two credentials. Thebes is the city of Sophocles' <i>Oedipus the King</i>, where Tiresias told Oedipus what the king could not see. The walk among the dead is <i>Odyssey</i> XI, where Odysseus summons the shades with blood; Circe has told him that Tiresias alone keeps his understanding in death, "but the other ghosts flit about aimlessly" (X 490–495, in Butler's translation). He is the only speaker who says he has been among the dead and come back, as Dante has in the <i>Commedia</i>.
sources: Homer, Odyssey X 490–495 and XI

line: 293
text: Eliot's note gives La Pia's line (<i>Purgatorio</i> V), a murdered wife's account of herself; the card has the passage. She begins with a request, "ricorditi di me", remember me, as Dante's dead often do (Guinizelli and Arnaut at XXVI 130 and 147). The first of Eliot's three Thames-daughters, speaking here, asks nothing. "Undid me" translates "disfecemi", and the same verb, disfare, is behind "undone" at line 63, where death has "disfatta" the crowd (<i>Inferno</i> III 57). "Siena made me, Maremma unmade me" is Eliot's own translation, of 1929.

text: The card gives the draft, some ninety lines of voyage and wreck, and Eliot's remark of 1959 that it was "rather inspired" by the Ulysses canto of the <i>Inferno</i>. There Ulysses, one of Dante's dead, tells from his flame how his ship, in sight of the mountain of Purgatory, was spun three times by a whirlwind and went down as the sea closed over it (XXVI 133–142). Pound cut the voyage and, in January 1922, insisted that Phlebas stay. Ulysses tells his own death; Phlebas has his told for him.

line: 329
text: Here the speaker counts himself among the dying. The crowd at line 63 was undone and seen from outside; now "we" are dying, and "were living" puts the living in the past tense in which Dante's dead tell their lives, as in La Pia's "Siena mi fé", Siena made me (<i>Purgatorio</i> V 134). The lines before, "He who was living is now dead" among them, are the Passion, as the card says; this stop is for line 329 alone, and the comparison with Dante's grammar is the pathway's own.
sources: Dante, Purgatorio V 133–134

line: 412
text: Eliot's note sends the key to Count Ugolino, starved in a tower at Pisa with his sons (<i>Inferno</i> XXXIII 46); the card tells the story. He is in the ninth and lowest circle, in ice among the traitors, gnawing the skull of the archbishop who starved him (XXXIII 1–3 and 14). He speaks so that his words may be "seed" bearing infamy for his enemy (7–9). The thunder's word means sympathise, Eliot's note says; in this pathway's reading the poem answers from the place in Dante furthest from pity.
sources: Dante, Inferno XXXIII 1–78

line: 428
text: Eliot's note gives the lines. Arnaut Daniel asks Dante to remember his pain and hides in the refining fire. Eliot had taken "Ara vos prec", now I pray you, as the title of his 1920 volume, printed "Ara Vus Prec", a misprint for Dante's "vos". In <i>Dante</i> (1929), translating the passage, he wrote that purgatory's flame, unlike hell's, "is deliberately and consciously accepted by the penitent", and "In their suffering is hope".
