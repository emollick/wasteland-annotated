## Findings

### dante line 62
Kind: fact
Problem: "two places at the edge of Dante's Hell, short of its torments" is true of Limbo and false of the neutrals. Inferno IV 28 is "ciò avvenia di duol sanza martìri" (grief without torments), as the stop says; but the neutrals, seven lines after the passage Eliot's note quotes, are tormented: "Questi sciaurati, che mai non fur vivi, / erano ignudi e stimolati molto / da mosconi e da vespe ch'eran ivi. / Elle rigavan lor di sangue il volto, / che, mischiato di lagrime, a' lor piedi / da fastidiosi vermi era ricolto" (III 64–69: naked, stung by hornets and wasps, their faces streaked with blood which worms gather at their feet), and they run behind a banner (III 52–54). What is true of both places is that they lie before Hell proper: the neutrals in the vestibule inside the gate, before Acheron, whom "lo profondo inferno" will not receive (III 41), and Limbo, the first circle, without torments. The reader's proposed "Neither is a place of punishment" would carry the same error. Point (4), the Southam citation, is settled as far as the scan allows and stands: Southam's Guide gives Eliot's 1950 sentences in every edition scanned (1968, 1974, 1990, 1994, 1996), in the note on the London Bridge lines, which first translates Inferno III 55–57 ("... I should never have believed that death had undone so many'). This is Dante's reaction to seeing the vast crowd of unhappy spirits") and then quotes the talk, inserting "[line 63]" after "I had not thought death had undone so many"; the note's key itself does not show in the snippets, but it is the note on the line Eliot's own note is on, and no other note in the Guide has the passage (the only other "What Dante Means to Me" in the 1994 scan is the introduction's sentence on the "unpoetical"). The description of the talk is accurate: "the vision of my city clerks trooping over London Bridge from the railway station to their offices", and the references given "in order to make the reader who recognized the allusion, know that I meant him to recognize it" (To Criticize the Critic scan, tocriticizecriti0000tsel).
Corrected: Eliot's notes send this crowd to two places at the edge of Dante's Hell: the neutrals in the vestibule inside the gate, whom heaven casts out and deep Hell will not receive (<i>Inferno</i> III 40–42), and Limbo, the first circle, where the unbaptised sigh in "grief without torments" (IV 28). In 1950 Eliot said that he gave the references so that a reader who recognised the allusion would know he was meant to; the crowd, he said, was his city clerks going from the station to their offices.
Sources unchanged: Dante, Inferno III 34–42 and 55–57 / Dante, Inferno IV 25–30 / Eliot, "What Dante Means to Me" (1950) / Southam, Guide, note on line 63.

### dante line 193
Kind: judgement
Problem: "has mourned 'the king my father's death' in Ferdinand's words from <i>The Tempest</i>" makes the quoted words Ferdinand's. They are the poem's (line 192). Ferdinand's, in I.ii, are "Sitting on a bank, / Weeping again the king my father's wreck, / This music crept by me upon the waters" (MIT Shakespeare text); Eliot gave the wreck to "the king my brother" at 191 and changed "wreck" to "death" at 192. The rest checks: Cornelia's dirge is The White Devil V.iv in the Gutenberg text ("Call for the robin redbreast, and the wren, ... The friendless bodies of unburied men"), sung over Marcello, whom Flamineo has killed; Alonso is alive; Phlebas's bones at 315–316; Part I's title is the Prayer Book's "The Order for the Burial of the Dead".
Corrected: The poem's dead are not buried. Part I is named for the burial service; its one buried body, the corpse in the garden, may be dug up again (71–75, Cornelia's dirge in Webster's <i>The White Devil</i>, over her murdered son, for "the friendless bodies of unburied men"); here bones lie in a garret, rattled "year to year"; Phlebas's are picked by the current (316). The man fishing at 189 has mourned "the king my father's death" in words from Ferdinand's in <i>The Tempest</i>, "the king my father's wreck", and Ferdinand's father, whom he thinks drowned, is alive.
Sources unchanged.

### dante line 428
Kind: fact
Problem: two things. (a) "then hid himself in the fire that refines them" is in quotation marks and is nobody's. The Sacred Wood prints the Italian line untranslated ("... and the errors of Arnaut are corrected— / Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina."), and Eliot's Dante (1929) renders it "Then dived he back into that fire which refines them" (scan dante0000tsel, the Purgatorio section, after "'... be mindful in due time of my pain,'"). Since the stop goes on to say that in 1929 Eliot "translated the passage", the quoted words will be read as his. Quote his rendering (1929 is quotable) or drop the marks. (b) "Eliot had taken 'Ara Vos Prec' as the title of his volume of 1920": the coordinator's point. Gallup's entry A4, in the 1969 scan the writer used (tseliotbibliogra0000gall), is headed "ARA VOS PREC [1920]" and transcribes the title page "a. First edition: Ara Vus Prec | by | T. S. Eliot | [device] | [London] The Ovid Press", with the front cover lettered "ARA VUS PREC", and quotes Eliot's later explanation that the correct title is Ara Vos Prec and that it "only happened to be Vus on the title page" because he did not know Provençal and was copying an Italian edition of Dante whose text was in error; Southam's note (1994) says the same, "not knowing Provençal, Eliot was misled by a misprint in the Italian edition of Dante which he had carried ...". The 1920 book that a reader will find reads "Ara Vus Prec", so the stop should carry the printed spelling and say it is a misprint of Dante's "Ara vos prec" (Purgatorio XXVI 145, as the Wikisource text and Eliot's note both have it). Eliot's explanation is after 1929: describe it, or quote no more than a phrase. The rest is exact: "and the errors of Arnaut are corrected" closes the list "The crime of Bertrand becomes more lurid; the vindictive Adamo acquires greater ferocity, and ..." in "Dante" in The Sacred Wood; Dante (1929): "In purgatory the torment of flame is deliberately and consciously accepted by the penitent" and "In their suffering is hope, in the anaesthesia of Virgil is hopelessness".
Corrected: Eliot's note gives the lines. Arnaut Daniel asks Dante to remember his pain and hides himself in the fire that refines them. Eliot took Arnaut's opening words as the title of his volume of 1920, printed "Ara Vus Prec", a misprint for Dante's "vos". In <i>The Sacred Wood</i> the line ends a list of what the next world does to Dante's souls, "and the errors of Arnaut are corrected". In <i>Dante</i> (1929), translating the passage, he wrote that purgatory's flame, unlike hell's, "is deliberately and consciously accepted by the penitent": "In their suffering is hope".
(If the editor prefers Eliot's own rendering in the first sentence: 'Arnaut Daniel asks Dante to remember his pain, "Then dived he back into that fire which refines them" in Eliot's translation of 1929'; the stop is then near the ninety-word limit and the last sentence should lose "translating the passage".)
Sources unchanged: Dante, Purgatorio XXVI 140–148 / The Sacred Wood, "Dante" / Eliot, Dante (1929) / Gallup, entry A4.
