# Reader's report, round 1: war ("After the war")

Read as the brief asks: STANDARD.md, then drafts/war.txt with poem.txt open, the lines at each stop before the note, and the marginal card at each line (glosses-index.txt, and the full card bodies in site-data.js) beside it, since the brief says a stop must not restate the card. I also read design/war.md for the intended stops, and PATHWAYS.md and MERGE-BRIEF.md for the titles of the other pathways.

## Accepted as they stand

None of the nine records is accepted whole. The title, "After the war", stands as it is, and so do the intro's first sentence ("The war of 1914–1918 and its aftermath, as the poem carries them."), which is short, stands alone in the corner and names the subject, and its third ("The poem never names the war."). The objections to the head are to the rest of the intro and to the why line.

Read through, the pathway holds together as one theme, what the poem keeps of the war and its aftermath, in the poem's order, and the stops do what the intro promises at its end, naming Brooks, Kenner, Hayward, Southam and Rainey where a claim is a reading. The why line says honestly why one would take it, except that its "from the trenches of rats' alley to London Bridge falling down" describes a pathway that begins at line 115, and this one begins in Munich at line 12. The main fault of the draft, in a reader's eyes, is that five of the eight stops (70, 115, 139, 199, 369) spend most of their words saying what the card at the line already says, sometimes in the card's own words, so that the stop's own matter (Hayward at 70, Southam's slang at 115, the Act's hours at 139, Gallipoli at 199, the Bolshevik connection at 369) arrives late and short. The corrected texts point at the card in a clause and give the stop's own matter the room. The other faults are the shapes the standard names: an aphoristic last sentence at 139 and 231, a summing-up close at 12, a loose end at 369, a name dropped bare ("Mayerling" at 12, "Hesse" at 369), and the hand-offs in the workshop's words.

The hand-offs. Four stops, not five, end by handing lines across, with six pointers between them: 70 ("The Dante pathway takes the meeting, the Grail pathway the corpse"), 199 ("The Wagner pathway takes Verlaine's Parsifal at 202"), 231 ("The loves pathway owns the scene") and 427 ("The method pathway takes the fragments at 431; the Dante pathway, Arnaut at 428"). As written they are the notes talking to each other: "takes" and "owns" are the words of the design's ownership table, and "the loves pathway", "the method pathway" are working ids a reader never sees. A reader needs two of the six. At 70 the corpse: a reader on a pathway about the war will take the corpse "planted last year in your garden" for the war dead, and the note passes it by, so it should say where the corpse is read: "For the corpse in the garden at 71, see the Grail pathway, which reads it as Frazer's buried god." At 231 the scene: the note reduces the typist and the clerk to a hat, and the reader has the whole scene open, so: "For the scene itself, see the pathway on the loves that fail." The other four go. The meeting at 69 is what the stop at 70 is itself about, so a pointer to the Dante pathway tells the reader nothing; the French line at 202 is given by the card at that line, and nothing of the war is passed by there; and 427 is the pathway's last stop, where the card at 431 gives Arnaut and the fragments and the reader is not being led past anything. If the editor wants any of them kept, the forms are: "The meeting itself, "There I saw one I knew", is read on the pathway on Dante and the dead"; "The French line at 202, from Verlaine's sonnet on Wagner's <i>Parsifal</i>, is read on the Wagner pathway"; "Arnaut in the fire at 428 is read on the pathway on Dante and the dead, and the fragments at 431 on the pathway on the past beside the present."

The intro's list. The long sentence ("It has the trenches in "rats' alley", a husband four years in the army and "demobbed", a pub whose hours the war had cut, a song the Australian troops sang, a Bradford millionaire in a silk hat, the hooded hordes of an eastern Europe that Eliot's note calls in decay, the capitals of fallen empires, and London Bridge falling down.") reads as a rhythmic list, not as an editor's plain account. Eight objects hang on the one verb "has", which is lost by the fourth; each is phrased as a small picture rather than named ("a Bradford millionaire in a silk hat", "the capitals of fallen empires"); and the sentence builds to a close on the poem's own words. The design's own rule for the intro is "not a list". It is also not the table of contents it presents itself as: "The stops take these in order" is not true, because the stops begin with two things the list does not name (the claim of nationality at 12, Mylae at 70), and "the capitals of fallen empires" is not a stop but a clause inside the stop at 369. What to do: keep a table of contents, because the reader at the chooser is deciding whether to take the pathway and this is what tells them, but in the editor's plain form, the stops' subjects in the stops' order with their line numbers, in sentences that keep hold of their verb, and with nothing in it that is not a stop. The corrected intro below does that. If the owner would rather have no enumeration at all, the one sentence "The eight stops go in the poem's order, from a claim of nationality at line 12 to London Bridge falling down at 427." does the job, and the guide's own list of stops does the rest.

## Objections

### war head
Problem: "Eliot wrote the poem in 1921 and published it in October 1922, three years after the Armistice" hangs the three years on October 1922, which is nearly four years after November 1918; the three years belong to the writing, and the why line's "three years before" needs the same anchor. "An eastern Europe that Eliot's note calls in decay" is not a construction a person would use. The eight-item sentence is a rhythmic list that does not match the stops (see above). "Carries" is used three times across the head, and the why line ("What the poem carries of the war") repeats the intro's first sentence ("as the poem carries them"), which the writing brief forbids. The why line's "from the trenches of rats' alley to London Bridge falling down" misdescribes the range: the pathway starts at line 12. The intro is 145 words; the corrected one is 159.
Corrected (intro): The war of 1914–1918 and its aftermath, as the poem carries them. Eliot wrote the poem in 1921, three years after the Armistice, and published it in October 1922; at Lloyds Bank, where he worked, he had been put in charge in 1920 of settling the bank’s pre-war debts with Germany. The poem never names the war. The eight stops go in the poem’s order. They are a claim of nationality (line 12) and a sea battle of 260 BC (70), set by readers beside the war just ended; the trench name in “rats’ alley” (115); a husband “demobbed” after four years in the army (139); the song the Australian troops sang (199); a Bradford millionaire’s silk hat (231); the “hooded hordes” of Eliot’s note on eastern Europe (369); and London Bridge falling down (427). Each says what of the war is in the passage, and names the reader who first said so where the claim is a reading.
Corrected (why): What the poem has of the war that had ended three years before, taken in order from the German line at 12 to London Bridge falling down.

### war line 12
Problem: "The line's claim of nationality is dated" is read first as "old-fashioned", and the reader has to wait a sentence to learn that it means the claim can be given a date. "The card gives Countess Marie Larisch" points at the wrong card: the card at this line is "German, not Russian"; Larisch is the card at 15. "Rainey's note on line 15 tells her history, in which the archduke was found dead in 1889" is cryptic where the card at 15 is plain (Crown Prince Rudolf, her cousin, found dead with Mary Vetsera at Mayerling in January 1889, and "the archduke's, my cousin's" fits him), and Kenner's "Mayerling" is then a name dropped bare; the stop should use the card's names. One fact to check: the stop has Southam dating Lithuania's independence from 1919, and the card at this line says it left the Russian Empire in 1918 [to check; the declaration was in February 1918, recognition later; the stop should carry whichever the checker settles, and not contradict the card]. The last sentence, "are thus placed in a Europe the war had ended", is the summing-up close; the corrected text puts the point first as a plain statement and lets the two facts follow.
Corrected: The poem's first speakers belong to the Europe of before the war. Southam's note on the line says Lithuania, a Baltic state long ruled by Russia, was independent only from 1919 [to check: the card here says 1918], after the war. The archduke of line 13 fits Crown Prince Rudolf, found dead at Mayerling in 1889; the card at 15 gives him and his cousin Countess Marie Larisch, whose memoir is behind lines 8–18. Hugh Kenner (1959) heard in Marie's voice "the vanished Austro-Hungarian Empire, the inbred malaise of Mayerling".

### war line 70
Problem: The first sentence repeats the card at this line (Mylae, 260 BC, Rome and Carthage, Matthiessen's identification), which the Cities pathway's stop at 70 also gives, and the card already has Brooks's reading too, the Punic War as a trade war and so a parallel to the war of 1914–18, so the third sentence repeats it again; what is the stop's own is Hayward and Stetson "as every man", and they are buried. "Read it so" is loose, and a reader cannot tell whether "as every man" means each of us or the Everyman of the morality play [to check Brooks's word]. The card gives the words "all the wars are one war" to Brooks and the stop gives "All wars are one war" to John Hayward through Southam; Southam's note does print the words in quotation marks after "A trade war (cf. 1914–1918)" (seen in the scan), so the stop is consistent with Southam, but a reader with the card open sees two owners, and the checker should settle whether both say it. On the hand-offs, see above: the Dante pointer goes, the Grail pointer stays in the plain form.
Corrected: The card at this line gives Mylae, the sea battle of 260 BC, and the reading of Cleanth Brooks (1939) that the Punic War, a trade war, is a parallel to the war of 1914–18. Southam's note on the line quotes Eliot's friend John Hayward to the same effect: "All wars are one war". Brooks also read Stetson, hailed as a shipmate of 260 BC, as every man. For the corpse in the garden at 71, see the Grail pathway, which reads it as Frazer's buried god.

### war line 115
Problem: The card at this line already ends "This is his unspoken answer to 'What are you thinking of?'", and the card at 111 says the same at length, so the third sentence says twice what both cards say; its one addition, that the trenches enter the poem as a thought not said aloud, is the stop's point and should stand on its own. "Cleanth Brooks (1939) set this sterile death against the pearls of line 125" leaves "this sterile death" with nothing to attach to (the line says bones, not death) and the pearls unexplained for a reader who has not taken the Metamorphosis pathway; a word on each, and a pointer to the card at 48, fixes it.
Corrected: The card gives Rats' Alley as a trench name; Southam's note on the line adds the suggestion that "rats' alley" was soldiers' slang for the trenches of the Western Front, alley being the word for a trench. The line is his unspoken answer, as the cards at 111 and 115 say, so the trenches enter the poem as a thought not said aloud. Cleanth Brooks (1939) set the sterile death of rats' alley against the pearls of 125, the drowned man's eyes of Ariel's song (the card at 48).

### war line 139
Problem: The first sentence repeats the card at this line ("demobbed", 1919 and 1920, four years), with "demobilized" where the card has "demobilised"; "closing time, under hours cut in the war" repeats the card at 141, which also already has the Licensing Act of 1921 and eleven in London. "Eight hours a day ending at ten at night, nine and eleven in London" cannot be parsed on one reading (nine what? eleven what?). The last sentence, "The husband home from the army and the state's clock on the wall are the homecoming as the poem has it", is the "X and Y are Z" aphorism with a clever figure in it; its plain content, that this scene is the poem's homecoming from the war, is the stop's reason for being and goes first, without the figure. For the editor: the loves draft also stops at 139 (the design gave it 142), which is not this stop's fault but may matter if the guide holds one stop per line.
Corrected: The scene is the homecoming from the war as the poem has it. Albert has "been in the army four years", and the card gives "demobbed", demobilised, as the slang of 1919 and 1920 for it. HURRY UP PLEASE ITS TIME is the landlord's closing time, under hours cut in the war (the card at 141) and fixed by the Licensing Act of August 1921, eight hours a day and closing by ten at night, or nine hours and eleven o'clock in London.

### war line 199
Problem: The stop repeats the card at 198–201 almost whole: the Australian troops, Mrs Porter's Cairo brothel, the cruder soldiers' versions and Eliot's note are all there. Its own matter is Southam's Gallipoli and the point that this is the poem's one song of the war and comes in cleaned up. "Sung, notably, as they landed at Gallipoli" has the knowing "notably". The hand-off to the Wagner pathway goes (see above); the card at 202 gives Verlaine's line and its Parsifal, and nothing of the war is passed by there.
Corrected: The card gives the ballad, the Cairo brothel-keeper its Mrs Porter is said to have been, and Eliot's note that it "was reported to me from Sydney, Australia". Southam's note on the line adds that the Australian troops sang it as they landed at Gallipoli in 1915, and that Eliot's wording obscures the point of the soldiers' version. It is the only song of the war in the poem, and it comes in cleaned up.

### war line 231
Problem: The stop is at 231, but its subject is the simile at 233–234, so a reader who opens at "He, the young man carbuncular, arrives," must look three lines on to find what the note is about; the design put it at 231 to divide the passage with the loves pathway (215, 249), but 233 is free and the writing brief allows a move of a line or two, so I suggest 233, and the corrected text names the lines either way. The card at 222–248 already places Bradford as the wool town that stood for fortunes made in the war and glosses the silk hat, so the second sentence half repeats it, though Southam's "crop of millionaires" and Rainey's "charges of wartime profiteering" are the stop's own and stay. "The one figure of the war's winners in the poem is a hat, and the clerk is judged by it" is the aphorism shape; the plain fact under it is that the poem's only war profiteer appears in a simile. "The loves pathway owns the scene" is the workshop's sentence; the pointer is one a reader needs, in the plain form.
Corrected: The clerk's assurance is measured against "a silk hat on a Bradford millionaire" (233–234). Southam's note on 234 says Bradford, a manufacturing town in the north of England, was reputed to have produced a crop of millionaires, men who made their fortunes supplying woollen goods in the First World War; Rainey's note says the wool town faced charges of wartime profiteering. The poem's one figure from the war's winners appears only here, as a hat in a simile. For the scene itself, see the pathway on the loves that fail.

### war line 369
Problem: "Hesse's <i>Blick ins Chaos</i>, the essay on the downfall of Europe" makes the book an essay: it is three essays, and the passage is the close of the first, on <i>The Brothers Karamazov</i>, as the card at this line says; and "Hesse" is a name dropped bare where the card says "Hermann Hesse". The card also already gives the headnote's "the present decay of eastern Europe" (which the corrected intro now quotes, so the stop can spare it), Eliot's letter to Hesse of March 1922, the visit on 28 May 1922 (at Montagnola, where the stop says "near Lugano", so the reader sees two names for one place) and a translation of the German, so the last sentence, "Eliot visited Hesse near Lugano on 28 May 1922", is both a repeat and a loose end, attached to nothing; it goes, and the Letters citation on the sources line is then for the checker to keep or drop. "Brooks (1939)" is "Cleanth Brooks (1939)" everywhere else on the pathway. The stop does not say what the five cities have to do with the war, though the intro promises "the capitals of fallen empires"; the corrected text adds the one clause the draft's own head and its stop at 12 (Kenner's "vanished Austro-Hungarian Empire") support.
Corrected: Eliot's note on lines 367–377 quotes the close of an essay in Hermann Hesse's <i>Blick ins Chaos</i> (1920); the card at this line gives the essay. In it half of Europe, or at least its eastern half, is on the road to chaos, drunk and singing. Southam's note on the hordes connects them with the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917, and Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the five cities of 375–376, among them Vienna, whose empire the war had ended, as unreal like the London of Part I.

### war line 427
Problem: "The card gives the rhyme" sends the reader to a card that is not at this line: the card is at 431–434 ("The last eight lines"), and it opens with this line, so say "the card at 431". Southam's printed first verse adds nothing the reader cannot see on the line, and "The bridge that crowd crossed comes back here" repeats the card's "The crowd of line 62 crossed this bridge"; Southam's note on 62, allowing an echo of the rhyme already in the crowd flowing over the bridge, is the stop's own matter and stays. The stop never says in plain words why the line is a stop on this pathway; the reason, that after the falling towers and the capitals of 374–376 it is London's own bridge that falls, is left for the reader to infer from "after 'Falling towers' and 'Vienna London'". The two hand-offs go (see above).
Corrected: The nursery rhyme is the first of the fragments, and the card at 431 gives it. Southam's note on the line prints its first verse, and his note on line 62 allows an echo of it already in the crowd flowing over London Bridge in Part I. On this pathway the line comes after the "Falling towers" of 374 and "Vienna London" at 376, so that after the capitals it is London's own bridge that falls, in a nursery tune.

Summary: 0 of 9 records accepted as they stand (the title stands, and the intro's first and third sentences); 9 objections, one on the head and one on each of the eight stops.
