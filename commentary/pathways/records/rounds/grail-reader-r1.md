## Two things asked in the instructions

**The five stops that open with "The card at this line gives..."** Three stops (0, 71, 386) open with the formula and two more (189, 424) carry it in their first sentence; 71 and 189 are consecutive stops, and so are 386 and 424. The repetition does wear. By the third the reader notices the formula before the point, and the formula puts the site's apparatus first and the poem second, in the words that sit beside the lines. Set the card aside and four of the five still say something of their own: 0 has Weston's own words and her claim that the land is the heart of the problem; 71 has the rite's question given to a man who wants the Dog kept off; 189 has Weston's castle by water and the poem's king with no wound and no castle; 424 has the knight who never comes and the one question, the king's own. 386 does not: its account of the empty chapel, the dry bones and the damp gust is the card's, and what is its own is one phrase of Weston's set against "Dry bones can harm no one" and two attributions. The corrections below drop the pointer at 0, put it inside the sentence at 71, 189 and 386, and leave 424 as it is.

**The intro and the first two stops for someone who has never heard of Weston or Frazer.** The intro does most of the work: it names both books and says what Weston argued and what Frazer supplied. But it never says who either was (Weston a scholar of the Arthurian romances, Frazer an anthropologist), never says what kind of book <i>The Golden Bough</i> is, names Cleanth Brooks without saying he was a critic, and never names the Fisher King or Frazer's gods, so that "the Fisher King" at 43, "Attis" at 55, "Osiris" at 71 and "Adonis" at 312 arrive bare. Its first sentence, the one shown alone in the corner, is two surnames to such a reader; it can stand, since the title sits above it and the second sentence can identify them, and it is the editor's own model sentence. Stop 0 makes sense only if the intro has been read; its last sentence assumes the reader has already connected the title to Weston's land, which the stop should say outright. Stop 1's first sentence, "Frazer's rites exist to bring the year back", would read to such a reader as if Frazer had rites of his own; after that sentence the stop is clear.

**Two things noticed across the whole.** Every stop is between 80 and 90 words and most are at 87 to 90, the limit rather than the target; beside the poem the notes read as uniformly heavy, and most of the corrections below are shorter. And the pathway says three times (19, 331, 353) that the freeing of the waters is the oldest form of the hero's task; once, at 19, is enough. The pathway otherwise holds together as one theme, in the poem's order, and ends where it should, with the king unhealed.

## Objections

### grail head
Problem: The why line rests on two bare surnames and near-repeats the intro's first sentence ("Weston's Grail legend and Frazer's dying god" beside "as Eliot took it from Weston and Frazer"), which the writer's brief says the intro must not do; the intro identifies neither Weston nor Frazer nor Brooks, never names the Fisher King or Frazer's gods (so those names are bare at 43, 55, 71 and 312), and at 157 words is well over the editor's "about 120".
Corrected:
why: The Grail legend behind the title, followed through the poem from the dead land of the first lines to the king fishing on the shore.
intro: The Grail legend as Eliot took it from Weston and Frazer. Eliot’s headnote to his notes credits the title, the plan and “a good deal of the incidental symbolism” to Jessie L. Weston’s <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920), a study of the Grail legend, and names beside it the anthropologist J. G. Frazer’s <i>The Golden Bough</i>. Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve an old rite, in which the wound of a king, the Fisher King, leaves his land waste and its waters dry until a knight asks the right question, so that the king is healed and the waters flow. Frazer supplied the rite, the god (Adonis, Attis, Osiris) who dies with the year and is buried or drowned so that he may return in spring. The critic Cleanth Brooks made the legend the key to the poem in 1939; in 1956 Eliot regretted having sent readers on a “wild goose chase” after the Tarot and the Grail.

### grail line 0
Problem: The last sentence is a patterned sum ("takes her title and her land; the stops say what it does with her king and his waters") of the shape the standard rejects, and the stop never says in plain words that the title is the name of Weston's land, which is the reason the pathway starts here; the opening "The card at this line gives the headnote" is the first of the five card openers.
Corrected: Weston's summary of the romances is that the object of the Grail quest is "the restoration to health and vigour of a King" whose infirmity "reacts disastrously upon his kingdom". Earlier scholars, she says, had treated the land's misfortunes as an accident of the story; for her the "Waste Land" is "the very heart of our problem", and it is that land the title names. The stops that follow say what the poem does with her king and his waters.

### grail line 1
Problem: "Frazer's rites exist to bring the year back" reads, to anyone who does not know that Frazer collected other peoples' rites, as if the rites were Frazer's own, and "bring the year back" is a flourish; Brooks is introduced a second time, which the corrected intro makes unnecessary. (Brooks's "cruelest" is his American spelling and not the poem's "cruellest" [to check that the quotation is exact]; a reader beside the poem may take it for a misquotation, so the scholar may prefer to end his quotation at "season".)
Corrected: The rites Frazer collected were performed to bring the spring back. The dead, he writes, "came back in the sprouting corn" and were imagined to return in the spring flowers, "the violets and the hyacinths", which "sprang from their dust". The poem's first voice feels that return as cruelty and prefers the "forgetful snow". Brooks (1939) wrote that the poem's people "are afraid to live in reality", so that "April, the month of rebirth, is not the most joyful season but the cruelest".

### grail line 19
Problem: "This is such a land before the giant is killed" is a sentence no one would say; the <i>Rig-Veda</i> and Indra are dropped without a word of what and who they are; and "no rain reaches it until" line 394 is stated as a fact about a land the poem leaves at line 30 (a shower falls at line 9), where the stop's real point is that this land's waters are the ones the legend's hero comes to free.
Corrected: These lines are the poem's fullest picture of the waste land, dry stone and dead tree and "no sound of water". Weston traces the Grail hero's oldest task, the freeing of the waters, to the <i>Rig-Veda</i>, whose ancient Sanskrit hymns praise the god Indra for killing the giant that had "imprisoned" the rivers: "Indra has filled the rivers, he has inundated the dry land." The waters of this land are still imprisoned, and the poem offers it no rain until the "damp gust / Bringing rain" of line 394.

### grail line 43
Problem: "The Grail's four talismans" and "the Fisher King" are dropped bare, the king for the first time in the pathway (the corrected intro names him); and the first and third sentences repeat what the card "The cards she deals" at line 46 already gives, Weston's "rise and fall of the waters" sentence and Eliot's "quite arbitrarily", so the stop's own matter, the suits and Brooks, is buried.
Corrected: Weston's chapter on the symbols holds that the four Grail talismans, Cup, Lance, Sword and Dish, survive as the four suits of the Tarot, a pack first used, she supposes, "to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land". Brooks (1939) wrote that Madame Sosostris "has fallen a long way from the high function of her predecessors". Eliot's note on line 46 ties one of her cards, the man with three staves, "quite arbitrarily" to the Fisher King.

### grail line 55
Problem: <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i> appears without Frazer's name or a word that it is his, and Attis is a bare name; nothing else is wrong.
Corrected: The missing card is the dying god's. Eliot's note on line 46 ties the Hanged Man to "the Hanged God of Frazer", a chapter of Frazer's <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i> on the god Attis, whose effigy hung on a pine and whose human representative, Frazer conjectures, was once hanged there. Weston's dead god is "not buried in the earth, but thrown into the water", and Brooks (1939) read "Fear death by water" as spoken by one who does not know "that the way into life may be by death itself".

### grail line 71
Problem: The first sentence repeats the card's account of the Osiris effigies, and calls them "corn-stuffed", the card's phrase for figures found in tombs, where Frazer's buried effigies are "made of earth and corn" [to check]; "a man who would keep the corpse in the ground, safe from the frost" passes a reading off as a fact and misreads line 73, where the frost is feared for the sprouting, not for the corpse.
Corrected: In Frazer, the Egyptian priests of Osiris buried effigies of the god made of earth and corn at the sowing and took them up with the corn "found to have sprouted from the body of Osiris", a sprouting hailed as the cause of the crops (the card at this line gives the rites). "Will it bloom this year?" is the rite's own question. The poem gives it to a man who asks, in the same speech, that the Dog be kept from digging the corpse up again.

### grail line 189
Problem: The opening clause, "The card at this line has Ferdinand and the Fisher King together", says nothing about the poem and drops Ferdinand bare; the stop's point, that the poem gives its king no wound and no castle, should be reached from the lines, not from the card.
Corrected: Brooks (1939) read the man fishing here, for "the reader who knows the Weston references", as the Fisher King (the card at this line has Ferdinand of <i>The Tempest</i>, whose lament the lines adapt). In Weston the king's sickness keeps his land waste, and his castle stands by water, "on or near the sea, or on the banks of an important river". The poem gives its king no wound and no castle, only a canal, a rat and the "White bodies naked on the low damp ground".

### grail line 202
Problem: Verlaine, Wagner's <i>Parsifal</i> and Weston's Perilous Chapel are all dropped bare, the chapel for the first time in the pathway; the first sentence repeats the card at this line, and the Weston sentence hangs loose from the two before it.
Corrected: The line is the last of Verlaine's sonnet on Wagner's Grail opera <i>Parsifal</i>, and the sonnet's tenth line is "Il a guéri le roi": he has healed the king. This is the only place where the poem touches the quest achieved, in another man's line, between Mrs Porter's soda water and the nightingale's "Jug jug". Weston herself ends her chapter on the Perilous Chapel (the chapel at line 389) with the Grail waking "to new life in the nineteenth century" through "the genius of Tennyson and Wagner".

### grail line 331
Problem: The Chapel Perilous is still unexplained here (the reader meets it at 202 and 331 before the card at 389 says what it is); the first sentence repeats the card "The road through the mountains"; and the last sentence says for the second time, after 19 and before 353, that the freeing of the waters is the legend's oldest form.
Corrected: Eliot's headnote to his notes on Part V names three themes, the second "the approach to the Chapel Perilous (see Miss Weston's book)". In Weston the chapel is a test the knight meets on his way to the Grail castle, and he rides to it through a storm; lines 331 to 359 are that road without a knight or a storm, through mountains "of rock without water" where the "dry sterile thunder without rain" is the thunder that will speak at line 400.

### grail line 353
Problem: "In Weston the oldest form of the hero's task" is the third time the pathway says this; the second sentence repeats the card at line 331 (Eliot's note, Chapman, "justly celebrated"); Ford Madox Ford is a bare name.
Corrected: The freeing of the waters is imagined here as sound only, and cancelled at line 359. The sound is the hermit-thrush's, the bird Eliot's note on line 357 names, whose "water-dripping song" the note calls justly celebrated. Eliot told the novelist Ford Madox Ford in 1923 that the poem had "about 30 good lines" and that they were "the 29 lines of the water-dripping song in the last part". The bird is in <a href="listen.html#rec-xc899696-hermit-thrush-quebec">the listening room</a>.

### grail line 386
Problem: The last sentence says the cock and the lightning are "what the land has lacked since line 24", which is not so, and claims as arrived the rain that the card at 389 says the poem does not show falling; Brooks's "perhaps also the Perilous Chapel" is said, in the scan of his essay, of "The ruined tower" of line 430 (the sentence runs "The ruined tower is, perhaps, also the Perilous Chapel, 'only the wind's home,'"), so it does not hedge about this chapel as the stop implies, and the contrast with Southam is not there [to check]; the rest of the stop repeats the card.
Corrected: Weston's Perilous Chapel (the card at line 389 gives it) is an adventure "fraught with extreme peril to life". The poem's chapel has no body, no hand and no voice, "only the wind's home", and "Dry bones can harm no one". Brooks (1939) and Southam's note both take it for Weston's chapel [to check: Brooks's "perhaps also the Perilous Chapel" is said of the ruined tower of line 430]. After the cock and the lightning comes "a damp gust / Bringing rain", the first rain offered to the dry land of line 24, though the poem does not say that it falls.
