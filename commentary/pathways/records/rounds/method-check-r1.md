## Findings

### method head
Kind: fact
Problem: "Matthiessen (1935)" carries no locator, and the intro's claim that Matthiessen "was applying them to Eliot's own work by 1935" rests on it. The book has the passage. In the scans of the second edition (achievementoftse0000foma_q8p1, achievementoftse0000foma_s5i9, achievementoftse0000foma; all three are the Oxford University Press edition of 1947 whatever their catalogue dates say, their title pages reading "1947 OXFORD UNIVERSITY PRESS" and their copyright pages "First published, 1935. Second edition, revised and enlarged, 1947"), the Dial quotation opens page 39: "remarked that: [39] THE ACHIEVEMENT OF T. S. ELIOT In using the myth, in manipulating a continuous parallel between contemporaneity and antiquity, Mr. Joyce is pursuing a method which ...", and Matthiessen's own sentence, which applies it to Eliot, runs to the foot of page 42: "... to give 'a shape and a significance to the immense panorama of futility and anarchy' of contemporary history, caused some of the experiments which he made to gain that end to appear too deliberate. Certainly some of his analogies with musical structure, in particular the summa- [43] ...". The contents page of that edition gives: I Tradition and the Individual Talent, 3; II The Problem for the Contemporary Artist, 34; III The 'Objective Correlative', 56; IV The Way a Poet Communicates His Meaning: The Auditory Imagination, 81; V The Integrity of a Work of Art, 97; VI The Sense of His Own Age, 132; VII The Plays, 155; VIII The Quartets. Pages 39–43 are therefore in chapter II, "The Problem for the Contemporary Artist". That chapter II is the 1935 text: Matthiessen's 1947 preface says "The first six chapters are still meant to stand as a whole", and C. L. Barber's preface to the 1958 edition (scans achievementoftse0000matt and others) calls them "the first six chapters, which the late F. O. Matthiessen published in 1935". No scan of the 1935 Houghton Mifflin printing itself is on archive.org (every copy listed under 1935 is the 1947 edition), so the page numbers are those of 1947 and are not for the citation; the chapter is. "By 1935" stands.
Corrected: sources line of the head: "Matthiessen (1935), ch. II" (the other two lines unchanged). The intro's text needs no change.

### method head
Kind: judgement
Problem: Two shapes the standard names. The intro's first sentence, "Old poems set in the same lines as modern scenes.", is a fragment in place of a sentence. And "Cleanth Brooks (1939) warned that the poem's parallels are often contrasts and its contrasts parallels" makes a warning of what Brooks states as the poem's method, and adds "often": his sentence (Modern Poetry and the Tradition, scan modernpoetrytrad0000clea_x3i8) is "The poet works in terms of surface parallelisms which in reality make ironical contrasts, and in terms of surface contrasts which in reality constitute parallelisms." The attribution and the sense are right; the verb is the writer's.
Corrected: "Old poems are set in the same lines as modern scenes." and "Cleanth Brooks (1939) held that the poem works by surface parallels that are really contrasts and surface contrasts that are really parallels, and the stops do not assume that the past comes off better."

### method line 176
Kind: judgement
Problem: (a) "Spenser himself names Elizabeth": what Spenser writes is "And great Elisaes glorious name may ring" (Wikisource text, line 157), the queen under her poetic name, and the poem's title page names a different Elizabeth, the Lady Elizabeth Somerset, one of the two brides. Southam's note ("Elizabeth, by name, and Leicester, as 'that great Lord', are referred to in stanzas 8 and 9") is correctly reported, but a reader who opens the card and looks for "Elizabeth" will find "Elisa". "That great lord" (line 139, lower-case "lord" in the Wikisource copy the site cites) is exact. (b) The locator "132–162" begins in the middle of stanza 8 (line 127 "At length they all to mery London came"); Southam refers to stanzas 8 and 9, which are lines 127–162.
Corrected: "Spenser himself names Elizabeth, as Elisa, and, as 'that great lord', Leicester (so Southam), the pair in the barge at 279." Sources: "Spenser, Prothalamion 1–36 and 127–162".
