### east head
Kind: fact
Problem: "Part V's title and last lines come from the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, where the thunder's one syllable is heard as three commands and 'Shantih', repeated, closes a scripture." The "where" clause puts the closing formula in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka. In Müller's translation, the site's text of the Upanishad (Sacred Books of the East 15, https://archive.org/details/p2upanishads00mluoft), the Bṛhadāraṇyaka ends with the list of teachers, "47. Pragâpati from Brahman, 48. Brahman is Svayambhû, self-existent. Adoration to Brahman!" (VI Adhyâya, 5 Brâhmana, 4), with no peace formula; in the same volume "Om! Peace! peace! peace!" closes the Kaṭha (its last paragraph, numbered 19) and opens and closes the vallîs of the Taittirîyaka. Eliot's note says only "a formal ending to an Upanishad", and the stop at 434 gets this right.
Corrected: Part V’s title and last lines come from the <i>Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad</i>, where the thunder’s one syllable is heard as three commands; “Shantih”, repeated, is the formula that closes an Upanishad.

### east line 173
Kind: fact
Problem: "says that all things are on fire, eye, ear, tongue, body and mind" leaves out the nose. Warren §73 (refs/warren.txt, p. 352): "The eye, O priests, is on fire; forms are on fire; … The ear is on fire; sounds are on fire; … the nose is on fire; odors are on fire; … the tongue is on fire; tastes are on fire; … the body is on fire; things tangible are on fire; … the mind is on fire; ideas are on fire". The list reads as complete, so the sermon's six senses should all be there. The quoted words "with the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation" are exact.
Corrected: Part III's title is the Buddha's. His Fire Sermon, preached at Gaya, says that all things are on fire, eye, ear, nose, tongue, body and mind, each burning "with the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation" (Warren's translation, which Eliot's note on line 308 names). The title comes first and the sermon is heard last, at line 308; between them come the river, Sweeney and Mrs. Porter, the merchant, the typist and the Thames-daughters.

Kind: fact
Problem: The sources cite "Southam, Guide, note on line 308", but Southam has no note keyed to line 308. A scan search of the 1994 Guide finds no "308" at all (`ol.py '"308" Burning Southam Buddha'` returns nothing, where the same form finds his notes "l. 401 Datta: give", "l. 411 Dayadhvam", "l. 418 Damyata", "l. 395 Ganga"); his only "Burning" is in a remark on a draft fragment ("a litany around 'burning'"). ### east line 327
Kind: judgement
Problem: "in the lines that look back on the Passion without naming it" states a reading as a fact, with no owner and no source (the stop has none). It is the site's own reading, on the card "torchlight" at 322–330 ("Lines 322 to 330 look back on the arrest, trial and death of Christ without naming him"), and it is the common one; but under the standard a stop with no sources should rest on the poem alone, and this clause does not. Pointing at the card keeps the stop source-free and honest.
Corrected: Part V is called What the Thunder Said, and this is its first thunder, "of spring over distant mountains", in the lines that the card at line 322 reads as the Passion; the second, at line 342, is "dry sterile thunder without rain". The thunder is heard twice before it speaks. When it speaks, at line 400, it will speak in Sanskrit.

### east line 396
Kind: judgement
Problem: "One more fact is worth weighing here." is an aside to the reader of the kind the standard rejects (a knowing wink; a sentence that tells the reader to weigh rather than telling them the fact). The fact itself is verified: Weston's chapter III opens with the Rig-Veda and Indra as "our nearest possible approach" to the beginning, and quotes "Indra has filled the rivers, he has inundated the dry land"; "the feat by which the Grail heroes, Gawain and Perceval, rejoiced the hearts of a suffering folk, i.e., the restoration of the rivers to their channels, the 'Freeing of the Waters'". "The thunder god" is fair to Weston, who says Indra "wields the thunderbolt certainly" and keeps "traces of his 'weather' origin" (same chapter).
Corrected: The poem goes to India for its rain. The Ganges is low, black clouds gather over Himavant, the Himalaya, and the jungle waits "humped in silence"; the card at this line gives the names. The rain the whole poem has wanted gathers in the country of the Upanishads. The first evidence Weston gives for the "Freeing of the Waters", the Grail hero's task, is the <i>Rig-Veda</i>'s Indra, the thunder god who "has inundated the dry land" (chapter III).

### east line 396
Kind: judgement
Problem: The Southam citations on this pathway (396, 398, 400–402, 412, 419, 433, 434, and the head) use the site's line numbers, but Southam keys his notes to the Faber count, which runs one behind the site's from line 346. The 1994 Guide (scan snippets) has "l. 395 Ganga: the familiar and affectionate name used in India…", "l. 397 Himavant: a holy mountain in the Himalaya range", "ll. 399–401: Eliot refers us…" (the thunder fable, with "Eliot's departure from the order of the legend, in placing Damyata last"), "l. 401 Datta: give", "l. 411 Dayadhvam: Eliot translates this as 'sympathize'", "l. 418 Damyata: Eliot translates this as 'control'", and the Shantih note, which Southam himself calls "the note to line 433". The rule (STANDARD.md, CITATIONS-BRIEF.md, works.txt) is "the line its note is keyed to", which literally means Southam's numbers; the site converts Eliot's own notes to its count but says nothing about commentaries. Either form is checkable; the editor should choose one for the whole site and say so in the Southam record in works.txt. The citation "note on line 308" is a separate matter (see line 308): no such note exists in either count.
Corrected: If Southam's own keys are used: 396 "Southam, Guide, note on line 395" and "Southam, Guide, note on line 397"; 400, 402 and 433 "Southam, Guide, note on lines 399–401"; 412 "Southam, Guide, note on line 411"; 419 "Southam, Guide, note on line 418"; 434 "Southam, Guide, note on line 433". If the site's count is kept, leave the draft's lines as they are and add to the works.txt record for Southam: "cited by the site's line; Southam's own keys run one behind from line 346".

### east line 400
Kind: judgement
Problem: "The card tells the fable" and "Eliot's note numbers the passage 5, 1": the card is "da", at lines 401–402, not at this line, and it already records the numbering ("Eliot's note numbers it '5, 1.'"); glosses-index.txt asks that a stop point at a card rather than repeat it. What the stop adds is new and verified (Deussen 1921, p. 489, running head "Brihadāranyaka-Upanishad 5,1.", the Zweites Brāhmanam beginning on that page). The order of the three words in the Upanishad (gods Damyata, men Datta, Asuras Dayadhvam) is verified in Müller and Deussen, and "a departure Southam records" is right (1994: "Eliot's departure from the order of the legend, in placing Damyata last, has given rise…"; 1974: "Eliot departs from the order of the legend, which reads Damyata, Datta, Dayadhvam").
Corrected: The card at line 401 tells the fable from <i>Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad</i> V.2, one syllable from Prajāpati heard as three commands, and records Eliot's numbering, 5, 1. His note cites page 489 of Paul Deussen's German translation; in the 1921 printing the fable begins on that page, under the running head 5, 1. The poem changes the order. The Upanishad has the gods' Damyata first and the demons' Dayadhvam last; the poem begins with the men's Datta and ends with the gods' word, a departure Southam records.

### east line 434
Kind: fact
Problem: "with the note's rewording in 1925" is wrong. The site's own copy of Poems 1909–1925 (Faber and Gwyer; the scan bwb_KR-589-929 is the second impression, April 1926) prints the note unchanged: "433. Shantih. Repeated as here, a formal ending to an Upanishad. 'The Peace which passeth understanding' is a feeble translation of the content of this word." Ricks and McCue's textual note (poemsoftseliot0000elio, scan search for "feeble translation of the content" with "our equivalent") reads: "our equivalent to] 1932+ || a feeble translation of the content of Boni, Hogarth, 1925". The wording "is our equivalent to this word" appears in the Faber resetting of Poems 1909–1925 of 1932 (poems0000unse_n9b4, and its 1934 reprint poems19091925030616mbp, both showing the page as 119 in the snippet) and in Collected Poems 1909–1935 (1936). So the citation "Poems 1909–1925, the note on Shantih" supports the opposite of what the stop says. The design file for the passion pathway (design/passion.md) carries the same "1925", so its stop at 434 needs the same correction.
Corrected: Shantih is peace. Eliot's note calls it, repeated three times, "a formal ending to an Upanishad"; Southam gives the full formula, "Om shantih shantih shantih", and Müller's volume prints it, closing the Kaṭha Upaniṣad, as "Om! Peace! peace! peace!" What the poem takes here is not a passage but the way a scripture closes. Eliot's gloss, "The Peace which passeth understanding", is the Prayer Book's blessing; the pathway on the Bible and the Prayer Book takes that up, with the rewording of the note from 1932. — Sources: replace "Poems 1909–1925, the note on Shantih" with "Ricks and McCue, commentary on line 434" (their textual note on the line; or "line 433" if the editor adopts the commentaries' own keys).
