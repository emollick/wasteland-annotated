# Check of the Wagner pathway (drafts/wagner.txt), round 1

Checked 20 September 2026 against poem.txt, eliot-notes.txt, works.txt, new-works.txt, refs/weston.txt, the librettos at librettoarchive.com (Tristan Act 1 and Act 3 pages, Parsifal Act 1 to Act 3 pages, the Götterdämmerung page), Larisch's My Past (archive.org cu31924028064792, djvu text), Verlaine's sonnet on fr.wikisource, the 1911 Britannica on Wikisource, the site's listen.html, and the scans through ol.py (Letters vol. 1, Southam 1968/1974/1990/1994/1996, Brooks 1939, Crawford 2015/2016, Ricks and McCue, Rainey).

Every quotation from the poem matches poem.txt; every reference to Eliot's notes matches eliot-notes.txt (notes on 31, 42, 202, 266, 424). Nothing of Eliot's after 1929 is quoted. The three listening-room anchors exist on listen.html (rec-tristan-1928-bayreuth-act1-sailor, rec-tristan-1928-bayreuth-act3-shepherd, rec-gotterdammerung-solti-1964-rhinemaidens), and the page's own descriptions agree with the stops (Bayreuth, July 1928, Columbia 78s; Solti, recorded 1964).

validate.py flags two things only, both warnings the writer rightly left: the quotations at 42 ("simply an extension of the last figure") and 202 ("ironically of another sort of foot-washing") are not in the poem because they are Brooks's words; both are verified below.

## Accepted as they stand

- head. All checked. Weston's preface: "During the Bayreuth Festival of 1911 I had frequent opportunities of meeting, and discussion with, Professor von Schroeder. I owe to him ... references which have been of the greatest assistance ... both of which have furnished important links in the chain of evidence" (refs/weston.txt, Preface, signed Paris, October 1919). Chapter XIII's last sentence: "it woke to new life in the nineteenth century, making its fresh appeal through the genius of Tennyson and Wagner." Open point (1), the Verdenal letter, is settled: the scans of Letters vol. 1 (1988 ed., lettersoftseliot0000elio_r8o7, leaf 710; rev. ed., lettersoftseliot0000elio, leaf 952, and lettersoftseliot0000elio_h3h2, leaf 954) head the letter "FROM Jean Verdenal   MS Houghton   Lundi 5 Février 1912   151 bis [rue St Jacques, Paris]", and the same leaf carries "Tristan and Isolde is terribly moving at the first hearing, and leaves you prostrate with ecstasy and thirsting to get back to it again" (the French on the same leaf: "Tristan et Y., du premier coup vous émeuvent atrocement"). Seymour-Jones's notes cite the same letter as "Jean Verdenal to TSE, 5 February 1912, Houghton". The citation "Letters, vol. 1, Jean Verdenal to Eliot, 5 February 1912" is right in fact and in form. The quoted phrase is five words of the editors' translation, within the rights rule. (The phrase "worn down to 'la la' at 306" is a reading; see the judgement under line 306.)

The libretto (librettoarchive, Dritter Aufzug, Vorspiel und erste Scene): "Die drei Rheintöchter ... tauchen aus der Fluth auf, und schwimmen, wie im Reigentanze, im Kreise umher"; "Frau Sonne sendet lichte Strahlen; Nacht liegt in der Tiefe: einst war sie hell, da heil und hehr des Vaters Gold noch in ihr glänzte!"; Siegfried offers the ring ("Kommt rasch! ## Findings

### wagner line 8
Kind: fact
Problem: Two of the five citations are wrong; the text itself checks out.
(a) "Southam, Guide, headnote to The Waste Land" is the wrong locator. In the cited 6th edition (1994; scan studentsguidetos0000sout_t0e0, leaf 294) the report of Knust is inside the note keyed "ll. 8–22" ("8–22: in 1935, F. O. Matthiessen made the suggestion that the 'heap of broken images' (line 22) in these ..."), which continues "At some time between 1913 and 1920–1, Eliot met the Countess Marie von Wallersee-Larisch, an Austrian noblewoman, a niece and favourite ..." and "(I must record a different view – argued by Herbert Knust in Wagner, The King and 'The Waste Land' (1967), where he proposes that My Past plays an important part in The Waste Land and ... Eliot's use of My Past (1913) by the Countess Marie Larisch, from which come several details in these lines, is discussed in the Introduction". The same note sits between "l. 8 Starnbergersee: a fashionable cosmopolitan lake-resort just south of Munich, visited by Eliot in August 1911" and "l. 10 Hofgarten". (In the 5th edition, 1990, studentssguideto0000sout leaf 232, and the 1974 edition, studentsguidetos0000sout leaf 166, the same note is keyed "ll. 8–18"; the site cites the 6th.) Open point (2) is thus settled: "note on lines 8–22".
(b) "Crawford, Young Eliot" has no locator, and nothing in the stop rests on it. Searches of both scans of Young Eliot (youngeliotfromst0000craw_p9b7, 2015; youngeliotfromst0000craw_c9y9, 2016) for "Starnbergersee" (limit 100), "Starnberger See" Eliot, "Larisch" Eliot (limit 100), "Marie Larisch" (limit 100), "Countess Marie Larisch", "King Ludwig" Eliot, and "Ludwig" drowned Munich return no hit in Crawford. (Crawford does quote Verdenal's "prostrate with ecstasy", leaf 538, but that belongs to the head, which cites the Letters directly, as it should.) A citation without a locator that supports no sentence breaks the rule in STANDARD.md and CITATIONS-BRIEF.md; remove it, or, if the writer had a passage in mind, cite it by chapter.
The facts stand on the other three citations, and the open points (3) and (4) are settled in the stop's favour:
- Point (4): Larisch, My Past, ch. VII ("The Mad King of Bavaria"), archive.org cu31924028064792: "as a member of the family I can most emphatically state that this was not the case. The King's madness assumed such a grave aspect that it became absolutely necessary to put him under restraint ... he chose death as a means of escape ... No one actually knows what happened on that last walk which he and his doctor took by the lake of Starnberg, but in the morning when the dead bodies were found, the King was holding the doctor down under the water ... Ludwig II died on June 13, 1886". So "put under restraint as mad", the drowning, the date and "counted herself one of his family" are all in ch. VII. Ch. I gives "Wagner's patron": "my first meeting with Richard Wagner, who, as is well known, owed his ultimate recognition as a genius to the kindness and patronage of Ludwig II." Both Larisch citations are used.
- Point (3): the 1911 Britannica article (Wikisource, 1911_Encyclopædia_Britannica/Wagner,_Wilhelm_Richard): "In 1863 he published the libretto of Der Ring des Nibelungen. King Ludwig of Bavaria was much struck with it, and in 1864 invited Wagner, who was then at Stuttgart, to come to Munich and finish his work there." and "On the 10th of June 1865 at Munich, Tristan und Isolde was produced for the first time". Both facts supported. The article is in vol. 28: Wikisource heads it "1911 Encyclopædia Britannica, Volume 28", the volume is "Vol 28 VETCH to ZYMOTIC DISEASES", and the transcription is proofread from the volume's scan, whose page Page:EB1911_-_Volume_28.djvu/252 carries the printed running head "236 WAGNER" and the article's opening "WAGNER, WILHELM RICHARD (1813-1883), German dramatic composer, poet and essay-writer, was born at Leipzig on the 22nd of May 1813." (See New works for the corrected entry.)

### wagner line 8
Kind: judgement
Problem: "Herbert Knust (1967) argued that her book, with its drowned king, shapes much of the poem" is reported through Southam, and Southam's words are that Knust "proposes that My Past plays an important part in The Waste Land" (6th ed., note on lines 8–22, as above). "Shapes much of the poem" is a fair paraphrase; "with its drowned king" is the writer's gloss, not Southam's, though Knust's title has "the King". Knust's book itself cannot be verified by the site's method: Open Library records it (Herbert Knust, Wagner, the King, and The Waste Land, The Pennsylvania State University Studies, no. 22, University Park: Pennsylvania State University, 1967, 87 pp., LCCN 68007279) with no scan, so the stop is right to cite Southam as the reporter rather than Knust, and the sentence says so. The editor may prefer wording closer to Southam.
Corrected: (optional) "Herbert Knust (1967) argued that her book plays an important part in the poem; Southam's Guide records the argument." Sources as corrected above; no Knust entry needed unless he is cited directly (a record is offered under New works).

### wagner line 31

Problem: "the young sailor sings it unseen, from the mast": the stage direction is "STIMME EINES JUNGEN SEEMANNS (aus der Höhe, wie vom Mast her, vernehmbar)", audible from above, as if from the mast (librettoarchive, Tristan Act 1, opening). Corrected: "In the opera the young sailor sings it unseen, from aloft as if from the mast, as the ship carries Isolde to her marriage with the King of Cornwall; ..." Sources unchanged.

### wagner line 42
Kind: judgement
Problem: Two small things. Kurwenal is named without a word of who he is (STANDARD: no names dropped without a word of who or what they are); he is Tristan's retainer, tending the wounded Tristan. And the line is not strictly the answer to the question: Kurwenal asks "Sahst du noch nichts? Kein Schiff noch auf der See?", the shepherd answers that Kurwenal would have heard a different tune if so, and then, told "Eifrig späh; und siehst du ein Schiff, so spiele lustig und hell!", he looks out to sea and reports "Öd und leer das Meer!" (librettoarchive, Tristan Act 3, first scene). The Brooks quotation is exact: "'Oed' und leer das Meer,' seems at first to be simply an extension of the last figure: that is, 'Empty and wide the sea [of ..." (modernpoetrytrad0000broo_f5w0 leaf 278; modernpoetrytrad0000clea_x3i8 leaf 258), and Brooks does treat the two Tristan quotations together in that paragraph.
Corrected: "The shepherd's report to Kurwenal, Tristan's retainer, who has asked whether any ship is yet on the sea and bids him keep watch. With it the two quotations ..." Sources unchanged.

### wagner line 277
Kind: judgement
Problem: The stop is right in substance, but the cited text does not show the poem's words, and the editor should know why. On the librettoarchive page the Rhine-daughters' vocalise is printed in the score's expanded form: "(Sie schließen wieder den Schwimmreigen.) Weialala, weialala heia leia wallala la / heia la la lei la la la la la la lei, / walla la la la weia la wallala weia la / la la wallala la la leia leia leia leia la la la!", and at the scene's end "Weialala weialala / leia leia wallala la la lei ...". The poem's exact "Weialala leia / Wallala leialala" is the form of the standard printed librettos: "(Sie schliessen wieder den Schwimmreigen). Weialala leia, wallala leialala. (Ferner Hornruf ...)" and, at the end of the scene, "DIE RHEINTÖCHTER Weialala leia, Wallala leialala. SIEGFRIED Im Wasser wie am Lande ..." (Götterdämmerung = Crépuscule des dieux, 1972, gotterdammerungc0000wagn, leaf 326; the 1985 English National Opera libretto, twilightofgodsgo0000wagn, leaf 134, the same). Wagner's own printed poem has none of these syllables: in Gesammelte Schriften und Dichtungen, Bd. 6 (1872; archive.org 11121452bsb), Der Ring des Nibelungen (1873; 11081625bsb) and Trilogie 4 (1874; 11185940bsb) the song runs straight from "Rhein-Gold, klares Gold! Wie hell strahltest du einst, hehrer Stern der Tiefe!" to "Frau Sonne, sende uns den Helden". The vocalise came with the music, which strengthens the stop's point that the syllables are sound, not words. Recommendation for the citations thread: the works.txt note on Wagner, Götterdämmerung should say that the librettoarchive text prints the refrain in the score's expanded form and that the poem's "Weialala leia / Wallala leialala" is the printed librettos' form, absent from Wagner's 1872 poem.
Corrected: (optional) "... since the syllables are sound, not words, the voice Wagner gave the river when he set the scene; they are not in his printed poem of 1872 and came with the music. The poem uses them twice ..." Sources unchanged.

### wagner line 306
Kind: judgement
Problem: "the refrain does not come back whole; two syllables stand for it, the last two of 'leialala'" is a reading stated as a fact, and the head repeats it ("worn down to 'la la' at 306"). Eliot's note on 266 assigns lines 292 to 306 inclusive to the Thames-daughters speaking in turn, so that "la la" is a remnant of "Weialala leia / Wallala leialala" is an inference, however plain. It is the usual one (Nims's Harper Anthology, for instance, glosses "306 la la: a tired echo of the Rhine-daughters singing"), but searches of the Southam, Ricks and McCue and Rainey scans ("la la" with Weialala, Rhine-daughters, Rhinedaughters, Rhinemaidens, Thames-daughters, 306) found no note on 306 to name as owner. Mark it as the usual reading.
Corrected: stop: "After the three daughters have spoken in turn, the last of them on Margate Sands, the refrain does not come back whole; the two syllables here are usually read as what is left of it, the last two of 'leialala'. Nothing of Wagner follows, ..." Head: "... set twice on the Thames (277–278, 290–291) and, as it is usually read, worn down to 'la la' at 306." Sources unchanged.

### wagner line 424
Kind: judgement
Problem: "healed in the third act, the act of Verlaine's sonnet at 202": the sonnet's octave is Act II (Parsifal "a vaincu les Filles", "la Femme belle", "l'Enfer") and its sestet Act III ("Il a guéri le roi, le voici roi lui-même ... Et, ô ces voix d'enfants chantant dans la coupole !"), so Act III is the act in which the sonnet ends, not its act. The rest checks: Parsifal Act I, Gurnemanz on Amfortas, "den heil'gen Speer hat er entwandt ... doch – eine Wunde brannt' ihm in der Seite: die Wunde ist's, die nie sich schliessen will"; Act III, "(Parsifal ... streckt den Speer aus, mit dessen Spitze er Amfortas' Seite berührt) ... die Wunde schliesst der Speer nur, der sie schlug"; Eliot's note on 424 names Weston's chapter on the Fisher King (her ch. IX); ch. XIII ends with Tennyson and Wagner as quoted. A fact the editor may want for this pathway, seen on fr.wikisource in the 1905 Vanier (Messein) printing of Amour (p. 42): the poem printed immediately before "Parsifal" in Amour is Verlaine's sonnet "À Louis II de Bavière", which ties the lake of line 8 to the Grail opera of 202 and 424.
Corrected: "... Wagner's Amfortas in <i>Parsifal</i>, wounded by the holy spear and healed in the third act, where Verlaine's sonnet at 202 ends, when Parsifal touches the wound with it." Sources unchanged.
