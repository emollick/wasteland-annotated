# Reader's report on the pathway "dante" (Dante and the dead), round 1

Read as the brief asks: STANDARD.md, then drafts/dante.txt with poem.txt open, the lines at each stop before the note. I also read the full text of the marginal cards the stops point at (from the site's js/data.js), since the index gives only their first 160 characters, and checked the three open pre-1930 quotations the notes make from Butler's Odyssey and the Dante essay in The Sacred Wood; all three are exact.

## Accepted as they stand

None of the eleven records as written. The matter of every stop is sound and in order, the theme is honestly in the poem at each line, and the pathway holds together (see the head section below); every objection is to wording, to what a stop leaves to the card, or, at 328, to placement. Several are small.

## The two questions asked

Stops that begin "The card gives" or "The card tells", read without the card open: at line 0, "The dedication card gives the line" leaves the reader not knowing what line, or that "il miglior fabbro" is Dante's; the rest of the stop can be followed only by a reader who already sees that "the better craftsman" translates the dedication. At 293, "The card gives La Pia's line" can be followed in outline (a reader learns that the poem's line translates hers and that she asks to be remembered) but not who she is. At 312, "The card gives the draft" comes last, after three sentences that repeat the card, so the stop can be followed but adds little. At 412, "The card tells Ugolino's story" cannot be followed at all without the card: nothing in the stop says who Ugolino is or what he has to do with a key turning in a door. The stop at 328 refers to "the card at 322", which covers 322–330 and is on the page at 328 and at 329; that one can be followed.

The stop placed at 328: it belongs at 329. Opened at 328, the reader sees "He who was living is now dead", the one line the note says it is not about ("this pathway stops only for line 329"); everything it discusses, "we", "were living" and the past tense, is at 329. Moved there, its "the lines before are the Passion" also takes in 328 correctly, as the card does ("The lines stop at the death: 'He who was living is now dead.'").

### dante head
Problem: The why line, shown under the title everywhere, drops "Arnaut" bare, and "the cantos of Dante that Eliot's notes point to" is not what the pathway does at three of its ten stops (69, 245 and 312 go where the notes do not); it also shares "in the refining fire" with the intro. In the intro, Ugolino, La Pia and Arnaut Daniel come without a word of who they are, and the last clause, "what he said in 1950 ... is described", is a rights note a reader has no use for. Read through, the pathway does hold together as one theme (the dead met, named, speaking and asking remembrance) and the stops answer the intro's question in order; the title is right.
Corrected:
title: Dante and the dead
intro: The poem’s dead, and what they keep of Dante’s. Five of Eliot’s notes send the reader to Dante, more than to any other writer: to the <i>Inferno</i> for the crowd on London Bridge (the notes on lines 63 and 64) and for Count Ugolino, starved in his tower (412), and to the <i>Purgatorio</i> for La Pia, a murdered wife (293), and for the troubadour Arnaut Daniel in the refining fire (428), the canto from which the dedication to Pound is also taken. Dante’s dead, met and known by name, tell the living who they were and ask to be remembered. This pathway stops at the poem’s dead in order and asks what each passage keeps of that. Eliot’s essay on Dante in <i>The Sacred Wood</i> (1920) and his <i>Dante</i> of 1929 are quoted where they bear on a line, and his lecture of 1950 on what Dante meant to him is drawn on at line 62.
why: The poem’s dead taken in order and set beside Dante’s, from the crowd on London Bridge to the poet Arnaut Daniel in the fire of Purgatory.

### dante line 0
Problem: Without the card, "gives the line" has no line, and the stop never says that the dedication is a quotation from Dante, so "the better craftsman" must be matched to "il miglior fabbro" by the reader; "then he asks Dante for a paternoster when he reaches heaven" leaves both pronouns open. The first and last sentences repeat what the dedication card already says (Guinizelli pointing to Arnaut; the canto's return at 428), and "so the canto at the front of the poem returns at its end" ties a bow. The fish simile is in quotation marks with no translator named: if it is a published translation, name it and restore the marks; otherwise it reads better as the plain gloss below.
Corrected: The dedication, "il miglior fabbro", the better craftsman, is Dante's; the card gives the line (<i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI 117). In the fire that purges the lustful, the poet Guido Guinizelli points Dante to the troubadour Arnaut Daniel with these words, asks Dante to say a paternoster for him in heaven, and vanishes into the fire like a fish going through water to the bottom (XXVI 127–135). Arnaut's own plea, at the end of the canto, gives the poem its last words from Dante, at line 428, where this pathway ends.

### dante line 62
Problem: The second sentence restates the card at 63, which already has Eliot's 1950 remark about the references and his "city clerks trooping over London Bridge from the railway station to their offices"; so half the stop adds nothing to what the page shows. The stop's own point, that both places are short of Hell's torments, is stated and then dropped, so the reader is not told what the two cantos do for the pathway. "The neutrals" wants a word of who they are.
Corrected: Eliot's notes send this crowd to two places at the edge of Dante's Hell, short of its torments: the neutrals, who lived without praise or blame, whom heaven casts out and deep Hell will not receive (<i>Inferno</i> III 34–42), and Limbo, where the unbaptised sigh in "grief without torments" (IV 28). Neither is a place of punishment. The crowd here is undone and sighing, "so many", and no one in it named until Stetson at line 69. The card has what Eliot said of this crowd in 1950.

### dante line 69
Problem: The likeness to the visible line (one known face in the crowd, stopped and hailed) is left for the reader to infer; "'Are you here, ser Brunetto?' (XV 23–30), Brunetto Latini, who had been his teacher (XV 82–85)" is an apposition across a citation that has to be read twice; "The comparison with Stetson is a reader's" is confusing on a site that calls its user the reader (it means: not Eliot's); A. C. Charity and Ethel Stephenson come with no word of who they are. The quotation from The Sacred Wood is exact.
Corrected: To know a face among the dead and stop it is Dante's scene. In <i>Inferno</i> XV a burnt figure catches Dante by the hem; it is Brunetto Latini, his old teacher, and Dante asks "Are you here, ser Brunetto?" (XV 23–30, 82–85). In <i>The Sacred Wood</i> (1920) Eliot placed the passage's emotion in "Brunetto's excellence in damnation". The comparison with Stetson is a critic's, A. C. Charity's (1974); when Ethel Stephenson, writing for lay readers in the 1940s, put it to Eliot, he said it had not occurred to him.

### dante line 193
Problem: The second sentence runs three clauses and a twenty-word parenthesis on semicolons and cannot be taken in on one reading with the lines open. "The fisher" is not explained (the reader has "While I was fishing"). "The poem's dead are not buried" is more absolute than the poem, which has "tumbled graves" at 388. And the stop, the only one with no Dante in it, never says why these bones are on this pathway; the last fact, that Ferdinand's father is alive, is left without its point.
Corrected: Dante's dead have their places; few of the poem's are buried. Part I is named for the burial service, and its one buried body, the corpse, may be dug up again (71–75, Cornelia's dirge in Webster's <i>The White Devil</i> over her murdered son, for "the friendless bodies of unburied men"). The bones here are rattled "year to year"; a current picks Phlebas's (316). The man fishing at line 189 has mourned "the king my father's death" in Ferdinand's words from <i>The Tempest</i>; Ferdinand's father, thought drowned, is alive.

### dante line 245
Problem: Tiresias is named for the first time on this pathway without a word of who he is (the intro does not mention him, and the card that explains him is at 218, not here); "where he told the king what he could not see" makes the reader sort two "he"s. Otherwise the stop is clear and says why the line is here; Butler's words are exact.
Corrected: Tiresias, the blind seer, gives two credentials. Thebes is the city of Sophocles' <i>Oedipus the King</i>, where Tiresias told Oedipus what the king could not see. The walk among the dead is <i>Odyssey</i> XI, where Odysseus summons the shades with blood; Circe has told him that Tiresias alone keeps his understanding in death, "but the other ghosts flit about aimlessly" (X 490–495, in Butler's translation). He is the only speaker who says he has been among the dead and come back, as Dante has in the <i>Commedia</i>.

### dante line 293
Problem: Without the card the reader is not told who La Pia is or that Eliot's note on this line quotes her, and "the Thames-daughter" is a name the pathway has not used before (it is Eliot's, in his note on 266). The matter is good, and the point about disfare is the best thing on the pathway; the last sentence gives the same words as the card's translation, and is kept only because it is Eliot's.
Corrected: Eliot's note gives La Pia's line (<i>Purgatorio</i> V), a murdered wife's account of herself; the card has the passage. She begins with a request, "ricorditi di me", remember me, as Dante's dead often do (Guinizelli and Arnaut at XXVI 130 and 147). The first of Eliot's three Thames-daughters, speaking here, asks nothing. "Undid me" translates "disfecemi", and the same verb, disfare, is behind "undone" at line 63, where death has "disfatta" the crowd (<i>Inferno</i> III 57). "Siena made me, Maremma unmade me" is Eliot's own translation, of 1929.

### dante line 312
Problem: Three of the four sentences restate the draft card on this line, which already has the Ulysses-canto remark, Pound's cutting of the voyage and both quoted letters; "The card gives the draft." is a four-word tail. Only the account of Dante's Ulysses is the stop's own, and it never says what Ulysses, one of Dante's dead telling his own death from his flame, has to do with Phlebas, who is told about, so the reader does not see why the line is on this pathway.
Corrected: The card gives the draft, some ninety lines of voyage and wreck, and Eliot's remark of 1959 that it was "rather inspired" by the Ulysses canto of the <i>Inferno</i>. There Ulysses, one of Dante's dead, tells from his flame how his ship, in sight of the mountain of Purgatory, was spun three times by a whirlwind and went down as the sea closed over it (XXVI 133–142). Pound cut the voyage and, in January 1922, insisted that Phlebas stay. Ulysses tells his own death; Phlebas has his told for him.

### dante line 328
Problem: The stop belongs at 329 (see above): at 328 the reader is opened on the line the note declines to discuss. In the wording, "So far the poem has looked at its dead from outside" does not survive the reader's memory of 293, where the first Thames-daughter has just spoken of herself in La Pia's past tense, and of 245, where Tiresias speaks; "gives the living the past tense in which Dante's dead speak of their lives" is hard to parse; and "Siena mi fé" is left untranslated here though 293 gave Eliot's rendering. The comparison is rightly marked as the pathway's own.
Corrected (line: 329): Here the speaker counts himself among the dying. The crowd at line 63 was undone and seen from outside; now "we" are dying, and "were living" puts the living in the past tense in which Dante's dead tell their lives, as in La Pia's "Siena mi fé", Siena made me (<i>Purgatorio</i> V 134). The lines before, "He who was living is now dead" among them, are the Passion, as the card says; this stop is for line 329 alone, and the comparison with Dante's grammar is the pathway's own.

### dante line 412
Problem: A reader who has not opened the card cannot follow the stop: it never says who Ugolino is, or that Eliot's note sends the key turning in the door to his tower, so "He is in the ninth and lowest circle" has no antecedent the reader knows. The rest is the stop's own, and the reading at the end is properly marked as the pathway's.
Corrected: Eliot's note sends the key to Count Ugolino, starved in a tower at Pisa with his sons (<i>Inferno</i> XXXIII 46); the card tells the story. He is in the ninth and lowest circle, in ice among the traitors, gnawing the skull of the archbishop who starved him (XXXIII 1–3 and 14). He speaks so that his words may be "seed" bearing infamy for his enemy (7–9). The thunder's word means sympathise, Eliot's note says; in this pathway's reading the poem answers from the place in Dante furthest from pity.

### dante line 428
Problem: "then hid himself in the fire that refines them" is in quotation marks with no owner, and the stop goes on to say Eliot made his own translation of the passage, which this is not; "Ara Vos Prec" is not glossed or tied to the plea Eliot's note prints, so the reader does not see that the title is this speech; "the line ends a list of what the next world does to Dante's souls" cannot be followed without the essay open (the list is of how each soul's place colours it, and the Italian line closes it, which is right); and the close chains two quotations on a colon. One fact looks wrong to me: the 1920 volume's title as printed is "Ara Vus Prec", the spelling Eliot let stand, not "Ara Vos Prec" [to check against Gallup A4].
Corrected: The note gives the lines. Arnaut Daniel asks Dante to remember his pain and hides in the refining fire. Eliot had taken Arnaut's first words, now I pray you, as the title of his 1920 volume, "Ara Vus Prec" [to check]. In <i>The Sacred Wood</i> the line closes a list of what the next world does to Dante's souls, "and the errors of Arnaut are corrected". In <i>Dante</i> (1929), translating it, he wrote that purgatory's flame, unlike hell's, "is deliberately and consciously accepted by the penitent", and "In their suffering is hope".
