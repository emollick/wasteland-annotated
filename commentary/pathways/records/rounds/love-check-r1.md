### love line 35
Kind: fact
Problem: "Lyndall Gordon reads the girl as Emily Hale", cited to "Gordon, The Hyacinth Girl", rests on a book nobody opened. No scan of The Hyacinth Girl (2022) exists on archive.org (a search for its title and subtitle finds nothing). The Gordon that is scanned does not carry the reading: Eliot's Early Years (1977, eliotsearlyyears0000gord_o1b6) mentions the hyacinth garden only among Eliot's "beatific moments" ("in the hyacinth garden 'looking into the heart of light, the silence'") and, of Hale, says "Helen Gardner suggests that it was she who inspired Eliot's nostalgia, twenty [years later]", which is about Burnt Norton; searches of An Imperfect Life (1998, tseliotimperfect00gord and its reprints) for "hyacinth girl" with "Emily Hale" return no passage joining them. The reading is real, but its owner is Eliot. Frances Dickey, "May the Record Speak: The Correspondence of T. S. Eliot and Emily Hale", Twentieth-Century Literature 66, no. 4 (December 2020), 431–62, reports (pp. 434–35) that in his letter of 3 November 1930 Eliot asks Hale "to reread some of his poems, especially the 'Hyacinth girl' passage and 'My friend, blood shaking my heart' in The Waste Land (ll. 35–41, 400–409), and compare these to 'Pipit' ('A Cooking Egg') and Ash-Wednesday to see how his love for her has developed", that his letter of 24 July 1931 "provides the autobiographical background of the 'Hyacinth girl' scene of The Waste Land and its frame of Wagnerian quotations", and (p. 443) speaks of "Eliot's identification of Hale as the Hyacinth girl and 'my friend' in The Waste Land". That Gordon's 2022 book makes the same identification its title is confirmed by its subtitle and by the reviews in Columbia Magazine (Joanna Scutts, Winter 2022–23: "Eliot's clear identification of Hale as The Waste Land's 'hyacinth girl'") and Colorado Review (Iris Jamahl Dunkle), but not by any scan. The sentence should stay, resting on the letter as Dickey reports it (described, not quoted, as the standard requires for a text after 1929), with Gordon named as the book that made it known; if the editor will not cite an unscanned book, drop the Gordon clause and keep Dickey. "Southam, Guide, note on lines 37–41" should go: nothing in the stop rests on Southam (the Wagner frame is the librettos', the Grail reading Grover Smith's), and no Southam note with that key surfaced in any search, whereas his other keys did.
Corrected: The poem's first love, and its failure. A girl remembers being given hyacinths; another voice remembers coming back late from the garden, unable to speak, "neither / Living nor dead". Wagner frames the scene: the sailor's love-song from Tristan's ship before it (31–34), the shepherd's empty sea after it (42). Grover Smith (1956) reads the garden as the Grail scene of Weston's book, the girl as Grail-bearer and the man as the quester who fails. Eliot himself, writing to Emily Hale in 1930, asked her to reread these lines for his love for her; Lyndall Gordon's <i>The Hyacinth Girl</i> (2022) is named for it. — Sources: Wagner, Tristan und Isolde, Act I / Wagner, Tristan und Isolde, Act III / Grover Smith (1956) / The Eliot–Hale letters, Eliot to Hale, 3 November 1930 / Dickey (2020) / Gordon, The Hyacinth Girl (the last only if the editor accepts a book verified by its reviews).

### love line 199
Kind: judgement
Problem: The last sentence, "The poem's one chaste lover gets one line.", is a bow-tying close and a paradox for effect (Parsifal is not a lover; Verlaine's sonnet says he "a vaincu les Filles"), which the standard rejects. The facts of the stop are verified: Eliot's note quoted exactly; Southam records the Gallipoli landing and, on Robert Sencourt's authority, Mrs Porter's Cairo brothel (Southam's note exists in every edition searched, though no snippet shows whether its key is "l. 199" or "ll. 199–201"); Gardner 1972 has the route "through the ritual washing of feet in Wagner's Parsifal to Verlaine's sonnet"; Verlaine's last line is the poem's line 202.
Corrected: The song is a brothel ballad. Eliot's note says it "was reported to me from Sydney, Australia"; Southam records that Australian soldiers sang it, notably as they landed at Gallipoli in 1915, and, on Robert Sencourt's authority, that Mrs Porter was a legendary brothel-keeper in Cairo. The French line after it ends Verlaine's sonnet on Parsifal, the knight who resisted the Flower-maidens; Helen Gardner (1972) traced the way from the soda water to Verlaine through the washing of Parsifal's feet in Wagner's opera. Parsifal, the one figure in Part III who refuses a seduction, has a single line, in French.

### love line 402
Kind: judgement
Problem: "To whom, and when, is not said" is contradicted by the line before the surrender, "My friend, blood shaking my heart" (403): the lines are addressed to a friend, which is exactly what Miller's reading rests on ("is a 'moment of surrender,' a giving of the self to a friend"). Miller's reading is verified and marked as his, and the stop does not assert his Verdenal thesis, so it can stand; the editor may also weigh that Eliot's letter to Hale of 3 November 1930, as Dickey reports it (p. 435), sends her to "My friend, blood shaking my heart" together with the hyacinth passage. "Webster's widow" is not in Webster: the speaker is Flamineo, warning men on their death-beds that their "howling wives … they'll re-marry / Ere the worm pierce your winding-sheet" (V.vi), so the remarrying is the wives', in the plural, and the standard asks for a word of who is speaking.
Corrected: The thunder's first word is give, and the one gift the poem records is "The awful daring of a moment's surrender", which "an age of prudence can never retract" and by which alone "we have existed". It is said to "My friend"; James E. Miller (1977) reads it as a giving of the self to a friend. What follows is where it will not be found: obituaries, memories, wills, and, in Eliot's note on 408, the wives whom Flamineo in Webster's <i>The White Devil</i> expects to remarry before the worm has pierced the winding-sheet. (If the Hale letter is wanted: after "friend." add "Eliot, writing to Emily Hale in 1930, pointed her to these lines with the hyacinth garden's." and add "Dickey (2020)" to the sources.)

### love line 419
Kind: fact
Problem: "Southam's note on the line records that Eliot's order of the three words, with this one last, has been much argued over", cited to "Southam, Guide, note on line 418", puts the remark in the wrong note. Southam's note on 418 reads "l. 418 Damyata: Eliot translates this as 'control'; other translations prefer…" (1974, 1990, 1994, 1996). The remark is in his note on the thunder's fable, keyed "ll. 399–401" (the 1994 scan cross-refers to it as "note to lines 399–401"): after the three replies ("the gods understand it as Damyata, 'Control yourselves'; the men, as Datta, 'Give…'; … 'Be compassionate'") comes "Eliot's departure from the order of the legend, in placing Damyata last, has given rise to much interpretative speculation" (1994, 1996; the 1974 edition, "Eliot departs from the order of the legend, which reads Damyata, Datta, Dayadhvam", has it in the same place, before "l. 401 Datta: give"). The east checker's report found the same key. "Much argued over" is a fair rendering of "much interpretative speculation".
Corrected: The third word, "control", is answered first by a boat well handled, then by a heart: "your heart would have responded". It is the poem's only love in the conditional, and it comes last, after the couples who could not speak or would not answer: a heart that would have answered, had it been asked. The card gives Eliot's sailing off Gloucester; Southam's note on the thunder's fable records that Eliot's departure from the Upanishad's order, in placing this word last, has given rise to much speculation. — Sources: Southam, Guide, note on lines 399–401 (and, if the gloss "control" is meant to rest on him too, Southam, Guide, note on line 418).
