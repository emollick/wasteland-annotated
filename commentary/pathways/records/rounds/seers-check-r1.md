### seers line 19
Kind: judgement
Problem: "Here the poem takes on the voice of prophecy" is a reading stated as a fact, without an owner; Southam's note on 20 only glosses the address ("God addresses his prophet, 'Son of man, stand upon thy feet, and I will speak unto thee'", 1994 scan), and no commentary I could find calls these lines the poem's prophetic voice. The rest of the stop holds: Ezekiel 2:1 and 6:4 ("And your altars shall be desolate, and your images shall be broken") are exact in the King James text; Southam's key is "l. 22 broken images: cf. Ezekiel vi, 4, 6, God's judgement upon the people ..." (1994; the 1968 and 1974 editions give vi, 6 only); Rainey's note on 22 reads "[broken images]: Perhaps an echo of Ezekiel 6:4, in which God ...", so both citations support the sentence. What the lines borrow, an address and a promise, is on the page, so the first sentence can be made descriptive instead of owned.
Corrected: The lines from here to 30 take a prophet's address and a prophet's promise. "Son of man" is how God addresses Ezekiel (the card at line 20 gives it), and the speaker who borrows it addresses someone who knows only "A heap of broken images"; Southam sets the phrase beside Ezekiel's judgement on idols, "your images shall be broken" (6:4). The voice promises what a prophet is given, something shown ("I will show you", twice), and what it shows is not a vision but "fear in a handful of dust".

### seers line 39
Kind: judgement
Problem: "Both failures return on the road to Emmaus (line 360)" is the writer's reading and overstates it: the eyes that cannot know the third answer the eyes that failed in the garden, but the shepherd's empty sea has no counterpart at 360–366, where counting finds fewer and looking finds more. Everything else is verified: in Act III Kurwenal tells the shepherd "und siehst du ein Schiff, so spiele lustig und hell!" and the shepherd, looking out to sea, answers "Öd und leer das Meer!" (librettoarchive.com, Act 3); Southam's note on 42 ends "reports that there is no sign of her ship: 'Desolate and empty the sea.'" and is followed by "l. 43 Madame Sosostris"; Rainey's book has Kurwenal (the scan shows the name, not the note's key, which in Part I is the site's).
Corrected: The first eyes in the poem proper are these, and they fail before light, not darkness: "Looking into the heart of light, the silence." Then Wagner's shepherd, set to watch for Isolde's ship and to pipe a merry tune if he sees it, reports the sea desolate and empty (line 42; the card gives the scene). In four lines sight fails twice: one who looks and cannot see, one who looks and finds nothing to see. The first of them returns on the road to Emmaus (line 360).

### seers line 43
Kind: fact
Problem: the last sentence passes a reading off as a fact. That the line refers to the law is recorded by none of the three commentaries that could be checked: Southam's notes on Part I pass from "l. 57 Mrs Equitone: a synthetic constructed name" straight to "ll. 60–76: in Matthiessen ..." (1994 scan, studentsguidetos0000sout_t0e0), so he has no note on 59, and his note on 43 says only that the Tarot is "now reduced to the comic banality of fortune-telling"; Rainey's scan yields for "Equitone" only the poem and a sentence of his introduction ("her friend is named Mrs. Equitone, a variant on the notion of equivocation"), and nothing for the line together with "police", "law", "Vagrancy", "illegal", "prosecution" or "fortune-telling"; the Ricks and McCue scan does not answer for the line at all. Grover Smith and Matthiessen show nothing either. Kenner (1959) does read it so: "Madame Sosostris, consulted by dear Mrs. Equitone and harried by police ('One must be so careful these days')" (The Invisible Poet, invisiblepoettse0000kenn_d9l6), and the site's present pathway already has "usually read as a fortune-teller's caution about the law". The statute is quoted rightly: s. 4 makes "every person pretending or professing to tell fortunes, or using any subtle craft, means, or device, by palmistry or otherwise, to deceive and impose on any of his Majesty's subjects" one who "shall be deemed a rogue and vagabond" (legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo4/5/83/section/4). So the sentence should keep the Act as the fact and give the reading its owner, Kenner. The rest of the stop is verified: Crome Yellow chapter XXVII has "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana" (Gutenberg #1999); Eliot's note on 46 is quoted exactly; lines 54–55 are exact; Southam's note on 43 gives "Mr Scogan, who passes under the name of Madame Sesostris (ch. 27)", and Rainey's book has Scogan.
Corrected: The poem's professional seer has a cold and, the card says, a name usually traced to Huxley's <i>Crome Yellow</i>. Eliot's note on line 46 says her reading comes true: the Sailor and the Merchant "appear later", and "Death by Water is executed in Part IV". Her sight has two blanks: a card she is "forbidden to see", and the Hanged Man she cannot find. Kenner (1959) saw her "harried by police" in her last line: under the Vagrancy Act 1824 anyone "pretending or professing to tell fortunes" was a rogue and vagabond.
sources: Huxley, Crome Yellow, ch. 27
Vagrancy Act 1824, s. 4
Kenner (1959)
Southam, Guide, notes on lines 43–55
Rainey, Annotated Waste Land, note on line 43

Corrected: The crowd that Dante's line has just called dead (the card gives his passage) comes over the bridge looking down, and no one in it looks up at the one who has just seen what it is. Against the command to look at line 48, the crowd is looked at and does not look.

### seers line 400
Kind: judgement
Problem: (point 4) "prophecy has come down to an oracle whose meaning lies with the hearer" is a reading, and "come down" a valuation, stated as fact without an owner; the fable supports the description (each group hears its own word and Prajāpati tells each "you have understood"), so the standard's "read as" marks it. Two smaller things: the stop calls the thunder "the poem's last voice of prophecy" while the stop at 426 calls Isaiah's words "the last prophet's words in the poem", and a reader will notice; and the name is "Prajapati" here but "Prajāpati" on the card at this line and in the east pathway. Müller is quoted exactly: "The divine voice of thunder repeats the same, Da Da Da, that is, Be subdued, Give, Be merciful" (Sacred Books of the East 15, V.2.3, p2upanishads00mluoft), and the fable's order (gods, men, Asuras) is as the stop has it. The citation is fine: works.txt's entry for the Upanishad is Müller's translation.
Corrected: The last voice in the poem to speak as an oracle is not a person's but thunder's, and it says one syllable. In the Upanishad (the card gives the fable) Prajāpati answers gods, men and demons with one syllable, Da, and each hears in it the word it needs; then, in Max Müller's translation, "The divine voice of thunder repeats the same, Da Da Da". Read the thunder, after the Sibyl, the clairvoyante and Tiresias, as an oracle whose meaning lies with the hearer; the poem takes all three meanings.
