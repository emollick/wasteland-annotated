# Reader's report, round 1: wagner ("Wagner")

Read as the brief asks: STANDARD.md, then drafts/wagner.txt with poem.txt open, the lines at each stop before the note, and the marginal card at each line (glosses.txt) beside it, since the brief says a stop must not restate the card.

## Accepted as they stand

None of the nine records is accepted whole. The title and the why line of the head are accepted as they are; the objection to the head is to the intro only.

Read through, the pathway holds together as one theme, Wagner's presence in the poem in line order, and the why line says honestly why one would take it: what the operas are doing at each place, and where to hear them. The listening-room links at 31, 42 and 277 are the pathway's best reason to exist, and all three ids are in listen.txt. The objections below are to wording, to notes that repeat the card at their line, to names dropped bare, and to two facts.

## Objections

### wagner head
Problem: "A fourth opera, <i>Parsifal</i>" is wrong: only two operas have been named before it (<i>Tristan</i>, <i>Götterdämmerung</i>), so <i>Parsifal</i> is the third. The corner sentence, "The Wagner in the poem, opera by opera", promises a walk the pathway does not make; it goes in the poem's order, with <i>Parsifal</i> split around <i>Götterdämmerung</i>. Weston is introduced as "Jessie Weston's book on the Grail" without the one fact a first-time reader needs, that Eliot's own note credits it with the poem's plan; "acknowledges in its preface talks at the Bayreuth Festival" is hard to parse; and "its argument ends by naming Tennyson and Wagner" is not so: the sentence closes her chapter XIII, "The Perilous Chapel" (refs/weston.txt line 5672; the draft's ch. XIII citation is right and the design's "chapter XIV" was wrong), and a chapter on the author of the romances follows it. "Amfortas" is dropped bare, and the Tennyson-and-Wagner sentence is repeated word for word at 424, so it is given to 424 below and dropped here (the head's ch. XIII citation goes with it). At 156 words the intro was at the limit.
Corrected: Wagner’s three operas in the poem. He is quoted three times, in German: the sailor’s song that opens <i>Tristan und Isolde</i> (lines 31–34), the shepherd’s report from its last act that the sea is empty (42), and the Rhine-daughters’ cry from the last act of <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, set twice on the Thames (277–278, 290–291) and worn down to “la la” at 306. A third opera, <i>Parsifal</i>, Wagner’s Grail opera, enters at line 202 through Verlaine’s sonnet on it. Jessie Weston’s <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, the Grail book Eliot’s first note credits with the poem’s plan, says in its preface that conversations at the Bayreuth Festival of 1911 gave her some of her evidence. Jean Verdenal, Eliot’s friend in Paris, wrote to him in 1912 that <i>Tristan</i> at a first hearing “leaves you prostrate with ecstasy.” It begins at the lake where Wagner’s patron was found drowned and ends with the Fisher King beside Amfortas, the wounded Grail king of <i>Parsifal</i>.

### wagner line 8
Problem: The first sentence is a fragment, and it says flatly that Ludwig "drowned", where the card at this line is careful ("found dead in the lake ... the verdict was suicide by drowning, though the autopsy found no water in his lungs") and the existing eliot stop says "found drowned"; a reader who has just read the card will notice the difference. "Counted herself one of his family" is a knowing phrase that leaves the reader guessing what she was to him; the card at line 15 says she was a niece of the Empress Elisabeth, and the relation to Ludwig should be said plainly [to check: Elisabeth and Ludwig were cousins].
Corrected: Ludwig II of Bavaria, Wagner's patron, was found drowned here in June 1886, after being declared mad and put under restraint. He brought Wagner to Munich in 1864; <i>Tristan und Isolde</i>, quoted at line 31, was first performed there the next year. Countess Marie Larisch, whose memoir is behind lines 8–18, was related to him through her aunt, the Empress Elisabeth [to check], and tells of his death. Herbert Knust (1967) argued that her book, with its drowned king, shapes much of the poem; Southam's <i>Guide</i> records the argument.

### wagner line 31
Problem: "A song of setting out" does not match the words on the page, which the card translates as "Fresh blows the wind toward home: my Irish child, where do you linger?", a homeward wind and a girl left behind; the second sentence restates the card (the sailor aloft and unseen, Isolde's marriage in Cornwall, the mockery); "the poem sets its first words just before the hyacinth garden" reads as the poem's first words, not the opera's; and the opening is a fragment. The stop's own matter, the placement after line 30 and before the garden, the opera's end, and the recording, should carry it.
Corrected: Straight after "fear in a handful of dust" the poem cuts to the first words sung in <i>Tristan und Isolde</i>, a sailor's song of the wind blowing homeward and a girl left behind; the card at this line gives the scene, and Isolde taking the song as mockery of her. The opera ends with both lovers dead, and the poem puts the opera's first words just before the hyacinth garden. The song, sung unaccompanied as if from the mast, is in the 1928 Bayreuth recording in the <a href="listen.html#rec-tristan-1928-bayreuth-act1-sailor">listening room</a>.

### wagner line 42
Problem: The stop's centre, that the two <i>Tristan</i> quotations "stand on either side of the hyacinth garden", is already in the card at this line in almost the same words, and "Kurwenal" is dropped bare (the card says who he is; the stop does not). Brooks's reading is left half-said: the sea "seeming at first" an extension of the last figure, and then nothing, so the reader waits for a second half that never comes; the corrected text closes the thought with the opera's own fact, and the scholar may prefer to give Brooks's next sentence instead [to check what Brooks goes on to say]. On the hand-offs, here and at 202, 306 and 424: as written they read as the notes talking to each other. "The love pathway takes the couple" names the pathway by its working id, not the title a reader sees ("The lovers"), and "takes", "has" and "gives" are the workshop's words for who owns a line. A pointer helps only where the note has plainly passed something by that the reader may want (here the couple, at 306 the three speeches), and only when it names the pathway as the reader will find it and says what is there; at 202 the hand-off stands in for an explanation the note owes, and at 424 the card already does the job, so those two are cut.
Corrected: The shepherd, watching for Isolde's ship while Tristan lies dying, answers Kurwenal, Tristan's servant, that nothing is on the sea; the card gives the scene. Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the two <i>Tristan</i> quotations together and noted that the empty sea, on its own, seems "simply an extension of the last figure", the lines just before it; in the opera no ship has crossed it. For the couple in the garden, take the pathway on the lovers; the exchange, from the same 1928 Bayreuth set, is in the <a href="listen.html#rec-tristan-1928-bayreuth-act3-shepherd">listening room</a>.

### wagner line 202
Problem: "A soldiers' song" is asserted without a word of why (the reader has Mrs. Porter open and only Eliot's note that the ballad "was reported to me from Sydney"), and "which the war pathway has" is jargon standing in for that word. The Kundry sentence restates the card at this line, which already gives the foot-washing as "a parallel critics have often drawn", so the stop's one contribution, Brooks as the owner of the parallel, is buried; Kundry herself is a name with an adjective and no identity; and "So ..." ties a bow.
Corrected: Verlaine's sonnet is on Wagner's <i>Parsifal</i>; the card gives it, and the foot-washing in Wagner's last act, where Kundry, the temptress of its second act and now penitent [to check], washes Parsifal's feet before he goes in to heal the king. Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the soda water of line 201 as reminding the poet "ironically of another sort of foot-washing". Wagner's Grail hall follows straight on the song of Mrs. Porter, which the Australian troops sang in the war; the pathway on the war takes it up.

### wagner line 266
Problem: "That is what the parallel brings to the Thames, a river with nothing bright in it" is a reading given as a fact, and "They ask Siegfried for the ring made from the gold, in vain" comes after it as a loose end, with Siegfried unnamed as anything, so a reader who does not know the <i>Ring</i> will not know who he is or why the ring matters; the first sentence restates the card at this line. The corrected text keeps every fact, puts the scene in order, and lets the two rivers stand side by side without telling the reader what to see.
Corrected: Eliot's note sends these lines to the scene that opens the last act of <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, and the card gives it: the three Rhine-daughters swim and sing of the gold stolen from them. The river's depths were bright while the gold lay in them and have been dark since, and the daughters beg Siegfried, the opera's hero, for the ring made from the gold, in vain. The parallel Eliot's note draws sets that darkened river beside the Thames of these lines, sweating "Oil and tar". The refrain comes at 277.

### wagner line 277
Problem: On the test asked for: the stop does tell the reader two things the line cannot, that the syllables are Wagner's own with nothing to translate and where the sung refrain can be heard; but "the poem uses them twice as a refrain before its three daughters speak in turn" is visible on the page and already in Eliot's note and in the stop at 266, and the instruction to listen and then read lines 266 to 291 aloud gives no reason for doing so, so as written it does not earn its place (the first pathway already says "Worth reading aloud" at 266). It earns it once it has its point: Eliot's note on 266 calls the passage a song, and the recording supplies the tune the page cannot. The opening is a fragment.
Corrected: The refrain itself, the Rhine-daughters' cry, stands as a line of the poem. The syllables are Wagner's, the voice he gave the river, sound and not words, and of the poem's three passages from him this is the one with nothing to translate. Eliot's note on line 266 says that a song begins there. Hear the three sing the refrain, in the 1964 Solti recording in the <a href="listen.html#rec-gotterdammerung-solti-1964-rhinemaidens">listening room</a>, and then read lines 266 to 291 aloud with the tune in your ear.

### wagner line 306
Problem: On the test asked for: the one thing here the reader cannot get from the line is that no Wagner follows, which the stop does say, though as "Nothing of Wagner follows" rather than plainly; the rest is on the page, and the second sentence says the first over again ("what is left ... is 'la la'"), the bow-tying shape. The hand-off is the jargon noted at 42. There is one more thing the line cannot tell and Eliot's note can: his note on 266 counts line 306 within the daughters' turns ("From line 292 to 306 inclusive they speak in turn"), so the two syllables are the third daughter's. For the scholar: the <i>Götterdämmerung</i> citation carries the refrain but not the point that "la la" is its remnant, for which the design listed Southam and Ricks and McCue on 306.
Corrected: After the three daughters have spoken in turn, the last on Margate Sands, the refrain comes back as two syllables, the last two of "leialala". Eliot's note on line 266 says "From line 292 to 306 inclusive they speak in turn", counting this line among theirs, so by his count the two syllables are the third daughter's. This is the last of Wagner in the poem. The three speeches themselves are read on the pathway on the lovers.

### wagner line 424
Problem: The first sentence repeats the card at this line nearly word for word ("Eliot's note points to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King, the wounded keeper of the Grail"), and "the grail pathway gives him" is jargon; the second sentence is one long tangle in which "the act of Verlaine's sonnet at 202" interrupts and "it" arrives long after its noun, the spear, has been lost; "the plain arid behind him" inverts the poem's words for effect; and the last sentence repeats the intro word for word and, as said under the head, misplaces the sentence, which closes Weston's chapter on the Perilous Chapel, not her book.
Corrected: The card gives Weston's Fisher King, the maimed king of the Grail castle. Set beside him the Grail king Eliot's generation knew from the stage, Amfortas in Wagner's <i>Parsifal</i>, wounded by the holy spear and healed by it in the third act, the act Verlaine's sonnet at 202 comes from. Wagner's king is healed; the poem's sits fishing, with the arid plain still behind him. Weston's chapter on the Perilous Chapel ends by saying that the legend woke again in the nineteenth century "through the genius of Tennyson and Wagner".

Summary: 0 of 9 records accepted as they stand (the title and the why line of the head stand); 9 objections, one on the head and one on each of the eight stops.
