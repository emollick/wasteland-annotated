## The two questions asked

**The openings.** Three stops open with the forbidden shape, a verbless sentence: 35 ("The poem's first love, and its failure."), 139 ("A marriage told in a pub.") and 215 ("The poem's central scene of love, watched by one who has been both sexes."; its second sentence, "First, Mr Eugenides's ... ; then the typist and the clerk ...", has no verb either). A fourth, 279, is the colon-in-place-of-a-verb shape: "The queen and Lord Robert Dudley, later Earl of Leicester, in a barge on the Thames, June 1561, talking of marriage where the Spanish ambassador could hear:" runs twenty-six words without a finite verb before the colon supplies one. The plain sentences: "This is the poem's first love, and it fails." (35); "Here a marriage is told in a pub." (139); "Tiresias, who has lived as a woman and as a man, watches the typist and the clerk" (215); "The card at this line gives the scene from Froude's history" (279); each is carried into the corrected text below. The other nine open with a finite verb and are not the shape: 49 ("The second card dealt is a woman."), 77 ("The room is furnished from ..."), 99 ("The picture over the mantel shows a rape."), 111 ("One speaks and the other does not answer"), 173 ("Eliot's note on 176 gives ..."), 199 ("The song is a brothel ballad." is a sentence), 292 ("Three girls speak in turn"), 402 ("The thunder's first word is give"), 419 ("The third word ... is answered"). The intro's first sentence, "The loves in the poem, taken in order, and how each of them fails.", is a fragment too; the writing brief's own model for the corner sentence is one, and the other heads use the form, so I give a version with a verb and leave the choice to the editor.

**Weston in the intro.** A reader who has not read Weston cannot follow it on one reading, for three reasons. First, the king is not named when he first appears ("the loss of virility" in the king): the reader has not been told that the Grail legend has a king, that he is wounded, or that his wound and his land go together; the Fisher King is named only in the next sentence, inside Brooks's reading, so the reader must work backward to join the two. Second, the step that matters is never said: that the wound is sexual and the land is barren because of it, so that couples whose loves fail are the poem's version of the barren land. The intro puts Weston's sentence and Brooks's phrase beside the couples ("Behind the couples stands ...") and leaves the reader to draw the line; that is pressing, not saying. Third, the one plain fact, that nothing grows, is given only in Weston's own abstraction, "a suspension of the reproductive processes of Nature", and the word that does the work, "impotent", arrives in Brooks's phrase rather than in the intro's own sentence. The corrected intro says the chain in order (a wounded king; a sexual wound; a land in which nothing grows because of it; Eliot's headnote; Brooks; what this pathway does with it) and marks the last step as the pathway's reading. The theme itself does not need Weston to stand; the stops at 139 and 215 are where his king is actually used, and they are corrected to say so plainly.

Two smaller things that run through the draft. Eliot's notes are cited as "Eliot's note on 92", "on 126", "on 429"; the standard's form is "Eliot's note on line N", and by the merge brief's rule N is the line the note glosses on the site's count, so the note on the pearls line, which Eliot numbered 126, is "Eliot's note on line 125" (the line "Those are pearls that were his eyes" is 125; 126 is "Are you alive, or not?"). And every stop is at 84 to 90 words, the limit rather than the sixty-word target, with 139 at five sentences; the corrections come down mostly by cutting what the card at the line already gives.

## Objections

### love head
Problem: The intro rests the theme on Weston and Brooks without saying plainly why the couples are read against a wounded king (see above): the king is unnamed at first mention, the Fisher King arrives a sentence late inside Brooks's reading, and the link from his sexual wound to the barren land to the couples is left for the reader to infer; "Behind the couples stands" asserts it. The first sentence is a fragment. The title and the why line stand.
Corrected:
title: The loves that fail
why: The poem’s couples and seductions, from the hyacinth garden to the typist’s divan, with what Tiresias and the thunder make of them.
intro: This pathway takes the poem’s loves in order, and each of them fails. Eliot’s note on line 218 says “all the women are one woman, and the two sexes meet in Tiresias”, the blind seer who has lived as both. Behind the couples the pathway sets a wounded king, the Fisher King of the Grail legend as Jessie Weston tells it in <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, which Eliot’s headnote credits with the poem’s plan. His wound is “the loss of virility”, and because of it nothing grows in his land, “a suspension of the reproductive processes of Nature”, until he is healed. Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the poem as that land, “rendered impotent by maiming or sickness”. This pathway sets each couple beside the king and reads each failed love as a form of his wound. It ends at the thunder, with the one love the poem speaks of as possible, in the conditional, at line 421.

### love line 35
Problem: The opening is a fragment. The Wagner sentence restates the cards at 31 and 42, which already set the two <i>Tristan</i> passages on either side of the garden, and "the sailor's love-song" is looser than the card's account of the song. "Grail-bearer" and "the quester who fails" are terms a reader who has not read Weston cannot follow (fails at what?), and Emily Hale is a bare name here while the card at this line already gives Gordon's reading and says who Hale was; "Lyndall Gordon" wants the date the site gives critics.
Corrected: This is the poem's first love, and it fails. A girl remembers being given hyacinths, and another voice the return from the garden, unable to speak; the cards at 31 and 42 give the Wagner on either side. Grover Smith (1956) reads the garden as Weston's Grail castle, the girl as the maiden who carries the Grail and the man as the knight who must ask about it and cannot speak [to check against Smith's wording]. The card at this line gives Lyndall Gordon's reading (2022) of the girl as Emily Hale.

### love line 49
Problem: The second sentence restates the card at this line, which already gives "beautiful lady", the nightshade and the drops for the pupils, so the stop credits Southam for what the page has without him. "Lil" is a bare name at line 49, ninety lines before she appears; "on the far side of the poem's women from the hyacinth girl" needs two readings; and "they fill it" is a flourish that says less than the plain fact.
Corrected: The second card dealt is a woman; the card at this line gives the two meanings of her name. Cleanth Brooks (1939) took her as "woman in the waste land", and Grover Smith (1956) put her with Madame Sosostris and with Lil, the wife in the pub scene of Part II, at the far end of the poem's women from the hyacinth girl. Eliot's note on line 218 says all the women are one woman; this card is dealt before any of those women has appeared.

### love line 77
Problem: The stop's frame, a room "furnished from two queens' loves", is the card's own last sentence ("Commentators commonly read the room as furnished with ruined loves"), and the card already gives Enobarbus, Dido's ceiling and Aeneas leaving her; only the two deaths and the last sentence are the stop's. Dido and Aeneas come without a word of who they are.
Corrected: The card at this line gives the room's furniture: the chair from Cleopatra's barge, in the speech of Antony's officer Enobarbus, and the "laquearia" from the ceiling of Dido's palace in the <i>Aeneid</i> (Eliot's notes on lines 77 and 92). Both queens' loves ended in death. Cleopatra killed herself after Antony's defeat, and Dido, queen of Carthage, on a pyre when Aeneas sailed. The woman in the chair sits among the furniture of those two loves, with a man who does not answer her (lines 111–138).

### love line 99
Problem: The second sentence retells the story while saying the card gives it, and the fourth restates what the card and the visible lines have ("Jug Jug" to dirty ears; the return at 203–206; the swallow at 429). "The first sexual act in the poem is on the wall as art" is the stop's one point, and it is put as an aphorism; said plainly, and tied to the room the couple of line 111 sit in, it says why the line is on this pathway.
Corrected: The picture over the mantel shows a rape; the card at this line gives Ovid's story of Tereus and Philomela and her change into the nightingale. It is the first act of sex the poem names, and it is on the wall as a picture, in the room where at line 111 a woman asks a man to speak to her and is not answered. Her cry comes back at lines 203–206 with "Tereu", the rapist's name, and Eliot's note on line 429 sends the swallow there back to her.

### love line 111
Problem: The first sentence restates the card at this line, and the whole of the Vivien material restates the cards at 111 (her marginal note) and 137 (the cut line and the 1960 copy); Vivien Eliot is not said to be the poet's wife, and a reader is not told why her note belongs on a pathway about loves, so it reads as a wink. The stop's own point, that what the man remembers is the failed love of Part I, is the one to lead with. "Eliot's note on 126" is the note on line 125 by the site's count.
Corrected: One speaks and the other does not answer; the card at this line gives the reading. What he remembers, at line 125, is "Those are pearls that were his eyes", and Eliot's note on that line (numbered 126) sends it back to the hyacinth garden and the drowned sailor: the poem's first failed love is in his head. Vivien Eliot, the poet's wife, read the scene in typescript; the cards here and at 137 give what she wrote beside it and the line taken out at her wish.

### love line 139
Problem: The opening is a fragment, and the stop has five sentences. "'What you get married for if you dont want children?' is Weston's theme:" is the "X is a Y:" shape and a reading given as a fact with no owner (Weston did not read the pub scene; the reading is the pathway's, or a critic's, and should be marked so). The Ellen Kellond sentence restates the card at this line, which has her and "pure Ellen Kellond".
Corrected: Here a marriage is told in a pub. Albert, four years in the army, "wants a good time"; Lil has had five children and took pills "to bring it off". In Weston's legend the land is waste because generation has failed; here a wife has paid a chemist to stop it, and the friend's question, "What you get married for if you dont want children?", puts that failure in the poem's own words [to check: the draft names no critic for the reading; if none is found, say it is the pathway's]. The goodnights at 172 are Ophelia's, whom Hamlet courted and cast off.

### love line 173
Problem: The first sentence restates the card at 176 (the "spousal verse", the poet by the Thames, the nymphs and the brides), and the second quotes the visible lines back with a grammatical slip ("The nymphs are departed, and so have their friends"). The contrast that is the stop's point, a wedding song over affairs that end without a trace, comes only in the last sentence.
Corrected: Eliot's note on line 176 names the poem the refrain comes from, Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i>, a song for a double wedding; the card at 176 gives it. Here the refrain is sung when the summer is over, for the nymphs and their friends, "the loitering heirs of city directors", who have gone with the season and "have left no addresses". Spenser's song was for two brides; this one is for the affairs of a summer, which end without a trace.

### love line 199
Problem: The opening is a sentence, not the forbidden shape, but "brothel ballad" compresses two facts the card gives separately (a soldiers' song; a brothel-keeper), and the middle of the stop restates the cards at 199 and 202, which already have the Australian troops, the Cairo brothel, Verlaine's sonnet, the Flower-maidens and the foot-washing parallel; what is the stop's own is Gallipoli and Gardner as the parallel's owner. "The poem's one chaste lover gets one line" is a bow-tying close with a wink in "gets".
Corrected: The lines are from a soldiers' ballad about a brothel-keeper; the card at this line gives it, and Southam's note adds that Australian troops sang it as they landed at Gallipoli in 1915. The French line after it ends Verlaine's sonnet on Wagner's <i>Parsifal</i>, the knight who resisted the Flower-maidens' temptation (the card at 202 gives the sonnet). Helen Gardner (1972) traced the way from the soda water to Verlaine through the washing of Parsifal's feet. The one line on the knight who refused follows straight on the brothel song.

### love line 215
Problem: The first two sentences have no verb, and the second is a list of quotations from lines the reader can see. The Eugenides sentence restates the card at 209, which already has the Brighton hotel and the proposition ("often read as a homosexual one"); Miller as its owner is the stop's addition, and a reader will want to know what kind of proposition Miller means, if the writer can say [to check]. "Oedipus Rex" is named without its author.
Corrected: Tiresias, who has lived as a woman and as a man and has "foresuffered all", watches the typist and the clerk; Eliot's note on line 218 calls what he sees "the substance of the poem". Just before, Mr Eugenides's "weekend at the Metropole", a Brighton hotel, is read by James E. Miller (1977) as a proposition. Cleanth Brooks (1939) found an irony in the witness, since in Sophocles' <i>Oedipus Rex</i> it is Tiresias who knows that the plague on Thebes comes from a sexual sin.

### love line 279
Problem: The opening is the colon-for-a-verb shape (see above), and it restates the card at this line, which already has Froude's letter, the barge, the ambassador, Dudley's wife's death and the marriage that never took place; "Froude" comes bare, and "the queen" is not called Elizabeth for a reader who does not know the name on the page is a queen's. The stop's own matter is Amy's death told plainly and Matthiessen's reading.
Corrected: The card at this line gives the scene from Froude's history: Queen Elizabeth and Robert Dudley, later Earl of Leicester, talking of marriage in a barge on the Thames, June 1561, in the Spanish ambassador's hearing. Froude's chapter tells what stood in the way: Dudley's wife Amy had been found dead at the foot of a staircase in September 1560, amid talk of murder. It never became a marriage, and F. O. Matthiessen (1935) found the "stale pretence" of their relationship as empty as the typist's and the clerk's.

### love line 292
Problem: "Three girls" is the writer's word where Eliot's note, which the stop cites, says Thames-daughters and the cards say daughters; "these mourn nothing and expect nothing" is a turn on the poem's word for effect; "the fire of passion of the Fire Sermon" doubles "of" and leaves the reader unsure whether the burning is the Buddha's or Augustine's. The stop otherwise says what the line does for the theme.
Corrected: The three Thames-daughters of Eliot's note on line 266 speak here in turn, a few lines each, and each tells a seduction: on the floor of a canoe by Richmond; at Moorgate, where "After the event / He wept"; on Margate Sands. Wagner's Rhine-daughters, whose refrain they have just sung, mourn the gold stolen from their river; these three make no complaint, and the last expects "Nothing". Their song runs straight into Augustine at Carthage and the "burning" of the Buddha's Fire Sermon (307–311), which the card at 307 gives.

### love line 402
Problem: The first and third sentences restate the card at this line almost word for word (the surrender as the one thing given; obituaries, memories, wills; Webster's remarrying widows), and "Webster's widow" is the bare form the standard's own example forbids. The stop never says why the surrender is on a pathway about loves, though the sister pathway on the Fire Sermon and the thunder promises that this one "reads the surrender as one made in love"; only Miller's reading is the stop's own.
Corrected: The thunder's first word is give; the card at this line gives the one gift the poem records, "a moment's surrender". This pathway takes the surrender as one made in love; to whom, and when, the poem does not say, and James E. Miller (1977) reads it as a giving of the self to a friend. The lines after it list where the gift is not recorded; for the spider at 408 Eliot's note quotes Webster's <i>The White Devil</i> on widows who remarry "Ere the worm pierce your winding-sheet".

### love line 419
Problem: The first sentence and the sailing restate the card at this line, which already has the boat, Gloucester and the turn to the conditional. "It is the poem's only love in the conditional" is given as a fact where the heart is not named and the reading is the pathway's; and "has been much argued over" tells the reader nothing usable about Eliot's order of the three words (what the argument is), where the pathway on the Fire Sermon and the thunder gives the Upanishad's own order and can be pointed to.
Corrected: The card here gives the boat and the turn at 421 to the conditional, "your heart would have responded". After the couples on this pathway, who could not speak or would not answer, this heart would have answered, had it been asked; it is the one love the poem speaks of as possible, and only in the conditional. Southam's note records that Eliot's order of the three words, with this one last, has been argued over; the pathway on the Fire Sermon and the thunder gives the Upanishad's order.
