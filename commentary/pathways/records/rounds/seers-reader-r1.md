# Reader's report, round 1: pathway "seers" (Prophecy and sight)

Read in this order: READER-BRIEF.md, STANDARD.md, drafts/seers.txt with poem.txt open, the poem's lines at each stop before the note. I also read Eliot's notes (eliot-notes.txt), the design section for this pathway (design/seers.md) and the full text of the marginal cards at the stop lines (glosses-index.txt gives their first line; the site's data.js gives the rest), so as to judge what a stop repeats. Where a stop attributes a point to a critic I searched the scans with ol.py. Brooks (1939) has "The fact that Tiresias is made the commentator serves a further irony. In Oedipus..."; Kenner (1959) has the note "which offers to supply the poem with a nameable point of view"; Eliot's 1927 essay on Middleton (in For Lancelot Andrewes, 1928) has "The irony could not be improved upon:" followed by the exchange, which ends at "I've given thee blind mate twice", as the stop says; and the Mother's reply in the play is "You may see, madam, / My eyes begin to fail" in every edition searched. Two attributions did not come up. Southam's Guide brings Ezekiel 37 in at line 391 ("Dry bones"), and I could not find it at the bridge; and the Vagrancy Act did not appear in the Guide at all, only in David Seabrook's All the Devils Are Here (2002) and in histories of astrology and spiritualism. Both are marked [to check] below.

Two questions I was asked to answer.

The cross-references. The pathway runs two threads, the seers and the eyes that fail, and three stops tie one to the other. At 39, "Both failures return on the road to Emmaus (line 360)" is a promise a reader cannot test at line 39, and the stop at 360 does not keep it: it says only "As in the garden at line 39, sight fails", and at 360 sight does not fail in the garden's way or in the shepherd's. The disciples on the road see a man and do not know him; the walker here sees one more than can be counted and cannot tell "whether a man or a woman". Neither is the eyes that fail in light at 39, nor the empty sea at 42. Read in order, the two sentences read as the notes tying themselves together, not as a pattern I could see in the lines. At 65, "Against the command to look at line 48, the crowd is looked at and does not look" is a contrast that is in the poem (the design asks for it), but the sentence is built as an antithesis, and it took me two readings to find who "the one who has just seen what it is" is; said plainly, it helps. The cross-references that help most are the ones Eliot made: his note on the pearls line sending it back to 37 and 48 (used at 48), and his note on line 46 associating the Hanged Man that Madame Sosostris cannot find with the hooded figure at Emmaus, which the pathway does not use, though it joins a seer's blank to the failing eyes of Part V and the card at 360 already gives it. My corrections at 43 and 360 use it and drop the garden-to-Emmaus tie.

The Vagrancy Act at 43. The connection is clear once the reader finds the line, but "her last line" is not quoted, and line 59 is seventeen lines below the stop. It reads as a fact, and it is an interpretation: that "careful" means careful of the law. The card at the same line gives the same Act and marks the reading "usually read as", adding that palmists and mediums were still being prosecuted under it in the 1920s, which is what makes "these days" fit an Act of 1824; the stop repeats the card and drops both qualifiers, and its "Vagrancy Act of 1824" left me wondering why a century-old law made these days dangerous. Since I could not find the reading's owner in the Guide, the card's own form, "usually read as", is the safe wording, with the Act left to the card.

Accepted as they stand: line 19. Within the head, the title and the why line stand as written; the why says plainly what the pathway contains and does not oversell it. Read through, the pathway holds together as one theme, and its order and its facts can stand. The objections below are to wording, to the two cross-references, to a reading passed off as a fact, and above all to stops that retell the card at their own line: 0, 43, 138, 218, 360, 400 and 426 each give back matter the reader has just read in the margin. Each correction keeps the facts and the sources of the draft and points at the card where the card already has the matter.

### seers head
Problem: "his foresight is of things that have always happened" is a paradox for effect, repeated at 218, and the sentence that carries it runs Tiresias and the thunder together with a semicolon and a "but". The first sentence, a noun phrase, follows the form of the other heads and stands; the title and the why line stand.
Corrected:
title: Prophecy and sight
why: The poem’s prophets, fortune-tellers and blind seers, from the Sibyl in the epigraph to Isaiah’s warning near the end, with the eyes that fail between them.
intro: Prophecy in the poem, and the sight it depends on. The poem is full of people who claim to see what others cannot, and it shows what each of them is allowed to see. Its epigraph is spoken by a man who saw a prophetess with his own eyes, and all she had left to say was that she wanted to die. Eliot’s note on line 218 calls the blind Tiresias the poem’s most important personage and what he sees its substance; but what he foretells is a scene he says he has “foresuffered”, enacted before on the same bed. The thunder near the end says one syllable, and each hearer must make out the word for himself. Between the seers come the eyes that fail, from the hyacinth garden to the road to Emmaus. The stops say at each what is seen, by whom, and on what authority.

### seers line 0
Problem: "a prophetess with one prophecy left, and it concerns herself" is a wink (wanting to die is not a prophecy, and the reader has to work out the joke), Trimalchio is named without a word of who he is, and the rest retells the epigraph card word for word (the jar, the boys, Apollo's offer, the heap of dust, the forgotten youth, the voice).
Corrected: The poem's first seer is the Sibyl of Cumae, a prophetess, hanging in a jar; when boys ask what she wants, she answers that she wants to die. The speaker is Trimalchio, the rich freedman of Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i>, who says he saw her with his own eyes; the card gives his words and Ovid's story of how she came by her years and forgot to ask for youth. The one thing left for her to foretell, in Ovid, is her own dwindling, until only her voice remains.

### seers line 39
Problem: "they fail before light, not darkness" and "one who looks and cannot see, one who looks and finds nothing to see" are antitheses for effect, "In four lines sight fails twice" is clever counting, and "Both failures return on the road to Emmaus" promises a pattern that the stop at 360 does not show (see above).
Corrected: The first eyes in the poem after Trimalchio's in the epigraph are these, and they fail while looking "into the heart of light". The next line is Wagner's shepherd, set to watch for Isolde's ship and to pipe a merry tune if he sees it, reporting the sea desolate and empty (the card at line 42 gives the scene). In the garden the eyes fail and see nothing; on the shore they work, and there is nothing to see.

### seers line 43
Problem: The stop repeats the sosostris card almost entire (Huxley, "appear later", "Death by Water is executed in Part IV", the Vagrancy Act) and drops the card's "usually read as" and its 1920s prosecutions, so a reading becomes a fact; "has a cold" restates the visible line; "her last line" is not quoted and is seventeen lines down. What the pathway needs here, the seer whose sight has blanks, is one clause of the draft. The owner of the reading is [to check]: I could not find the Act in Southam's Guide.
Corrected: Madame Sosostris is the poem's seer by trade; the card at this line gives her name's source and Eliot's note on line 46, which ties her cards to what follows. What she cannot see matters here: a card she is "forbidden to see", and the Hanged Man, whom she does not find; the same note associates him with the hooded figure at line 364. Her parting line, "One must be so careful these days", is usually read as a fortune-teller's caution about the law; the card gives the Vagrancy Act.

### seers line 48
Problem: "is Eliot's own: the word is not in Ariel's song" makes a colon do the work of a verb, and "Eliot's own" has to be decoded as "Eliot's addition"; the last sentence repeats the pearls card (the return at 125 and Eliot's note pointing to 37 and 48); and "Eliot's note on line 126" sends a reader to the site's line 126, the wrong line, since the note Eliot numbered 126 is on the site's 125, as the card says.
Corrected: Ariel's song, which the card gives, has no "Look!"; the command is Eliot's addition, and the one order to look in Part I. Ariel sings to Ferdinand, who believes his father drowned, and the pearls are what the sea has made of a dead man's eyes, "something rich and strange". The card gives the line's return at 125, the one thing the man in Part II says he remembers, and Eliot's note there (numbered 126), which sends it back here and to the garden of line 37, where eyes failed.

### seers line 65
Problem: "no one in it looks up at the one who has just seen what it is" took me two readings (the one is the speaker); "the crowd is looked at and does not look" is an antithesis for effect; "here no one prophesies to the dead" ties a bow. The attribution to Southam of Ezekiel 37:9 at this line I could not confirm: his Guide brings Ezekiel 37 in at line 391, "Dry bones", so [to check].
Corrected: The crowd that Dante's line has just called dead (the card gives his passage) comes over the bridge with every eye on the ground, and the one who looks at it, and sees what it is, is the speaker. The one command to look in Part I was "Look!" (line 48); here no one looks. Southam sets the passage beside Ezekiel's valley of dry bones, where the prophet is told to "Prophesy unto the wind" so that the slain may live (37:9) [to check]; these dead have no prophet.

### seers line 138
Problem: The first sentence retells the chess card at length (Livia, the widow, the Duke, Bianca) before pointing at it, and "eyes that are 'lidless' cannot even close" is a turn for effect that adds nothing to the card's gloss, "lidless" is sleepless. The card calls the woman at the board "a mother" and the stop "a widow" (Livia's word for her), and "Eliot's note sends the chess to" is not a phrase a person would say.
Corrected: Eliot's note names the chess game in Middleton's <i>Women Beware Women</i>; the card gives the scene, in which Livia keeps a widow at the board while the Duke seduces her daughter-in-law Bianca. Eliot quoted the exchange in 1927, ending at "I've given thee blind mate twice", and wrote that "The irony could not be improved upon". In the play the widow answers that her "eyes begin to fail". The watcher at the board sees nothing of what matters; the eyes at this line are "lidless", sleepless as the card says.

### seers line 218
Problem: The first sentence and the quotation from Eliot's note repeat the tiresias card (Juno, Jupiter, the blindness, the foreknowledge, "the most important personage"); "His foresight is of what has always happened:" is the paradox again, with a colon; and the reader is not told who Tiresias is, the blind prophet of Thebes, until the Sophocles clause. Brooks and Kenner are confirmed in the scans.
Corrected: Tiresias is the blind prophet of Thebes; the card gives Ovid's story of how Juno blinded him and Jupiter gave him foreknowledge. Brooks (1939) drew the irony that the commentator here is the seer who in Sophocles told Oedipus the cause of the plague on Thebes. What he foretells at line 229 he has already seen, "foresuffered all", enacted before on the same bed. Kenner (1959) read Eliot's note, which calls him "the most important personage in the poem", as an offer of "a nameable point of view".

### seers line 360
Problem: The first two sentences restate the third card (Luke 24:16 and 24:31, Shackleton); "As in the garden at line 39, sight fails" does not hold, since here the eyes see more than can be counted, not less, and the note does not say what is common; and the tie Eliot himself makes, the hooded figure and the Hanged Man of line 55, is left to the card. The design gives this stop "their eyes were holden" and the counting.
Corrected: Eliot's headnote to Part V names the journey to Emmaus; the card gives Luke's story and Shackleton's. The pathway takes one clause from Luke: the disciples walked beside the risen Christ and "their eyes were holden that they should not know him" (24:16). Here counting and looking give different answers, and what the eyes see is hooded, "a man or a woman" unknown. Eliot's note on line 46 associates this figure with the Hanged Man, the card Madame Sosostris could not find at line 55.

### seers line 367
Problem: Hesse is named without a word of who he is (the card has him), and the second sentence, an instruction with a point, tails off into fragments for rhythm ("from line 378 plain statement, a woman fiddling on her hair, towers upside down"); at ninety words the stop is at the limit.
Corrected: Eliot's note on these lines quotes the close of an essay on Dostoevsky's <i>The Brothers Karamazov</i> by the German novelist Hermann Hesse: half of Europe is on the way to chaos, singing drunkenly as Dmitri Karamazov sang, and while the citizen laughs, "der Heilige und Seher hört sie mit Tränen", the saint and the seer hear them with tears. Read lines 367 to 385 as what such a seer hears and sees: questions first, "What is that sound", "Who are those hooded hordes", and from line 378 plain statements.

### seers line 400
Problem: The second sentence retells the da card (Prajapati, gods, men and demons, one syllable, Müller's line); "the poem's last voice of prophecy" collides with "The last prophet's words" at 426, which comes after it; and "prophecy has come down to" sums the pathway up. The roll-call of the Sibyl, the clairvoyante and Tiresias is the design's and stays.
Corrected: After the Sibyl, Madame Sosostris and Tiresias, the poem's prophecy passes to a voice that is not a person's. The thunder says one syllable, and the card at line 401 gives the fable in which gods, men and demons each hear a different word in it. The meaning of the oracle lies with the hearer, and the poem, at lines 402, 412 and 419, takes all three words in turn and follows each with an answer in its own voice.

### seers line 426
Problem: The stop restates the fisher-king card (Isaiah 38:1 in full, "lands" not "house", no answer); "Weston's Fisher King" is a bare name (the card explains him) and "whom the card takes with" is not a phrase a person would say; the design asks this stop to say only that the poem's last prophet is Isaiah and what the office has come to.
Corrected: The last prophet in the poem is Isaiah, and the card gives his words to the dying King Hezekiah, "Set thine house in order: for thou shalt die, and not live" (Isaiah 38:1; the same words in 2 Kings 20:1). The fisher on the shore, the wounded keeper of the Grail as the card takes him, speaks the prophet's sentence himself, as a question and about his lands. No prophet answers him; the next line is London Bridge falling.

Summary: 1 record accepted as it stands (line 19; the title and the why line stand within the head), 12 objections (the head and eleven stops), each to wording, to a name left bare, to a stop repeating its card, or to a cross-reference; two attributions marked [to check] (Southam's Ezekiel 37:9 at line 65, and the owner of the Vagrancy Act reading at 43), no fact or source otherwise disputed.
