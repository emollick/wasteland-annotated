# Reader's report, round 1: method ("The past beside the present")

Read in this order: READER-BRIEF.md, STANDARD.md, drafts/method.txt with poem.txt open, the poem's lines at each stop before the note, and the full text of the marginal card at each stop (the index gives 160 characters; the site's js/data.js gives the rest), since a stop must not repeat the card. I also checked the quotations a reader would take on trust: the two Dial sentences in the intro are exact against the archive.org scan of the November 1923 number ("Mr Joyce", without a stop, is as the Dial printed it); Baudelaire's dirty yellow fog is his line 9; "Burn'd on the water", "most intelligent, very elegant" and "that great lord" are as the site's library gives its passages; Brooks's "surface contrasts which in reality constitute parallelisms" and "alike as well as different" and the facsimile's "spelt into my ruins" are found by ol.py in the scans. One quotation is inexact: Goldsmith's line is "is—to die", and the stop at 253 drops the dash. I could not reach Spenser's text to see Elizabeth and "that great lord" for myself; the stop cites Southam for it and I leave it as it stands.

## Accepted as they stand

No record in full. Within the head, the title and the why line stand as written: the title is a plain name for the theme, and the why says honestly what the pathway does, from Chaucer's April to the fragments, without overselling it. Read through, the pathway holds together as one theme, nine pairings in the poem's order with the 1923 essay at both ends, and its facts, its order and its sources can stand. Every objection below is to wording, to a name left bare, to a stop repeating what the card at its line already gives, to a hand-off, or to a sentence in which the note narrates its own method; each correction keeps the facts and the sources of the draft.

## The two questions asked

The hand-offs. Five stops end by giving a line to another pathway: 1 ("The Bible pathway takes the part's title"), 60 ("The Dante pathway takes the crowd"), 77 ("The loves pathway takes her at 111"), 196 ("The war pathway takes the soldiers' song at 199") and 253 ("the loves pathway takes her at 249"). As written they read as the notes talking to each other. "Takes" is the workshop's word for which pathway owns a line, not a word a reader has any use for; the pathways are named by their working ids (Bible, loves, war) and not by the titles a reader will see in the chooser ("The Bible and the Prayer Book", "The loves that fail", "After the war"); and each is a tail after a full stop, with nothing in the note leading to it. They differ in whether they help. At 60 the pointer earns its place: a reader who took a pathway of old poems in modern scenes will expect Dante's "I had not thought death had undone so many" two lines down, and needs to be told that the crowd is read elsewhere on purpose. At 77 and 253 it helps in a weaker way: each note ends on the woman, and a reader who wants her scene rather than the parallel is sent to it. At 1 it does not: the note has said nothing about the part's title, so "takes the part's title" has no antecedent. At 196 it does not either: the card at 198 explains the song, and the song is no old poem, so no reader of this pathway expects a stop there. Keep 60, 77 and 253, each rewritten as a sentence that names the pathway by its title and says what is read there; cut 1 and 196. (The titles used below are the design's; if a title changes, the sentence changes with it.)

The method sentences. Five stops narrate the pathway's own method: "This is the pathway's first instance and its rule" (1), "Here the method runs the other way" (128), "Here the device is the negative" (176), "This is the method at its smallest, one line kept and one thing changed" (185), "Here the substitution is complete" (196); the last sentences of 128 and 431 ("The past reaches this room by way of a dance tune"; "The essay says order; the line says propping up") are the same voice closing the note. It reads as a guide leading a tour, announcing each exhibit's place in the sequence before showing it, and twice ("runs the other way", "the device is the negative") the announcement is a puzzle until the next sentence solves it. A scholarly editor's note says what the poet kept and what he changed, and lets the reader see the pattern; on this pathway the intro has already set the frame, so the stops need not keep pointing at it. What to do: cut the sentences that only announce (at 1, 176 and 196), and where the announcement carries an observation, state the observation as a fact about the lines, with Eliot or the line as its subject rather than "the method" (at 128, "The parallel here was not made by Eliot: the song had already set Shakespeare to ragtime"; at 185, "Eliot keeps the line but for one phrase and changes only what is heard"; at 196, "Every term of Day's sentence is replaced ... except 'in the spring'"). One instruction to the reader at the first stop, saying what the stops that follow ask, is kept, since the standard allows an instruction that has a point.

## Objections

### method head
Problem: The intro names F. O. Matthiessen without a word of who he is, and its sentence about him comes again word for word at 431; “warned” is not what Brooks did (he described how the poem works); “set in the same sentence as a modern scene” is not true at 1 or 60, where the old poem is answered or laid over, not quoted; and the corner sentence, “Old poems set in the same lines as modern scenes”, can be read as poems whose setting is modern. The second Dial sentence is also quoted at 431, so the intro below keeps its second half and the stop its first. The title and the why line stand.
Corrected:
title: The past beside the present
why: The places where an old poem and a modern scene are made to share the same lines, from Chaucer’s April to the fragments at the end.
intro: Old poems and modern scenes in the same lines. In November 1923, reviewing Joyce’s <i>Ulysses</i> in <i>The Dial</i>, Eliot wrote that “in manipulating a continuous parallel between contemporaneity and antiquity, Mr Joyce is pursuing a method which others must pursue after him”, and that the method gave “a shape and a significance to the immense panorama of futility and anarchy which is contemporary history”. He was writing about another man’s book, but the critic F. O. Matthiessen was applying the sentences to Eliot’s own poem by 1935. This pathway stops where the parallel is plainest, where Chaucer, Baudelaire, Shakespeare, Spenser, Marvell, John Day or Goldsmith is set beside a modern scene in the same lines, so that the reader sees both at once. Cleanth Brooks (1939) wrote that the poem’s parallels are often contrasts and its contrasts parallels, and the stops do not assume that the past comes off better.

### method line 1
Problem: Six sentences, two over the limit, and the first three say what the card at this line already says (Chaucer's showers, the drought of March, the pilgrimages, the first line answering), so the stop's own observation, that the rain still reaches the roots, is buried; "This is the pathway's first instance and its rule" is the guide announcing the tour, "folk" is Chaucer's word passed off as ours, and the last sentence, on the part's title, hangs on nothing, since the note has not mentioned the title.
Corrected: The card gives Chaucer's opening, the April of sweet showers and pilgrimages. Eliot's first sentence answers those lines without quoting them. It keeps the month, and keeps the rain at the roots, where Chaucer's showers have "perced to the roote" and Eliot's stir "Dull roots", and it adds the word "cruellest". Nothing on the page names Chaucer; a reader who knows his lines hears both Aprils at once, and each stop that follows asks the reader to do the same.

### method line 60
Problem: The first sentence repeats the card and Eliot's note (the spectre in daylight) and drops the verb from its second half ("and the card the poem"), and the third repeats the card's 1950 remark in words no one would say ("and the realistic and the phantasmagoric fused"); "(his line 9)" is a reference that belongs on the sources line, where it already is. The hand-off is the one on this pathway that earns its place, since the reader will expect Dante's crowd two lines down, but as written it names the pathway by its working id and says nothing about what the crowd is.
Corrected: Eliot's note quotes the opening of Baudelaire's "Les Sept Vieillards", and the card gives it, a Paris in which a ghost stops the passer-by in broad daylight. Eliot lays that city over the City of London at nine on a working morning, and Baudelaire's dirty yellow fog becomes "the brown fog of a winter dawn". The card has Eliot's own account, from 1950, of what he learned from Baudelaire about the modern city. The crowd on the bridge, Dante's, is left to the pathway on Dante and the dead.

### method line 77
Problem: Five sentences; "the Cydnus" is bare (the card says it is a river), the first and fourth sentences repeat the card, which already has Enobarbus, the barge, Dido's ceiling and Milton's Eden, and "changes each thing in it" overstates, since "like a burnished throne" is kept whole. The hand-off can stay, because the note ends on the woman, but not as "The loves pathway takes her at 111".
Corrected: The card gives the speech of Antony's officer Enobarbus on Cleopatra's barge. Eliot keeps its shape and changes the things in it: the barge that "Burn'd on the water" is a chair that "Glowed on the marble", and the perfumed sails are her "strange synthetic perfumes". A queen's arrival by water becomes a woman in a room whose grandeur is borrowed too, from Dido's palace in Virgil's <i>Aeneid</i> and Eden in Milton's <i>Paradise Lost</i>. What she says at 111 is read on the pathway on the loves that fail.

### method line 128
Problem: Six sentences, and four of them repeat the card at this line (the song and its date, the chorus, the reversed pair, the added syllable), so the stop's own point, that the popular song had made the parallel before Eliot did, is given only as "Here the method runs the other way", a puzzle until the next sentence; "The popular present" is not a phrase a person would say, and "The past reaches this room by way of a dance tune" ties a bow.
Corrected: The card gives the song, a ragtime number of 1912 whose chorus calls it "most intelligent, very elegant". The parallel here was not made by Eliot: the song had already set Shakespeare to ragtime, and what runs through the man's head instead of an answer to "Are you alive, or not?" is that song. The card notes the added syllable and the reversed chorus.

### method line 176
Problem: "Here the device is the negative" cannot be understood on one reading (the negative of what?), and the last sentence cannot be taken in at all: Brooks's quotation, "as well as the reverse", Spenser's Elizabeth and Leicester, "(so Southam)" and the barge at 279 are run together on a semicolon, so the reader is not told what parallel lies under this contrast. The first sentence repeats the card ("spousall verse", the nymphs gathering flowers).
Corrected: The card gives Spenser's wedding poem and its refrain, the line each of his stanzas ends on. Eliot keeps the refrain and puts the modern river into it as a list of what is missing: the nymphs "are departed", and the Thames "bears no" bottles, papers or cigarette ends. Cleanth Brooks (1939) wrote that the poem works by "surface contrasts which in reality constitute parallelisms"; Spenser's poem, B. C. Southam notes, itself names Elizabeth and, as "that great lord", Leicester, the pair in the barge at line 279.

### method line 185
Problem: "This is the method at its smallest" is the guide's voice, and "one line kept and one thing changed" is not exact, since "always" goes too; "arguing his mistress into bed" is a knowing phrase where the card's "urges his mistress to yield" is plain; the speaker does not hear the rat, which creeps; and the last sentence repeats the card, which already has the second use at 196 and the misnumbered note.
Corrected: The card gives Marvell's lines, in which the lover urging his mistress not to wait hears "Time's wingèd chariot hurrying near". Eliot keeps the line but for one phrase and changes only what is heard: for the chariot, "The rattle of the bones" and a chuckle, while a rat creeps past the speaker, who is fishing in the canal at 189. Marvell's lover hears time coming; the speaker here hears bones. The line comes again at 196, the next stop.

### method line 196
Problem: The first and third sentences repeat the card at 198, which already gives Day, Ovid's stag and the whole list of substitutions, and "Here the substitution is complete" announces what the list then shows; the stop's own point, the one phrase kept word for word, is left without the observation that makes it worth keeping, that Day's spring is water and Eliot's a season (the library record at this line has it). The hand-off to the song at 199 is cut: the card explains the song, and it is no old poem.
Corrected: Eliot's note quotes John Day's <i>Parliament of Bees</i>, in which horns and hunting bring the hunter Actaeon to the goddess Diana, bathing; the card at 198 gives the passage. Every term of Day's sentence is replaced, motors for hunting, Sweeney, from Eliot's own poems, for the hunter, Mrs Porter's soda water for the goddess's spring, except "in the spring", which is kept word for word. In Day, as the library's record says, the spring is the water Diana bathes in; in Eliot's line it is the season.

### method line 253
Problem: Olivia Primrose is a name without a word of who she is; "the queen of line 279" is a queen the reader has not met at a line not yet reached; the first two sentences repeat the card (the song, "is to die", the first line kept, the gramophone); and "is to die" drops the dash Goldsmith has ("is—to die"). The hand-off is kept, reworded, since a reader at this scene may well want it read as one of the poem's couples.
Corrected: The card gives the song from Goldsmith's <i>The Vicar of Wakefield</i>, a seduced daughter's, in which the one art left her "is—to die". Eliot keeps its first line and runs it into the typist's room, where the remedy is a record on the gramophone; the two remedies stand side by side without comment. Cleanth Brooks (1939) set her beside Elizabeth, the queen in the barge at 279, and found them "alike as well as different". The scene is read at 249 on the pathway on the loves that fail.

### method line 431
Problem: Seven sentences; the first repeats the card ("The poem's own line among the quotations"), the last repeats the intro word for word (Matthiessen), and the Dial quotation repeats the intro's. "The essay says order; the line says propping up" is right but clipped, and the card has already glossed "shore", so the stop can give the gloss in a clause and stop. "The Fragments view" is a site view a first-time reader may not have met; "the card" alone would do if the editor prefers.
Corrected: Among the borrowed lines of the ending, which the card and the Fragments view give one by one, this line is the poet's own. In the draft it read "spelt into my ruins"; "shored against" was the revision. Set it beside the sentence of 1923 in which Eliot called Joyce's method "simply a way of controlling, of ordering" the panorama of contemporary history. The essay's word is order; the poem's is shoring, a builder's word for propping up what would otherwise fall.

Summary: 0 records accepted as they stand (the title and the why line stand within the head), 10 objections (the head and all nine stops), every one to wording, a bare name, a repeated card, a hand-off or a method sentence; none to a fact or a source, except that "is to die" at 253 wants Goldsmith's dash.
