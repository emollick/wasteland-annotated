# Reader's report on the pathway "change" (Metamorphosis), round 1

Read as the brief asks: STANDARD.md, then drafts/change.txt with poem.txt open, the lines at each stop before the note. I also read the full text of the marginal cards the stops point at (from the site's js/data.js, since the index gives only their first 160 characters), and checked the stops' quotations and paraphrases against open texts of The Tempest (I.ii), Ovid (Metamorphoses VI 650–674 and III 316–338) and the Pervigilium Veneris; the quotations are exact and the paraphrases hold, apart from the two slips noted at 243 and 257. The two phrases the pathway takes from Brooks ("one of Eliot's major symbols"; "the way into life may be by death itself") are in the scan of Modern Poetry and the Tradition, the second in the sentence that goes on "The drowned Phoenician Sailor is a type of the fertility god whose image was thrown into the sea annually", which is what stop 315 paraphrases. All nine stops are under ninety words (76 to 89) and the intro is 148; nothing is over length, but nothing is near the target of sixty either, and the corrections below shorten only where a sentence was the card's.

## Accepted as they stand

Line 191. Ferdinand is placed in his scene, the play's facts (the king alive, the son given back in the last act) are stated as facts, and "the poem keeps the mourning and drops the return" says what the line does for the theme in plain words; it is the stop that best earns the pathway's claim. The one thing it leans on, who Ariel is, is supplied by the corrected intro.

## The two questions asked

Which card is which. On this site "card" means the note in the margin, and every draft uses it so ("the card at this line gives Ovid's passage"). In Part I the poem's own cards are on the page: Madame Sosostris deals them, line 47 says "Here, said she, / Is your card", and the retired Water pathway called the Sailor "a card in a pack". The stop at 48 uses the word in both senses in successive sentences. "It comes in a bracket on a card" is the tarot card, which the reader has just read about. "The card at this line gives Ariel's song" then reads as the same card, and is nearly true of it, since the bracket is a line of the song; so a reader can go on without ever learning that a note in the margin is meant, and "as the card says" in the next sentence, which the tarot card does not say, is a puzzle. At 125 the ambiguity runs the other way: there is no card in the margin at that line, so "line 48, the card" could be the Sailor's card or the margin's (Eliot's note, "Cf. Part I, l. 37, 48", means the line, which is on the Sailor's card). Elsewhere on this pathway (99, 191, 203, 257, 315) "the card" is unambiguous, because no tarot card is on the page. The seers draft's stop at 48 ("Ariel's song, which the card gives") has the same collision. The fix is to name the tarot card as Madame Sosostris's or the Sailor's and the note as "the card in the margin" wherever both are on the page, which on this pathway is at 48 and 125.

Whether the stops earn the claim. The claim is made three times as fact, and each time it rests on something the reader can see, so it is mostly earned; where it is pressed is in the wording. At 48 the fact is that the poem quotes the pearls twice and the sea-change never, and the card prints the song, so the reader can check; "leaves the promise of the sea-change behind" adds a promise the stop itself has just taken away, since the song is not true and the father is alive. At 191 the play's facts are stated and the poem's line is quoted, and "keeps the mourning and drops the return" is plain. At 257 "the drowning that the play undoes is not undone here" presses: nothing is undone in the play, there was no drowning, and the point is 191's again in figurative dress, with "the one splendour in the poem comes in on borrowed music" as a flourish. At 315 the claim is earned by the lines, which have bones, a current and a whirlpool and nothing the man becomes, but the stop asserts "has no sea-change" before it has set the song's coral and pearls beside them, and it closes on a verdict against Brooks by understatement ("the lines give the current and the bones") rather than a plain statement. At 429 the claim is earned by the Latin, which is a question, and by the clause Eliot's line drops; but the stop then trails into sources and never says why the pathway ends here. At 99, 125, 203 and 243 the stops describe the borrowed changes and what the poem does with them, and press nothing. What the pathway lacks is an owner for its reading as a whole: the intro attaches Brooks to a different claim (that death by water may be the way into life), so the pathway's own reading, that the poem shows its sources' changes and makes none of its own, is nowhere marked as a reading. The corrected intro marks it as the pathway's.

### change head
Problem: The first sentence of the intro, shown alone in the corner, is a figure ("Two books of changed bodies lie under the poem") where the corner wants a plain sentence that names the subject. Ariel is named in the why line and the intro without a word of who he is, and Phlebas without a word of who he is. The last clause, "the stops mark that reading as his and set the texts beside the lines", is a note on method that a reader has no use for, and it attaches Brooks to a claim that is not the pathway's: his reading is that death by water may be the way into life, the pathway's is that the poem makes no change of its own, and that reading has no owner anywhere in the draft. Read through, the pathway holds together as one theme, the title is right, and the why line is honest about what the pathway asks.
Corrected:
title: Metamorphosis
intro: The poem’s changed bodies, from Ovid and from <i>The Tempest</i>. Ovid’s <i>Metamorphoses</i> gives it Philomel, raped and turned into a nightingale (99), and Tiresias, who was a woman for seven years (218); the song the spirit Ariel sings in <i>The Tempest</i> gives it a drowned king with eyes turned to pearls and a body that suffers “a sea-change / Into something rich and strange”. The poem quotes the pearls twice, at 48 and 125, and the sea-change never. This pathway follows the changes: what Ovid’s and Shakespeare’s bodies are turned into, what becomes of the poem’s own people, the drowned Phlebas among them, and the change the last Latin line asks for, when shall I be like the swallow. That the poem makes no change of its own is the pathway’s reading. Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the poem’s people as not knowing that “the way into life may be by death itself”; the stops set it beside the lines.
why: Ovid’s changed bodies and the sea-change of Ariel’s song, from Philomel to the swallow, and whether anyone in the poem is changed.

### change line 48
Problem: "Card" means two things here within two sentences (see above): the bracket is on a tarot card, "the card at this line" is the note in the margin, and a reader can take the second for the first and then find "as the card says" a puzzle. The stop also re-quotes the sea-change lines that the margin's card prints in full, and says neither who Ariel is nor what the first change is (eyes to pearls). "Leaves the promise of the sea-change behind" presses: the stop has just said the father is not drowned, so the song promises nothing; the fact, that the pearls are quoted twice and the sea-change never, is enough, and the reading belongs in the intro as the pathway's.
Corrected: The first change in the poem is a drowned man's, eyes turned to pearls, in a bracket on one of Madame Sosostris's cards. The line is from the song the spirit Ariel sings in <i>The Tempest</i> to a prince who thinks his father drowned; the card in the margin prints the whole song, which goes on to a sea-change into "something rich and strange". The father, as that card says, is not drowned. The poem quotes the pearls, here and again at line 125, and the sea-change never.

### change line 99
Problem: "The two sisters" and "the child's murder" cannot be followed without the card open: the stop never says who the second sister is or whose child was killed, and Tereus is not tied to the "barbarous king" of the line. "A voice that cannot be violated" is the card's gloss of "inviolable", word for word. The Brooks sentence is a bare tag that does no work where it stands, but it is the one thing that tells the reader Philomel matters beyond this line, so it stays. The stop's claim is earned, since the change is finished in Ovid and "still the world pursues" is on the page; the corrected text keeps it and adds what the line does with the finished change, which is hang it on a wall.
Corrected: In Ovid the change comes in the middle of a pursuit. Tereus, the "barbarous king", chases Philomel and her sister Procne, his wife, sword drawn, after they have killed his son; they are birds before he reaches them, with the marks of the killing on their breasts (the card gives the story). The poem hangs the finished change on a wall as a picture and keeps the pursuit in the present tense, "still the world pursues". Cleanth Brooks (1939) called Philomela one of the poem's major symbols.

### change line 125
Problem: The first sentence has no verb. "Line 48, the card" is ambiguous the other way from 48: there is no card in the margin at this line, so the reader must guess between the Sailor's tarot card and the margin's card at 48 (Eliot's note means the line, which is on the Sailor's card). "Another man's change" leaves the reader to work out which man. "Goes unanswered" is not quite what the page shows, since the next words are his, "But / O O O O that Shakespeherian Rag". The typescript fact is the stop's own and carries it.
Corrected: The pearls come back as the one thing the man remembers when asked whether he knows, sees or remembers "Nothing". Eliot's note on line 126 sends the reader to line 37, the hyacinth garden, and to line 48, the pearls on the Sailor's card. In the typescript the line began "The hyacinth garden" and ended with the word yes; the garden was cut. What is left in his head is the drowned king's change from Ariel's song, and "Are you alive, or not?" is answered with a line of ragtime.

### change line 203
Problem: The second sentence is the card's: the vocative, Lyly and Eliot's note on line 100 are all on the card at line 206, and "in the vocative" is a grammar term a plain reader may not have. What the stop has of its own, that Philomel is now a sound with the crime's name in it, is right and earned and wants the room; the reader also needs to be told what has changed since line 99, that the picture and the body are gone and the cry comes on its own after Mrs Porter's song.
Corrected: At line 99 the change of Philomel was a picture on a wall. Here, after Mrs Porter's song, there is no picture and no body, only the cry, "Jug jug", with "So rudely forc'd" from line 100 and "Tereu", the rapist's name, Tereus, as the bird calls it. The card gives Lyly's song, the Elizabethan source of the words. The change has gone from a woman to a bird to a sound, and the sound keeps the crime in it as a name.

### change line 243
Problem: "The one the poem prints in full" is not what the reader sees: at line 243 there is no Latin, and it is Eliot's note on line 218, not the poem, that prints Ovid. Tiresias is named in a stop for the first time without a word of who he is beyond his seven years, and "Struck into a woman" is hard to follow, since he was not struck but struck the snakes. The rest is clear and earned: "one who has been both parties" is what the change is for, and the lines say so.
Corrected: Tiresias, the blind seer of Thebes, has the one change that Eliot's notes give in full. His note on line 218 prints Ovid's Latin and says that "the two sexes meet in Tiresias". In Ovid he struck two coupling snakes and was a woman for seven years, then a man again; having known love from both sides, he was called to judge which sex has the more pleasure. "Foresuffered all / Enacted on this same divan or bed" is said by one who has been both parties.

### change line 257
Problem: The first sentence has no verb and repeats the card. "The record the typist put on with an 'automatic hand'" misreads the lines: the automatic hand smooths her hair, and then she puts a record on. "The play ends with his father alive" is stop 191's again, and the close presses: "the drowning that the play undoes" was never a drowning, as 191 has said, and "the one splendour in the poem comes in on borrowed music" is a flourish on a word that happens to occur once. What the line does for the theme can be said plainly: in the play Ferdinand follows the music and it brings him to Miranda; here nobody follows it, and it comes to a church wall.
Corrected: The line is Ferdinand's again, from the speech behind lines 191–192, as the card says; here the music is a record the typist has put on after smoothing her hair "with automatic hand". In the play the music allays the sea's fury and Ferdinand's grief, and he follows it, "Or it hath drawn me rather", to Miranda, whom he is to marry. Here it goes by the speaker and along the Strand, and comes not to a person but to the walls of Magnus Martyr and their "Inexplicable splendour".

### change line 315
Problem: "Has no sea-change" is asserted before the reader has been shown the comparison; set the song's two lines (bones to coral, eyes to pearls) beside these two and the claim needs no pressing. "Passes the body back through" gives the current a verb the English does not give it (the poem's "He passed"), and the "back" is the French's. The last clause, "the lines give the current and the bones", is a verdict on Brooks by understatement; said plainly it is fair, and it is what the lines show.
Corrected: In Ariel's song the drowned man's bones are coral and his eyes pearls. Phlebas's bones are picked "in whispers" by a current; he passes "the stages of his age and youth" and enters the whirlpool. In the French, on the card, the current carries him back through the stages of his former life; the bones and whirlpool are the English's additions. Brooks (1939) took Phlebas for the drowned fertility god, whose death is "the way into life"; the lines have a current, bones and a whirlpool, and nothing he becomes.

### change line 429
Problem: After its first sentence the stop is the card's matter (the card on the last eight lines, keyed at 431, has the Pervigilium, its translation, Procne and Tennyson) and it ends on Tennyson, a source, not on the theme. "The poem stops before that clause" is ambiguous, since "a late Latin poem" has just been named. The claim is earned by the Latin, which is a question, and by the dropped clause; what the stop does not say is why the pathway ends here, that the bird the poet would become is the one Philomel's sister became, so the last wish is for the change the pathway began with. Tennyson's half of the line is left to the card and the Fragments view; the sources line keeps him. "The swallow is Procne" is the tradition's identification, which the card at line 99 explains (Ovid names neither sister's bird); the site's cards state it as the stop does, so I have not qualified it.
Corrected: The last change in the poem is asked for, not made. The Latin ends the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i>, a late Latin poem for Venus's spring festival: the nightingale sings, the poet is silent and asks when he will become like the swallow, so that he may cease to be silent, and Eliot's line drops that clause. Eliot's note here points to Philomela in Parts II and III. The swallow is Procne, her sister, and the bird the poet would become is the one her story ended in.

Summary: 1 record accepted as it stands (line 191), 9 objections (the head and eight stops), all to wording, to what a stop repeats from or leaves to the cards, and to two readings pressed as facts at 48 and 257; no fact found wrong, two loose paraphrases (243, 257) corrected, and the two uses of "card" at 48 and 125 made to say which card.
