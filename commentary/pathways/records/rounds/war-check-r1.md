# Check of the war pathway (drafts/war.txt), round 1

Checked 20–21 September 2026 against poem.txt, eliot-notes.txt, works.txt, new-works.txt, the cards in site-data.js (litauen, marie, stetson, nerves, rats-alley, lil, hurry-up, mrs-porter, typist, hordes, fisher-king, fragments, and the library record london-bridge), the sibling drafts (dante, grail, wagner, love, method) for the hand-offs, the Licensing Act 1921 as enacted (legislation.gov.uk, ukpga/Geo5/11-12/42, the King's Printer PDF ukpga_19210042_en.pdf; the site gives the Act as PDF only), Hesse's Blick ins Chaos in the Seldwyla printing of 1922 (archive.org bub_gb_vKbrAc4LfyYC, the copy works.txt names), and the scans through ol.py and the writer's paging wrapper olpage.py (Southam 1994 studentsguidetos0000sout_t0e0 and 1996 guidetoselectedp0000sout_a4m2; Rainey 2006 annotatedwastela0000elio; Brooks 1939 modernpoetrytrad0000clea_x3i8 and the 1967 printing; Matthiessen 1935 achievementoftse0000foma_q8p1; Kenner 1959, four scans; Letters vol. 1 in the 1988 edition, lettersoftseliot0000elio_r8o7 and others, and the revised edition, lettersoftseliot0000elio and lettersoftseliot0000elio_h3h2; the facsimile in its 1994 reprint, wastelandfacsimi0000elio_t8k9; Gordon 1977 and Grover Smith 1961 where they turned up). Open Library's leaf numbers are not page numbers and none is given below. The service's ranking is by book, not page, so a common word often hides a commentary beyond the first hundreds of hits; every note below was reached with a distinctive phrase, and where a phrase could not be found that is said.

Every quotation from the poem matches poem.txt ("rats' alley", "Stay with me", "been in the army four years", HURRY UP PLEASE ITS TIME, "a silk hat on a Bradford millionaire", "Falling towers", "Vienna London"). Eliot's notes are quoted exactly: the note on 199 ("was reported to me from Sydney, Australia"), the headnote to Part V ("the present decay of eastern Europe"), and "Eliot's note on lines 367–377" is the right site-numbered form of the note he headed 366–76. Nothing of Eliot's after 1929 is quoted (the 1920 letter is paraphrased, not quoted, and would be quotable anyway). The hand-offs point at stops that exist: dante 69 and 428, grail 71, wagner 202, method 431; the loves pathway has 215 and 249, which cover the typist's scene.

validate.py flags five things, all warnings the writer rightly left: the quotations at 12, 70, 199, 369 and 427 are not in the poem because they are Kenner's, Hayward's (through Southam), Eliot's note's, the headnote's and Southam's words. All five are verified below (the one at 427 with a correction of what Southam calls it).

The seven open points are settled as follows, with the evidence under the stops: (1) the Letters print a letter headed "TO His Mother, MS Houghton, 15 February 1920, 18 Crawford Mansions, My dear dear Mother", and print the sentence "settling all the pre-War Debts between the Bank and Germans, which is an important appointment, full of interesting legal questions"; the citation may stand in the Letters' form (see head). (2) Southam's Hayward is John Hayward, whose notes are in Leyris's French edition of 1947; "Eliot's friend" is not what Southam says of him (see 70). (3) Southam's Bradford note is printed "l. 243" in both the 1994 and 1996 editions, a misprint for 234; cite it as "note on line 234" (see 231). (4) Southam's London Bridge note is keyed "l. 426"; his echo of the rhyme is not in a note on line 62 but in his note on the key lines (412–414; his key for it did not show in a snippet), and the crowd-and-rhyme link belongs to Kenner (see 427). (5) "the capitals of fallen empires" overstates (see head). (6) The Act is dated 17 August 1921; s. 1 gives eight hours ending not later than ten at night, nine hours and eleven in the metropolis; the card at 141 says the hours were cut in the war (see 139). (7) The Letters' editorial note gives the visit "at Montagnola, near Lugano, on 28 May 1922"; Eliot's note misquotes Hesse's "in heiligem" as "im heiligem"; the stop need not say so (see 369).

- line 70. All checked. Matthiessen (1935), achievementoftse0000foma_q8p1: "The name of the battle where Stetson fought is that of one in which the Carthaginians ..."; Brooks gives him the credit: "Mylae, as Mr. Matthiessen has pointed out, is the name ..."; and Southam: "Matthiessen, too, observes that this reference (assimilating the recent World War and Mylae) underlines ...". Brooks (1939): "The Punic War was a trade war—might be considered a rather close parallel to our late war. At any rate, it is plain that Eliot in having the protagonist address the friend in a London street as one who was with him in the Punic War rather than as one who was with him in the World War is making the point that all the wars are one war; all experience, one experience", and, on the same page, "completes the universalization of Stetson begun by the reference to Mylae. Stetson is every man including the ...", so "as every man" is Brooks's own phrase, and the card's "all the wars are one war" (Brooks) and the stop's "All wars are one war" (Hayward in Southam) are both right: two owners, two wordings. Rainey's note is keyed 70 ("[Mylae]: A city on the northern coast of Sicily ..."). The one thing to weigh is "Eliot's friend", under Findings.

Although [no evidence has] been found, it is plausible, since 'alley' was the slang term for the trenches, which were plagued with rats, ominous, familiar ...". The card at 115 gives the trench name and the card at 111 the usual reading. (A small point on "Stay with me" under Findings.)

## Findings

### war head
Kind: fact
Problem: "Eliot wrote the poem in 1921 and published it in October 1922, three years after the Armistice" hangs the three years on October 1922, which is three years and eleven months after 11 November 1918. The three years belong to 1921, the year of the writing (the card at 115 has it right: "less than three years before these lines were written, in 1921"). The rest of the sentence is verified: the facsimile's introduction, in its 1920 section (the running head "xvii 1920 INTRODUCTION" is in the snippet), says he "had been put in charge of settling all the pre-War Debts between the Bank and Germans, 'an important appointment, full of interesting legal questions'", quoting the letter; the Letters print the sentence in Eliot's own words, "settling all the pre-War Debts between the Bank and Germans, which is an important appointment, full of interesting legal questions. I shall have ..." (1988 edition and revised edition alike), and print a letter headed "TO His Mother, MS Houghton, 15 February 1920, 18 Crawford Mansions, My dear dear Mother" (the same day has a letter to Henry Eliot and a postcard to Ottoline Morrell). No snippet shows the sentence and the heading on one page, so the pairing rests on the facsimile's footnote as the writer read it; the volume, the recipient and the date are all confirmed, and the citation "Letters, vol. 1, Eliot to his mother, 15 February 1920" may stand. "The Criterion, October 1922" for the publication is right (works.txt).
Corrected: "Eliot wrote the poem in 1921, three years after the Armistice, and published it in October 1922; at Lloyds Bank, where he worked, he had been put in charge in 1920 of settling the bank’s pre-war debts with Germany." Sources unchanged.

### war head
Kind: judgement
Problem: "the capitals of fallen empires" for lines 375–376 overstates. Of the five, London was the capital of an empire that had not fallen in 1922 (the poem's point is that it is next), and Athens was a city-state, the head of a league, not an imperial capital; Jerusalem, Alexandria and Vienna fit. The commentators do not say it: Brooks (1939), "The cities, Jerusalem, Athens, Alexandria, Vienna, like the London of the first section of the poem are 'unreal'"; Kenner (1959), "the destruction by fire of civilization after civilization"; Gordon (1977), "great cities in the course of time, Jerusalem, Athens, Alexandria, Vienna. For him the fate of such cities is of no importance"; Rainey's note on the Hesse passage says it "refers to the Russian Revolution and the collapse of the German and Austro-Hungarian" empires, which covers Vienna alone. The reader's corrected intro drops the phrase; if the list is kept, name the thing plainly.
Corrected: "... the hooded hordes of Eliot’s note on eastern Europe, the five cities of 375–376, Vienna among them, and London Bridge falling down."

### war line 12

Problem: "The card gives Countess Marie Larisch" points at the wrong card. The card at line 12 is "German, not Russian" (litauen); Larisch is the card at 15–18 (marie). The rest of the stop is verified: Southam's note keyed "l. 12" gives the translation ("'I am not Russian at all; I come from Lithuania; I am a real German.' Stephenson's translation renders this line in broken English ...") and continues "This statement compresses the history of Lithuania's perilous nationhood. Rainey's note keyed "15 [Marie]" has "In 1877 Marie married Georg, Count Larisch von Moennich. In 1889 the archduke was found dead", the stop's very words; his note on 12 is only the translation. Kenner (1959), all four scans: "Another voice evokes the vanished Austro-Hungarian Empire, the inbred malaise of Mayerling, regressive thrills, objectless travels: And when we were children ...", so the quotation is exact.
Corrected: "The card at 15 gives Countess Marie Larisch; Rainey's note on line 15 tells her history, in which the archduke was found dead in 1889, ..." Sources unchanged.

### war line 12

### war line 70
Kind: judgement
Problem: "Eliot's friend John Hayward" is right in fact (Hayward shared Eliot's flat from 1946 to 1957) but it is not how Southam presents him, and nothing on the sources line supports "friend". Southam's introduction says of Leyris's Poèmes 1910–1930 (Paris, 1947) that its editor "was advised by Eliot and John Hayward", that "In 1946–7, Hayward assisted Pierre Leyris in the preparation of Poèmes 1910–1930", and his list of works has "Hayward, John, notes in Leyris"; Gardner (1949) likewise: "Mr John Hayward supplied for this edition" the notes. The value of the quotation for this pathway is that the notes were made with Eliot's advice, which is what a reader should be told.
Corrected: "Southam's note on the line quotes John Hayward, whose notes to the French translation of the poems (1947) were made with Eliot's advice: the Punic war was a trade war, and 'All wars are one war'." Sources unchanged.

### war line 139
Kind: fact
Problem: The Act's hours are a limit, not a fixed closing time. Licensing Act 1921 (11 & 12 Geo. 5, c. 42), dated "[17th August 1921.]", s. 1(1): the hours of sale on week days "shall be as follows, that is to say: eight hours, beginning not earlier than eleven in the morning and ending not later than ten at night, with a break of at least two hours after twelve (noon): Provided that—(a) in the application of this provision to the metropolis 'nine' shall be substituted for 'eight,' and 'eleven at night' shall be substituted for 'ten at night'"; s. 1(2) leaves the hours within those limits to the licensing justices, with default hours ending at ten, or eleven in the metropolis, pending their order; s. 20 defines "the metropolis" as the administrative county of London with the area within four miles of Charing Cross, so "London" is a fair short form. "Eight hours a day ending at ten at night, nine and eleven in London" should therefore read "not later than ten"; and the sentence "nine and eleven" needs its nouns (the reader made the same point). The card at 141 does say the hours were cut in the war ("Pub hours had been cut during the war by government order"), and the Act itself repeals the wartime measures (s. 15: the Intoxicating Liquor (Temporary Restriction) Act, 1914, and the Defence of the Realm (Amendment) (No. 3) Act, 1915, and the Central Control Board (Liquor Traffic)). Everything else in the stop is verified: the card at 139 gives "demobbed" as the slang of 1919 and 1920; Rainey's note keyed 139: "A popular contraction of 'demobilized,' or released from military service"; Southam has a one-line note "l. 139 demobbed" (seen in the love check); line 148, "He's been in the army four years".
Corrected: "HURRY UP PLEASE ITS TIME is closing time, under hours cut in the war (the card at 141) and fixed by the Licensing Act of August 1921: eight hours a day, ending not later than ten at night, or nine hours and eleven o'clock in London." Sources unchanged.

### war line 139
Kind: judgement
Problem: Part I of the Act came into operation two months after it was passed (s. 17), that is on 17 October 1921, and Part II of the poem was written in the first half of 1921, so the pub of the poem kept the wartime Board's hours; the Act fixed them for the poem's readers of 1922, not for its writing. The sentence is true as it stands; the editor may want "and then fixed by".
Corrected: (optional) "... under hours cut in the war (the card at 141) and then fixed by the Licensing Act of August 1921: ..."

### war line 369
Kind: fact
Problem: "Southam's note on the hordes connects them with the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917" misplaces his reading. Southam's note keyed "ll. 368–9" (his numbering; the site's 369–370) refers the hordes to Hesse ("Eliot refers us to Blick ins Chaos (1920) (A Glimpse into Chaos) by Hermann Hesse ...") and then reads the line before them: "the 'Murmur' (line 367) would be the voice of Mother Russia lamenting the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917" (both editions; his 367 is the site's 368, "Murmur of maternal lamentation"). So the Revolution is his gloss on the lamentation, with the hordes referred to Hesse's eastern Europe. The citation "Southam, Guide, note on lines 368–369" is exactly his key and stands.
Corrected: "Southam's note on the lines refers the hordes to Hesse and reads the 'Murmur of maternal lamentation' of 368 as the voice of Mother Russia lamenting the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917; ..." Sources unchanged.

### war line 369

### war line 369
Kind: fact
Problem: "Hesse's <i>Blick ins Chaos</i>, the essay on the downfall of Europe" makes the book an essay. The title page reads "Blick ins Chaos. Drei Aufsätze" (three essays), and the passage Eliot quotes is the close of the first, headed "Die Brüder Karamasoff oder Der Untergang Europas. Einfälle bei der Lektüre Dostojewskis" (archive.org bub_gb_vKbrAc4LfyYC, the 1922 printing), where the sentence stands: "Schon ist halb Europa, schon ist zumindest der halbe Osten Europas auf dem Wege zum Chaos, fährt betrunken in heiligem Wahn am Abgrund entlang und singt dazu". The sources line has the essay right; the text should agree with it and with the card at this line ("three essays, two of them on Dostoevsky; the passage is the close of the first").
Corrected: "Eliot's note on lines 367–377 quotes the close of the first essay in Hermann Hesse's <i>Blick ins Chaos</i> (1920), on <i>The Brothers Karamazov</i> and the downfall of Europe, where half of Europe, or at least its eastern half, is on the road to chaos, drunk and singing." Sources unchanged.

### war line 369
Kind: judgement
Problem: Open point (7). The visit is verified: the Letters' editorial note to the letter "TO Hermann Hesse, TS Schiller-Nationalmuseum, 13 March 1922, 12 Wigmore St, London W.1, Monsieur, Pendant ..." reads in the 1988 edition "TSE visited him at Montagnola, near Lugano, on 28 May 1922" and in the revised edition "would visit him at Montagnola, near Lugano, on 28 May 1922", and the volume also prints a postcard to Ottoline Morrell of 28 May 1922 from Lugano and a letter to Hesse of 24 May 1922 from the Hotel Bristol, Lugano; Crawford (Young Eliot) has the same 13 March letter. So "Letters, vol. 1, Eliot to Hermann Hesse, 13 March 1922" is the right locator for the note, and the sentence is true, though the card at this line already has it (with "Montagnola" where the stop says "near Lugano"; both are the editors' words). The misquotation: Eliot's note prints "fährt betrunken im heiligem Wahn"; Hesse's text has "in heiligem Wahn", and the card's German quotes Hesse correctly. It is a slip of Eliot's or his printer's, of one letter, and it has nothing to do with the war; the stop should not carry it. It belongs, if anywhere, to the Notes lens or the card, as a one-clause aside ("Eliot's note prints 'im heiligem' for Hesse's 'in heiligem'").
Corrected: no change to the stop for this point; the editor may drop the last sentence as the reader proposes and keep the Letters citation only if the sentence stays.

### war line 427
Kind: fact
Problem: Three things. (a) "The card gives the rhyme": no card is keyed at 427; the rhyme is given by the card at 431–434 ("The last eight lines"), which opens with it, and by the library record attached to this line. (b) "Southam's note on the line prints its first verse, 'London Bridge is falling down, falling down, / London Bridge is falling down'": Southam's note is keyed "l. 426" (his numbering; the site's 427), and it prints three lines and calls them the refrain: "'London Bridge is falling down, falling down, / London Bridge is falling down, / My fair lady', the refrain of a nursery rhyme. Until 1750 London's only bridge over the Thames, by the eighteenth century it was in a ruinous state and the nursery rhyme declares how it can be set right." The stop drops the third line without a mark and calls the refrain a verse. (c) "his note on line 62 allows an echo of it already in the crowd flowing over the bridge in Part I": not found, and what Southam does say is elsewhere. His one other mention of the rhyme is in his note on the key lines (412–414), the note that explains Eliot's references for them ("Dante, Inferno xxxiii, 46, quoting the words of Ugolino della Gherardesca, a thirteenth-century Italian ..." and Bradley): "... the captives left to starve'. Beyond these references may also stand an echo of the nursery rhyme 'London Bridge is falling down' (see note to line 426). Later in the song come the lines 'Take the key and lock her up ...'" (both editions; the printed key of this note did not show in a snippet, and it is not cited here). The reading the stop wants, that the crowd on the bridge at 62 is already tied to the rhyme, is Kenner's (1959), all four scans: "... a communal life now sacrificed to abstract transportation— A crowd flowed over London Bridge, so many, I had not thought death had undone so many.) is linked by the nursery rhyme with feudal rituals ('gold and silver, my fair lady') and festivals ...". Searches of both Southam scans for the rhyme with "crowd flowed over London Bridge", "nursery rhyme", "62" and "falling down" found no note on 62 that says it.
Corrected: "The card at 431 gives the rhyme. Southam's note on the line prints its refrain, 'London Bridge is falling down, falling down, / London Bridge is falling down, / My fair lady', and Hugh Kenner (1959) linked the crowd flowing over the bridge in Part I with the rhyme. The bridge that crowd crossed comes back here as the first of the fragments, a nursery tune after 'Falling towers' and 'Vienna London'. ..." Sources:
    sources: Southam, Guide, note on line 426
    Kenner (1959)

### war line 427
