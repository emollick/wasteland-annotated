# Reader's report on the pathway "passion" (The Bible and the Prayer Book), round 1

Read as the brief asks: STANDARD.md, then drafts/passion.txt with poem.txt open, the lines at each stop before the note. Since glosses-index.txt gives only the first 160 characters of each card, I also read the full text of the card at every stop (from the site's js/data.js), because the brief's test of use turns on whether a stop says something the card at its line does not. The King James verses the stops quote all match the Gutenberg text; the committal ("earth to earth ... in sure and certain hope of the Resurrection to eternal life") matches the Church of England's text of the 1662 burial service; the two Augustine quotations match Pusey's translation on Gutenberg. I did not check Brooks, Kenner, Gardner, Southam or the Dial; those are the scholar's.

## Accepted as they stand

None of the fourteen records as written. The title stands. The matter of every stop is sound as far as I can check it, the theme is honestly in the poem at every line, and read through the pathway holds together as one theme: at 1, 25, 322, 360, 391 and 426 the same thing is shown each time, the poem taking a passage's words and leaving out what the passage goes on to (the resurrection, the shelter, the recognition, the bones standing up, the reprieve), and 67, 259, 307 and 367 are looser but each says why it is here. The objections are to wording, to what the cards at the lines already give (which is a great deal: at 20, 25, 307, 322, 360, 367, 391, 426 and 434 the card already has most of the stop), to one placement (259, which belongs at 264), to one fact marked [to check] (the Ecclesiastes bracket at 25), and to one disagreement between a card and a stop (the date at 434), which PATHWAYS.md has since settled for the stop.

## The two questions asked

The head. What the pathway is for is said, and said plainly, in the intro's last sentence: it "follows what the poem does with the scriptures it quotes, and says at each stop what a passage takes from them and what it leaves out". A reader who gets that far knows. But the why line, which is what a reader sees wherever the pathway is offered, is forty words of contents, the same contents the intro gives again at greater length (the burial service, the prophets, the Passion, Emmaus, "Shantih"), and it does not say what the pathway does with them; it can be shorter, and the version below is thirty-two words. What the pathway will not do is said in the compressed form "this pathway does not read the poem as a conversion", after a baptism date joined to it by a semicolon; a first-time reader has to work out that "as a conversion" means as the record of one or as a step towards Eliot's, and why 1927 is in the sentence at all. Both are said plainly below.

The last sentences. Line 1, "The part buries them and raises none": a bow. The sentence before it has already put the part's dead under a service that promises resurrection; the one new thing, that nothing rises in Part I, is delivered as an antithesis, and the figure is loose, since the part buries no one (the crowd walks, the corpse was "planted", and it is asked whether it has begun to sprout). Line 25, "The shelter the prophet promised is where death is shown": a bow, and a paradox for effect. The two sentences before it have said that Isaiah's rock is a shelter and that under this one the reader is shown fear in a handful of dust, the burial service's dust; the sentence adds nothing. Line 391, "Its crow is followed by lightning and rain": says only what lines 392 to 395 show, and the card at this line adds that the poem never says the rain falls; coming after Brooks's cock that "chases away the powers of evil", it hints that the crow brings the rain without saying so or saying whose reading that is. All three should go; what each stop has to say is said in its body. Three others end the same way in a smaller degree: 67 ("The reading is some readers', not the note's") restates the sentences before it; 259 quotes "Inexplicable" back at the visible line; 367 closes on a callback, "as the Passion was told without its names".

## Objections

### passion head
Problem: The why line is at the limit (forty words) and is a second table of contents; it repeats the intro and does not say what the pathway is for. In the intro, "does not read the poem as a conversion" is too compressed, and the baptism is given with no word of why (see above); "the Passion told without its names" is odd (whose names?), and a reader is not told that the Passion is the arrest and death of Christ until the stop at 322; Isaiah is given as a source on a level with Ezekiel and Ecclesiastes, which Eliot's notes give, when the card at 25 calls it a commentators' comparison; and at 156 words the intro has no room for plainer sentences, so the list of the middle stops (182, 264, 307) is cut below to make it, since the guide shows them. Cleanth Brooks, Augustine and "the Communion service" are otherwise fine as they are.
Corrected:
title: The Bible and the Prayer Book
intro: The Bible and the Book of Common Prayer in the poem. Part I is named after the Prayer Book’s burial service; the desert of lines 19 to 30 speaks in words from Ezekiel and Ecclesiastes, and its rock is usually set beside Isaiah’s. Part V opens with the arrest and death of Christ told without his name, as Cleanth Brooks read it in 1939, and goes on to the road to Emmaus, where the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples; Eliot’s last note glosses “Shantih” with the blessing that ends the Communion service. Eliot was baptised into the Church of England in June 1927, five years after the poem. This pathway does not read the poem as the record of a conversion, or as a step towards one. It follows what the poem does with the scriptures it quotes, and says at each stop what a passage keeps of its source and what it does not.
why: What the poem takes from the Bible and the Prayer Book, and what it leaves out, from the burial service that names Part I to the blessing quoted in Eliot’s last note.

### passion line 1
Problem: The first sentence runs fifty words with two quotations inside it. "The part's dead, the crowd on London Bridge, the corpse in the garden" reads as a list, and the crowd and the corpse are sixty and seventy lines ahead of a reader at line 1, so they want their line numbers. The last sentence ties a bow (see above); the point is better made from the poem, which asks its questions over the corpse and gets no answer. No card covers the part title, so the service is the stop's own.
Corrected: Part I is named after the Prayer Book's "Order for the Burial of the Dead". The service begins "I am the resurrection and the life" and commits the body "earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust; in sure and certain hope of the Resurrection to eternal life". The part so named has its dead, the crowd of line 62 and the corpse of line 71, and none of the service's hope; the questions asked over the corpse, "Has it begun to sprout?" among them, get no answer.

### passion line 20
Problem: The first sentence and most of the second are already on the card at this line (Eliot's note to Ezekiel 2:1, "a rebellious nation", the note on 23 to Ecclesiastes 12, and "A heap of broken images" traced to Ezekiel 6), and the card's references already cite Rainey's note on 22; "already done" hangs at the end of a clause, and "Lawrence Rainey's note" comes without a word of what it is a note in. The stop's own matter, that Ezekiel's son of man is set on his feet to speak and this one is told he cannot say, and that the threat is here already carried out, should carry it.
Corrected: The card at this line gives Ezekiel and Ecclesiastes, and traces the "heap of broken images" to Ezekiel 6. Ezekiel's "Son of man" is set on his feet to speak and sent to "a rebellious nation"; the one addressed here is told he "cannot say, or guess". In Ezekiel the breaking of the images is a threat, "your images shall be broken" (6:4); here it has already happened.

### passion line 25
Problem: Isaiah 32:2 is already on the card at this line ("commentators cite Isaiah 32:2"), so the verse should be pointed at, not given again; "Southam's note" drops Southam bare; the bracket "(Genesis 3:19, and Ecclesiastes 12:7)" follows a quotation that is Genesis's only (Ecclesiastes 12:7 says "Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was") [to check whether the writer meant to quote both], and the colon-and-three-quotations sentence is heavy. The last sentence ties a bow (see above). The dust reading is the pathway's own, against the card's Tennyson and Sibyl, and should be marked as such.
Corrected: The card at this line gives Isaiah 32:2; Helen Gardner (1949) set the verse beside this rock, and B. C. Southam's <i>Guide</i> records it. In Isaiah the rock is "the shadow of a great rock in a weary land", a shelter promised for the reign of a righteous king. What is shown under this one is "fear in a handful of dust", and the dust, on this pathway, is that of Genesis 3:19, "dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return", and of the burial service, "dust to dust".

### passion line 67
Problem: "Dante's crowd of the dead" drops Dante bare on a pathway that has not mentioned him; Eliot's note ("A phenomenon which I have often noticed") is already quoted on the card at this line; and the last sentence, "The reading is some readers', not the note's", only says over again what the two sentences before it have shown. The card also gives Eliot's hours at Lloyds, a few doors along, 9.15 to 5.30, which is the plainest answer to the ninth-hour reading and worth pointing at. The reading itself is rightly given with Southam as its recorder.
Corrected: The card at this line gives the church, and Eliot's hours at the bank round the corner. The "final stroke of nine" has been read as the ninth hour of the Crucifixion, when Christ cried out and died (Matthew 27:46, Mark 15:34). B. C. Southam's <i>Guide</i>, in its note on line 68, records the reading and points out that the Gospels' ninth hour is the ninth of daylight, about three in the afternoon, not nine in the morning.

### passion line 182
Problem: The opening, "Psalm 137 in the Prayer Book's wording:", is a verbless fragment on a colon and repeats the card's first words; "is that exile" gives a reading as a fact; "the pathway meets him again at line 264" is the pathway talking about itself. The matter (the harps, the captors' request, the exile set between Spenser's lines, Coverdale) is the stop's own and good, and the card has none of it beyond the first verse.
Corrected: The card gives the wording and the lake. In the psalm the exiles in Babylon hang up their harps when their captors ask for "one of the songs of Sion", and answer "How shall we sing the Lord's song: in a strange land?" The singer, between two lines of Spenser's wedding-song for the Thames, speaks as that exile, and his song is short, "I speak not loud or long". The Prayer Book's psalms are Miles Coverdale's translation, of the 1530s; his tablet is in the church of line 264.

### passion line 259
Problem: Opened at 259 the reader has the bar and the mandoline on the screen and the church four lines down, and the note's first word is the church; the intro promises "a City church at 264", Eliot's note is on 264, and the church is named there, so the stop belongs at 264 (or at 263, where the sentence about its walls begins). The first sentence and the County Council's report are on the card at this line, which gives the 1920 scheme in detail; "proposed for demolition" has no date; and the last sentence quotes "Inexplicable" back at the visible line, with only "the poem's one church interior" added, which can stand on its own. The London Letter and Coverdale's tablet are the stop's own.
Corrected (line: 264; the text also reads at 259): The card gives the church and the 1920 scheme to demolish nineteen City churches, this among them. In his London Letter to <i>The Dial</i> of May 1921 Eliot wrote that the City churches give the business quarter "a beauty which its hideous banks and commercial houses have not quite defaced". The County Council's report, which his note on line 264 cites, records the tablet to Miles Coverdale, once rector here and reburied here, whose psalms are the Prayer Book's (line 182). It is the poem's one church interior.

### passion line 307
Problem: Nearly all of it is on the card at this line: Book III in Pusey's translation, Book X with the same "pluckest me out" quotation, the pairing with the Buddha, and Eliot's note calling it "eastern and western asceticism" and "not an accident". The first two sentences are verbless fragments ("Augustine's Confessions, Book III, in Pusey's translation, which Eliot's note quotes." and "At line 309, Book X, from the chapter on the temptation of the eyes:"), and Augustine is never said to be anyone. The stop's own matter is three things, that the voice is a convert's, that the cry comes from the chapter on the temptation of the eyes, and that these are the poem's only words addressed to God. (Eliot's note on the pairing is numbered 312 in eliot-notes.txt; the card at this line and the design's editor both call it the note on line 309, so the number stands.)
Corrected: The card gives both passages, the Carthage of Book III and the "Thou pluckest me out" of Book X, and Eliot's note on line 309 pairing Augustine with the Buddha. Augustine's <i>Confessions</i>, written about 400, are a convert's account of his life, and what the poem takes from them is the convert's memory of his lust and, from the chapter on the temptation of the eyes, his cry to be pulled free. "O Lord Thou pluckest me out" are the poem's only words addressed to God.

### passion line 322
Problem: The first two sentences repeat the card at this line almost word for word (the arrest with "lanterns and torches and weapons", Gethsemane and "being in an agony", the death), and the card also has "The lines stop at the death"; the opening "The Passion without its names." is a fragment, and "its names" is odd. What is the stop's own is Brooks's reading and the "we" of line 329. The head credits Brooks with the Passion reading, so the stop should say he read the lines so, and it should say what Eliot's own headnote, which the card quotes, names instead, since that is the one place the poet speaks of these lines.
Corrected: The card gives the Passion, the arrest, trial and death of Christ, event by event, and Eliot's headnote to Part V, which names three themes for the section's first part and does not name this. Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the lines as the Passion, and wrote that the gardens' frosty silence "associates Christ in Gethsemane with the other hanged gods", the gods of Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i>. After the death the gospels tell the resurrection. The poem does not; its "we" are "now dying".

### passion line 360
Problem: The first sentence restates the card at this line (Luke 24, the stranger, the bread, the vanishing), and the card also has Eliot's note on line 46 and the Hanged Man; "Luke 24:" opens on a colon without a verb; "the question is never answered" does not say which question. The stop's own point, that the poem keeps the unknown companion and leaves out the recognition, should carry it. The sources line cites Shackleton's South for a claim the text does not make (the text never mentions Shackleton); the card has him, and the corrected text points at it.
Corrected: The card gives Luke's story and Shackleton's, and Eliot's note on line 46 associating the Hanged Man with the hooded figure. In Luke the two disciples know the stranger when he breaks bread, and he vanishes. The poem keeps the unknown companion, "wrapt in a brown mantle, hooded", the only form in which the risen Christ enters it, and leaves out the recognition; the speaker cannot tell a man from a woman, and the question the line asks is never answered.

### passion line 367
Problem: The Luke 23 reading is already on the card at line 368 ("'Maternal lamentation' has been connected with the women who 'bewailed and lamented' Christ on the way to the cross"), as is Hesse; what the stop adds is Kenner as an owner of the reading, the verse itself, Jerusalem at 375 and "no mourner in sight". "Hermann Hesse" is dropped without a word of what he wrote, and the last clause, "as the Passion was told without its names", is a callback that ties a bow and repeats the odd phrase from 322.
Corrected: The card at line 368 gives Hermann Hesse's essay on eastern Europe, which Eliot's note on these lines quotes, and the other reading, of the women who followed Christ to the cross; Hugh Kenner (1959) is one who made it. Those women were told "Daughters of Jerusalem, weep not for me, but weep for yourselves, and for your children" (Luke 23:28), and Jerusalem heads the five cities named at line 375. The lamentation here is a sound "high in the air", with no mourner in sight.

### passion line 391
Problem: Five sentences, one of them a fragment ("Then the cock."), and the opening "Ezekiel 37:" is verbless. The card at this line already gives the cock that crowed at Peter's denial and the cock of Hamlet; the stop gives Hamlet again and leaves out Peter's cock, which is the one that belongs on a Bible pathway. The last sentence ties a bow (see above). The valley of bones is told well and is the stop's own, and the "Son of man" asked there is the prophet of line 20, which is worth saying.
Corrected: In Ezekiel 37 the prophet of line 20 is set down in a valley of bones and asked "Son of man, can these bones live?"; he prophesies to them, and they stand up "an exceeding great army". Here no one prophesies, and the bones can neither live nor harm. The card at this line gives the cock that crowed at Peter's denial and the cock of <i>Hamlet</i>; Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the poem's cock as the bird of folk-lore "whose voice chases away the powers of evil".

### passion line 426
Problem: The card at line 424 already has Isaiah's message, the wall, the weeping, the fifteen years, "lands" for "house" and the unanswered question; the stop retells it all, opens "Isaiah 38:1:" without a verb, drops "Weston's Fisher King" without a word of who Weston is, and ends on two quoted fragments in a row ("at least", "my lands"). What the pathway adds is the reprieve the poem leaves out and the command turned into a hedged question.
Corrected: The card at line 424 gives Isaiah's message to King Hezekiah, "sick unto death", and the fifteen years the king was given when he turned his face to the wall and wept; Hugh Kenner (1959) and B. C. Southam's <i>Guide</i> record the verse. The poem's king takes the sentence without the reprieve. Isaiah's command has become a question, and the setting in order is hedged with "at least".

### passion line 434
Problem: The card at this line already has Philippians 4:7, "the Anglican blessing", "a feeble translation" and the change to "our equivalent"; the second sentence says the first over again ("The poem's last word is glossed by its author with the Church's blessing"), and "the words stood in Poems 1909–1925" is hard to follow (which words, and stood how?). The card and the stop disagree on a date: the card says the note read "our equivalent to this word" from <i>Poems 1909–1925</i> (1925); the stop and its sources say the 1926 impression kept "feeble translation" and the change came in the resetting of 1932. PATHWAYS.md now records that the checking scholars found the stop right (Ricks and McCue's textual note gives 1932) and that the card is to be corrected, so the stop's 1932 stands and the corrected text keeps it. What the stop adds, and should keep, is which service the blessing ends, its wording, and the poem's two ends, the burial service and the Communion blessing.
Corrected: The card gives the note and Philippians 4:7. The note's English is the blessing that ends the Prayer Book's Communion service, "The peace of God, which passeth all understanding, keep your hearts and minds"; the poem that began under the burial service ends with a Sanskrit word glossed by its author with the Communion blessing. In 1922 the note called this "a feeble translation"; the wording stood in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>, and when that book was reset in 1932 it became "is our equivalent to this word".
