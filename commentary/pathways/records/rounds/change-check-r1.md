### change line 99
Kind: fact
Problem: The sources line "Ovid, Metamorphoses VI 667–674" does not cover the pursuit the stop describes. In the Latin Library's text the marker 665 stands at "flet modo seque vocat bustum miserabile nati", so the sword-drawn chase, "nunc sequitur nudo genitas Pandione ferro", is 666; 667 is "corpora Cecropidum pennis pendere putares", where the sisters are already on wings; 668–670 give the two birds and the marks of the murder still on the breast ("neque adhuc de pectore caedis / excessere notae, signataque sanguine pluma est"); 674 ("nomen epops volucri") is Tereus's hoopoe. The text of the stop is right; the range starts one line late.
Corrected: sources line "Ovid, Metamorphoses VI 666–674" in place of 667–674; the other two lines unchanged.

### change line 243
Kind: fact
Problem: "Struck into a woman for seven years and back into a man" reverses Ovid: Tiresias struck, and was not struck. III 325–332 (the marker 325 stands at "corpora serpentum baculi violaverat ictu"): "nam duo magnorum viridi coeuntia silva / corpora serpentum baculi violaverat ictu / deque viro factus (mirabile) femina septem / egerat autumnos; octavo rursus eosdem / vidit, et 'est vestrae si tanta potentia plagae' / dixit, 'ut auctoris sortem in contraria mutet, / nunc quoque vos feriam.' percussis anguibus isdem / forma prior rediit, genetivaque venit imago." He struck two coupling snakes with his staff and was made a woman; seven autumns later, in the eighth year, he struck the same snakes again and his former shape returned. The rest of the stop checks: "seven years" (327–328), "known love from both sides" ("Venus huic erat utraque nota", 324), "called in to judge which side has the more pleasure" (Jupiter's "maior vestra profecto est / quam quae contingit maribus ... voluptas", Juno's denial, "placuit quae sit sententia docti / quaerere Tiresiae", 321–324, and "arbiter", 333). One more word: "the one the poem prints in full" is the note's printing, not the poem's, as the next sentence says. The card at 218 already tells the snakes, so the stop need only get the agent right.
Corrected: Tiresias's change is the one the poem's notes print in full. Eliot's note on line 218 gives Ovid's Latin and says that the two sexes meet in him. A woman for seven years after striking two coupling snakes, and a man again when he struck them a second time, he had known love from both sides, and was called in to judge which side has the more pleasure. So "foresuffered all / Enacted on this same divan or bed" is said by one who has been both parties. (88 words; sources unchanged.)

### change line 315
Kind: judgement
Problem: "Cleanth Brooks (1939) took Phlebas for the drowned god of the fertility cults, whose death is 'the way into life'" hardens Brooks. Both sentences are in his discussion of Madame Sosostris's cards, not of Part IV, and both are hedged: "The drowned Phoenician Sailor recalls the drowned god of the fertility cults. Miss Weston tells that each year..." and "she warns him against death by water, not realizing any more than do the other inhabitants of the modern waste land that the way into life may be by death itself. The drowned Phoenician Sailor is a type of the..." (bwb_P9-AUL-981, the 1939 printing). On Part IV he writes only that "The general function of the section, 'Death by Water,' is readily apparent. The section forms a contrast with 'The Fire Sermon' which precedes it"; no sentence of his naming Phlebas was found. "Recalls" and "may be" are his words; "took for" and "is" are not.
Corrected: The poem's own drowned man has no sea-change. The current picks his bones "in whispers" and passes the body back through "the stages of his age and youth" into the whirlpool. The French original, on the card, has the current carry him off through the stages of his former life; the bones and the whirlpool are additions of the English. Cleanth Brooks (1939) said the drowned Phoenician Sailor "recalls the drowned god of the fertility cults", whose death may be "the way into life". (84 words.)

### change line 429
Kind: fact
Problem: "The swallow is Procne, Philomel's sister" is a reading given as a fact, and its owner, Brooks, is on the sources line but not in the text. Brooks 1939: "the quotation from Pervigilium Veneris: 'When shall I be like the swallow.' The allusion is also connected with the Philomela symbol ... The sister of Philomela was changed into a swallow as Philomela was changed into a nightingale. The protagonist is asking therefore when shall the spring, the time of..." (bwb_P9-AUL-981). The Pervigilium does not make the identification: in the lines before (the site's linked copy, la.wikisource), "Adsonat Terei puella subter umbram populi: / Ut putes motus amoris ore dici musico, / Et neges, queri sororem de marito barbaro. / Illa cantat; nos tacemus", the singing bird is Tereus's "puella", a sister complaining of a barbarous husband, that is, the wife, Procne herself; and Ovid VI 668–669, "quarum petit altera silvas, / altera tecta subit", names neither sister's bird, as the card at line 99 says ("Which sister became the nightingale he leaves open"). So "Ovid, Metamorphoses VI 667–674" cannot support the sentence and should go. A smaller point: "The Latin ends the Pervigilium Veneris". In the standard text the line (90) is followed by "perdidi Musam tacendo, nec me Phoebus respicit. / sic Amyclas, cum tacerent, perdidit silentium." and the refrain (91–93); in the site's linked copy, the Romagnosi edition of 1848, by the refrain alone. "From the last stanza" is true of both. The rest checks: "illa cantat, nos tacemus", the question "quando fiam uti chelidon, ut tacere desinam?" (the linked copy reads "Quando fiam, ut chelidon, et tacere desinam?"), the poem stopping before the "ut" clause, Eliot's note "Cf. Philomela in Parts II and III", and Tennyson's song in Part IV.
Corrected: The last change in the poem is asked for, not made. The Latin is from the last stanza of the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i>: the nightingale sings, the poet is silent and asks when he will become like the swallow and cease to be silent; the poem stops before that clause. Eliot's note here refers to Philomela in Parts II and III, and Cleanth Brooks (1939) took the swallow for Procne, Philomel's sister. Tennyson's "O Swallow, Swallow" in <i>The Princess</i> is a lover's message sent south with the bird. (87 words; "a late Latin poem for Venus's spring festival" is on the card at the last lines and can be restored if the Tennyson sentence is shortened.) Corrected sources: Pervigilium Veneris, the last stanza / Tennyson, The Princess, IV ("O Swallow, Swallow") / Brooks (1939).

### change line 429
Kind: judgement
Problem: Whether to add Rainey's note on the reading. Rainey's note is keyed to 428, his count (running head "EDITOR'S ANNOTATIONS TO LINE 428", lemma "428 [Quando fiam . . . swallow]: Eliot's note refers..."); it records that the Boni and Liveright text reads "Quando fiam ceu chelidon" and "The text reads the same in every early printing: in the Dial...", and that Eliot "unequivocally wrote and typed 'ceu chelidon,' not 'uti chelidon'", with a reference to the facsimile, pp. 80–81 and 88–89. The site's card "The last eight lines" already says all of this ("Every printing from 1922 to 1932, and Eliot's fair copies, read 'ceu'; the standard Latin text has 'uti,' and Eliot's line was changed to 'uti' in Collected Poems 1909–1935 (1936). Lawrence Rainey, in his annotated edition of the poem, doubts that the change was Eliot's."), so the stop should not repeat it, and it has no room to. Two notes for the works list rather than the stop: the la.wikisource copy the site links is the Romagnosi edition of 1848, unnumbered, reading "ut chelidon" and "quando ver venit novum?" and lacking lines 91–92, so "lines 89–93" in works.txt is a count from another edition; and A. M. Cook's Latin Anthology (1912; latinanthologyse00cookuoft) prints "quando fiam ceu chelidon ut tacere desinam?", so "ceu" was a printed reading in Eliot's day, not only his. Ricks and McCue's scan did not answer for "chelidon".
Corrected: add nothing to the stop; if a pointer is wanted, "the card at the last lines gives the reading", and on the sources line "Rainey, Annotated Waste Land, note on line 428" (his key, not the site's 429).
