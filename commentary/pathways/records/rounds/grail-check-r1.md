## Findings

### grail line 386
Kind: fact
Problem: "Brooks (1939) called it 'perhaps also the Perilous Chapel'; Southam's note says it is" attaches Brooks's hedge to the wrong thing, and the contrast it draws (Brooks hedges, Southam affirms) does not survive. The sentence in Brooks (1939 printing, bwb_P9-AUL-981; the same in every scan) is said of Nerval's ruined tower, "Le Prince d'Aquitaine à la tour abolie", line 430, in his account of the closing fragments: "The ruined tower is perhaps also the Perilous Chapel, 'only the wind's home,' and it is also the whole tradition in decay. The protagonist resolves to claim his tradition ..." His "perhaps" is whether the tower is also the chapel. The chapel of lines 386–395 he takes for the Perilous Chapel without hedging: he calls the passage "the passage on the Perilous Chapel" ("proper preparation for the passage on the Perilous Chapel which follows it"), the journey "the journey to the Perilous Chapel of the Grail story", and adds "The visit to the Perilous Chapel, according to Miss Weston, was an initiation". Southam (note on his l. 388) has "this is the Chapel Perilous of medieval legend, surrounded by horrors". So Brooks and Southam agree, and the stop should say so. (The reader's rewording of the last sentence is compatible with the text: the poem says the gust is "Bringing rain" and does not say that it falls.)
Corrected: The card at line 389 gives Weston's Perilous Chapel, an adventure she says is "fraught with extreme peril to life". The poem's chapel has no body, no hand and no voice, "only the wind's home", and "Dry bones can harm no one". Brooks (1939) read these lines as "the passage on the Perilous Chapel", and Southam's note calls it "the Chapel Perilous of medieval legend". What follows is what the land has lacked since line 24, a cock, a flash of lightning and "a damp gust / Bringing rain".
Sources unchanged: Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. XIII / Brooks (1939) / Southam, Guide, note on line 388.

### grail line 1
Kind: judgement
Problem: The quotation is exact: the 1939 printing (bwb_P9-AUL-981, leaf 278) reads "Men are afraid to live in reality. April, the month of rebirth, is not the most joyful season but the cruelest. Winter at least kept us warm in forgetful snow", and every American reprint keeps "cruelest"; only British reprints (Rajan's collection, 1947) re-spell it "cruellest". So "cruelest" is Brooks's spelling, not a misquotation, and beside the poem's "cruellest" (line 1) a reader may take it for one. A quotation is exact or it is not one, so I would keep his spelling as it stands; if the editor prefers not to set the two spellings side by side, the recast below ends the quotation before the word, as the reader proposed.
Corrected: text as it stands (keeping "cruelest"); or, if recast: Frazer's rites exist to bring the year back. The dead, he writes, "came back in the sprouting corn" and were imagined to return in the spring flowers, "the violets and the hyacinths", which "sprang from their dust". The poem's first voice feels that return as cruelty and prefers the "forgetful snow". Cleanth Brooks, who in 1939 read the poem through Weston and Frazer, wrote that its people "are afraid to live in reality", so that April, "the month of rebirth, is not the most joyful season" but the cruellest.
Sources unchanged.

### grail line 19
Kind: judgement
Problem: Two statements about the poem are stronger than the text allows. "The poem's fullest picture of the waste land" is contestable when lines 331–359 give twenty-nine lines of rock without water; "first picture" is safe. "No rain reaches it until the 'damp gust / Bringing rain' of line 394" ignores "Dull roots with spring rain" (line 4) and "With a shower of rain" (line 9); what is true is that the drought of these lines is not broken until Part V. Everything from Weston is right (ch. III, lines 1122–1150).
Corrected: These lines are the poem's first picture of the waste land, dry stone and dead tree and "no sound of water", before any quest has begun. Weston traces the Grail hero's oldest task, the freeing of the waters, to the <i>Rig-Veda</i>, whose hymns praise Indra for killing the giant that had "imprisoned" the rivers: "Indra has filled the rivers, he has inundated the dry land." This is such a land before the giant is killed; its drought is not broken until the "damp gust / Bringing rain" of line 394.
Sources unchanged.

### grail line 43
Kind: judgement
Problem: "holds that ... the pack was first used 'to predict the rise and fall of the waters ...'" makes a hedged suggestion of Weston's into her position. Ch. VI (lines 2600–2604): "But if the connection with the Egyptian and Chinese monuments, referred to above, is genuine, the original use of the 'Tarot' would seem to have been, not to foretell the Future in general, but to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land." The four suits are stated flatly ("They exist to-day as the four suits of the Tarot", line 2533), so that half stands.
Corrected: Weston's chapter on the symbols holds that the Grail's four talismans survive as the four suits of the Tarot, and suggests that the pack may first have been used "to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land". Brooks (1939) wrote that Madame Sosostris "has fallen a long way from the high function of her predecessors" and "is engaged merely in vulgar fortune-telling". Eliot's note on line 46 ties one of her cards, the man with three staves, "quite arbitrarily" to the Fisher King.
Sources unchanged.

### grail line 71
Kind: judgement
Problem: Three points. (a) "Corn-stuffed effigies" is not a conflation of the card's tomb figures with the sowing rite: Frazer's exact wording for the Khoiak burial is "at the great festival of sowing in the month of Khoiak the priests used to bury effigies of Osiris made of earth and corn" (ch. XXXIX, lines 19968–19970), but a few lines later he himself writes "the burial of corn-stuffed images of Osiris in the earth at the festival of sowing was designed to quicken the seed" (lines 20004–20005), beside "corn-stuffed figures" for the tomb burials (line 19995) and, in ch. XL, "the corn-stuffed effigies of Osiris found in Egyptian tombs" (line 20250). Either phrase is Frazer's; "effigies of the god made of earth and corn" is the wording of the passage the stop quotes from, and the editor may prefer it since the card uses "corn-stuffed" of the tomb figures. (b) "a man who would keep the corpse in the ground, safe from the frost and from the Dog" is a reading stated as a fact, and line 73 ("Or has the sudden frost disturbed its bed?") fears the frost for the sprouting rather than for the corpse; the reader's rewording (the man asks, in the same speech, that the Dog be kept from digging the corpse up again) keeps to what the lines say. (c) The second citation, Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXIII (The Gardens of Adonis), supports nothing in the note: all of it is ch. XXXIX, and ch. XXXIII does not mention Osiris.
Corrected: In Frazer the priests of Osiris buried effigies of the god made of earth and corn at the sowing and took them up with the corn "found to have sprouted from the body of Osiris", a sprouting hailed as the cause of the crops (the card at this line gives the rites). "Will it bloom this year?" is the question the rite asks. The poem gives it to a man who asks, in the same speech, that the Dog be kept from digging the corpse up again.
Sources: Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXIX only.

### grail line 312
Kind: judgement
Problem: The second citation, Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. IV, is not used: the note names Frazer for the Alexandrian rite and Brooks for the reading, and nothing in it comes from Weston. Ch. IV does describe the same rite in its own terms (at Alexandria "a Head, of papyrus, representing the god, was, with every show of mourning, committed to the waves", lines 1683–1686), so the editor may either drop the citation or let the note say that Weston tells the same story.
Corrected: sources: Frazer, The Golden Bough (1922), ch. XXXII / Brooks (1939); or keep Weston ch. IV and add a clause that uses it.
