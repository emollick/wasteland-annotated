### passion line 20
Kind: fact
Problem: "Lawrence Rainey's note on line 22 hears the echo." Rainey's note is hedged: "22 [broken images]: Perhaps an echo of Ezekiel 6:4, in which God judges …" (annotatedwastela0000elio, scan search "Ezekiel 6" "broken images"). The stop turns a possibility into a hearing. Everything else in the stop is verified (see above).
Corrected: Eliot's note sends "Son of man" to Ezekiel 2:1, where God sets the prophet on his feet to speak to "a rebellious nation"; his note on line 23 sends the cricket to Ecclesiastes 12, the chapter on old age and the grave. The desert speaks in the prophets' words, but this son of man is told he "cannot say, or guess", and the "heap of broken images" is what Ezekiel's God threatened, "your images shall be broken" (6:4), already done. Lawrence Rainey's note on line 22 offers the verse as a possible echo. — Sources unchanged.

### passion line 25
Kind: fact
Problem: '"dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return" (Genesis 3:19, and Ecclesiastes 12:7)' gives one wording to two verses. The words are Genesis 3:19's alone ("for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return"); Ecclesiastes 12:7 reads "Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it." The rest stands: Isaiah 32:1–2, Gardner (chapter IV, "The Dry Season", and the sentence quoted above) and Southam's note keyed to line 25 are all confirmed, so the sources line is right as written.
Corrected: Isaiah 32:2 promises a king in whose reign a man shall be "as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land"; Helen Gardner (1949) set the verse beside this rock, and Southam's note gives it. What the shadow shows here is "fear in a handful of dust", and the dust is scripture's and the burial service's: "dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return" (Genesis 3:19; in Ecclesiastes 12:7 "the dust return to the earth as it was"), "dust to dust". The shelter the prophet promised is where death is shown. — Sources unchanged.

### passion line 307
Kind: fact
Problem: "Augustine's <i>Confessions</i>, Book III, in Pusey's translation, which Eliot's note quotes." Eliot's note does not quote Pusey; it rewords him. Pusey (Gutenberg #3296, the opening of Book III): "To Carthage I came, where there sang all around me in my ears a cauldron of unholy loves." Eliot's note: "to Carthage then I came, where a cauldron of unholy loves sang all about mine ears." The card "carthage" at this line already says "the line, like Eliot's note, rewords it", so the stop contradicts the card.
Corrected: Augustine's <i>Confessions</i>, Book III, in Pusey's translation, which Eliot's note adapts. At line 309, Book X, from the chapter on the temptation of the eyes: Augustine, still entangling his steps "with these outward beauties", says "but Thou pluckest me out, O Lord, Thou pluckest me out". The convert's memory of his lust is set beside the Buddha's sermon on fire, a pairing Eliot's note on line 309 calls "eastern and western asceticism" and "not an accident". "O Lord Thou pluckest me out" are the poem's only words addressed to God.

### passion line 322
Kind: judgement
Problem: "Cleanth Brooks (1939) wrote that the gardens' frosty silence 'associates Christ in Gethsemane with the other hanged gods'." Brooks's sentence names two references, not one: "The reference to the 'torchlight red on sweaty faces' and to the 'frosty silence in the gardens' obviously associates Christ in Gethsemane with the other hanged gods." The quoted words are exact; the antecedent is narrowed.
Corrected: The Passion without its names. The torchlight is the arrest by night, when Judas comes to the garden "with lanterns and torches and weapons" (John 18:3); "the frosty silence in the gardens" is Gethsemane, where "being in an agony he prayed more earnestly" (Luke 22:44); then the death, "He who was living is now dead". Cleanth Brooks (1939) wrote that the reference to the torchlight and to the frosty silence "obviously associates Christ in Gethsemane with the other hanged gods". The resurrection is not told, and the "we" who speak "are now dying". — Sources unchanged.
