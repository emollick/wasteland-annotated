# Batch 3, reader, round 2

### unreal-city

Verdict: satisfied

Clear, and every sentence is a fact with its source. I paused once, at "what poetry could make of the modern city, the fusion of ...", but it reads on the second look and I would leave it alone.

### london-bridge-dead

Verdict: not yet

The note is now good, and the three things below are small.

1. "live without hope, in desire, and there is no weeping" — "without hope, in desire" is Dante's phrase, but unmarked it reads as the annotator's own pairing, and "in desire" on its own stopped me. Put: "where the unbaptised and the virtuous pagans live, in Dante's words, "without hope, in desire", and there is no weeping, only sighs that make the eternal air tremble (IV, 25–42)."
2. "Eliot said in 1950 that he had altered Dante's line on purpose, and that he gave the references in his notes so that a reader who recognised them would know he was meant to." — two things in one sentence. "Dante's line": which one? Coming after the sentence on line 64 I took it to be the sighs line, but the note hangs on line 63 and a reader may take it the other way; the scholar should say which line Eliot meant. And "would know he was meant to" leaves the reader to finish the sentence. If it is the sighs line, put: "Eliot said in 1950 that he had altered the line about the sighs on purpose, and that he gave the references in his notes so that a reader who recognised a borrowing would know he was meant to recognise it."
3. "He was one of them, at Lloyds Bank at the top of King William Street, from 1917 to 1925." — the two "at"s make it stumble. Put: "He was one of them. Lloyds Bank, where he worked from 1917 to 1925, was at the top of King William Street."

### woolnoth

One thing only; the rest should stand as it is.

1. "runs up from London Bridge to the Bank" — I read "the Bank" as a bank, and the next sentence is about Lloyds Bank. Put: "runs up from London Bridge to the Bank of England".

### stetson

Verdict: not yet

1. "Mylae was the sea battle of 260 BC in which Rome first beat Carthage at sea, early in the First Punic War. F. O. Matthiessen identified the battle in 1935, and Cleanth Brooks in 1939 read the Punic War as a trade war and so a parallel to the war of 1914–18; on that reading, in Brooks's words, "all the wars are one war"." — "sea battle ... at sea" says it twice; "identified the battle" left me asking "as what?"; "and so a parallel" is missing a word; "on that reading, in Brooks's words" qualifies the same thing twice. Put: "Mylae was the battle of 260 BC, early in the First Punic War, in which Rome beat Carthage at sea for the first time; the identification is F. O. Matthiessen's (1935). Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the Punic War as a trade war, and so as a parallel to the war of 1914–18: in his words, "all the wars are one war"."
2. "The friend met in the City of London a few years after the Armistice was "in the ships" twenty-two centuries before." — the "few years" against "twenty-two centuries" is the count for effect that the brief warns against, and the sentence states the poem's conceit as if it were the note's own fact. Put: "The speaker greets a friend in the City of London, a few years after the Armistice, as someone who was "in the ships" with him at Mylae."
3. "Other suggestions are the slouch hat of the Australian troops at Gallipoli, and Kipling's bank clerk Charlie Mears" — a hat and a man are not the same kind of suggestion, and I had to work out that the hat is a suggestion about the name. Put: "The name has also been linked to the slouch hat of the Australian troops at Gallipoli, and Stetson himself to Kipling's bank clerk Charlie Mears, who remembers rowing in an ancient galley."

### corpse

Verdict: not yet

1. "Frazer's <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, the two volumes Eliot's headnote names (with the title garbled), describes" — "two volumes ... describes" trips the ear, and "garbled" sends me off to find out how. Eliot's version is on the site in the headnote, so show it. Put: "In <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, the two volumes Eliot's headnote names as "Atthis Adonis Osiris", Frazer describes two rites of a god who dies and returns."
2. "Effigies of Osiris made of earth and corn were buried at the Egyptian festival of sowing and taken up when the grain had sprouted, and corn-stuffed figures of the god have been found in tombs; their sprouting, Frazer says, signified his resurrection." — three facts in one sentence, and by the time "their sprouting" arrives I have lost what "their" is. Put: "Effigies of Osiris made of earth and corn were buried at the Egyptian festival of sowing and taken up when the grain had sprouted. Corn-stuffed figures of the god have also been found in tombs. The sprouting of the grain, Frazer says, signified his resurrection."
3. "This is the plainest of them, asked in the voice of a man talking to a neighbour about his bulbs." — "this" is a reference, and a reference is not "asked". Put: "This is the plainest of them. The questions are the ones a man asks a neighbour about his bulbs."

### dog

Verdict: not yet

1. "Act V scene iv, sung, the stage direction says, "in several forms of distraction"." — three commas in a row and a participle hanging off a play title; I had to read it twice. Put: "... Act V scene iv. The stage direction says she does this "in several forms of distraction"."
2. "Eliot alters the couplet in five places." — a fact, so for the scholar, but the reader can check it against the quotation shown above the note, and I count six: Webster's "But keep" is Eliot's "Oh keep". If "Oh" for "But" is not counted, a reader who compares the two texts will wonder why.
3. "the well-meant modern thing that digs up what should be left to lie" — "left to lie" sounds like letting the dead rest, which is the opposite of the point (the god has to stay buried to come back), and the phrase has a knowing tone. Put, for the scholar to check against Brooks: "the modern concern for man that digs up the buried god and so prevents his return".

### hypocrite-lecteur

Verdict: not yet

1. "the prefatory poem of <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i> (1857), which Eliot's note calls its preface. Baudelaire's catalogue of vices ends with the ugliest of them, Ennui, and then turns on the reader as its accomplice." — "prefatory poem ... its preface" says the same thing twice before I see why, and "its accomplice" has no clear "its". Put: "The last line of "Au Lecteur", the poem that opens <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i> (1857) and that Eliot's note calls its preface. Baudelaire's catalogue of vices ends with the ugliest of them, Ennui, and then turns on the reader as an accomplice."
2. "In the 1922 text the line is still Stetson's, inside the quotation marks and opening with "You!"; but Baudelaire's line is addressed to the reader, and a reader who knows it is addressed too." — "Stetson's" reads as if Stetson speaks the line, and "still" made me look for a later text that differs. Put: "In the 1922 text the line is spoken to Stetson: it is the last line of the speech that begins at line 69, inside its quotation marks, and it opens with "You!". But Baudelaire's line is spoken to the reader, and a reader who knows it is addressed as well."
3. "would know he was meant to." — the same unfinished sentence as in the note on line 63, word for word, and a reader who opens both will notice. Put: "would know he was meant to recognise it." If the editor wants the two notes to differ, this one could end instead: "Eliot said in 1950 that his notes were there so that a reader who recognised an allusion would know it was meant."
