### title
Verdict: correct
Checked: Eliot's headnote against work/poem-part1.md (the quoted phrase is exact). Dickens, Our Mutual Friend, Book I ch. 16, Gutenberg #883: "You mightn't think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices." Exact. Weston 1920 (Cambridge UP) from knowledge. The facsimile not reachable; Wikipedia confirms that only Parts I and II of the typescript carry the working title.
Substance:
1. "written over the first two parts": the phrase is typed as a heading on the typescripts of Parts I and II ("HE DO THE POLICE IN DIFFERENT VOICES: Part I. THE BURIAL OF THE DEAD", and likewise Part II). "Written over" suggests a hand-written overwriting. Say "typed at the head of".
2. "The waste land is the country, the king, and the reader who has not yet asked anything." A reading, unattributed, and the third term is not Weston's: her book has the king, the land and the questing knight, not the reader. Cut or mark as the site's own reading.
3. "Our Mutual Friend (1865)": serialised May 1864 to November 1865; "(1864–65)" is the usual form. Add "Book I, chapter 16" so a reader can find it.
4. "A poem that reads the news of the world aloud in as many voices as it can do. Keep that title in your head; it explains more than the Grail does." Reading passed as instruction, and it contradicts the paragraph above, which reports Eliot's own note crediting Weston. If a reading is wanted, give whose: Calvin Bedient's He Do the Police in Different Voices (1986) built a whole book on the working title. Otherwise cut.
5. "a much older fertility cult": Weston's own terms are a "Nature Cult" and the mystery rites of Adonis and Attis; "fertility cult" is a fair gloss. The summary of her thesis is otherwise accurate.
Draft:
Body: Eliot's first note credits the title, "the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism" of the poem to Jessie L. Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (Cambridge, 1920). Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve, under a Christian surface, the rites of a much older fertility cult: a king wounded or impotent, his land laid waste with him, and a healing that depends on a stranger who arrives and asks the right question. Eliot's note recommends the book to "any who think such elucidation of the poem worth the trouble."

The typescript had another title. Parts I and II are headed <i>He Do the Police in Different Voices</i>, a phrase from Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (1864–65), Book I, chapter 16, where Betty Higden praises her foundling Sloppy: "You mightn't think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices." The heading was dropped before publication and is known from the drafts, published in facsimile in 1971.

### epigraph
Verdict: correct
Checked: Petronius, Satyricon 48, Latin Library (thelatinlibrary.com/petronius1.html): wording matches the site's quote; speaker Trimalchio. Ovid, Metamorphoses XIV.130–153, Latin Library (ovid.met14.shtml): "ego pulveris hausti / ostendens cumulum, quot haberet corpora pulvis, / tot mihi natales contingere vana rogavi" (136–38); "excidit, ut peterem iuvenes quoque protinus annos" (139); the shrinking, 147–53. Conrad, Gutenberg #219: "at some vision—he cried out twice, a cry that was no more than a breath: / 'The horror! The horror!'" Pound's letter and Eliot's reply from Paige's Selected Letters of Ezra Pound (1950) and The Letters of T. S. Eliot vol. 1, from knowledge; not reachable online today. The 1930 Liveright printing on archive.org carries the Petronius epigraph on its title page.
Substance:
1. "as many years of life as there were grains in a handful of sand": Ovid's word is dust, "pulvis" (XIV.136–37: a heap of dust she had scooped up, "pulveris hausti... cumulum"). "Sand" is the translators' habit. Since the note goes on to connect this to "a handful of dust" at line 30, the correction strengthens it. The same slip is in red-rock.
2. "She shrank until she could be kept in a bottle": the shrinking is Ovid's (she will dwindle until only her voice is left, XIV.147–53); the jar is Petronius's. The note should say the story is Ovid's, since the epigraph itself tells none of it.
3. "three languages before the first line": with the dedication in Italian on the same page, four. Drop the count.
4. The Conrad quotation. Conrad's published text reads "at some vision—he cried out twice, a cry that was no more than a breath: 'The horror! The horror!'" The site's comma-dash, the dash for the colon and the lower-case second "the horror" reproduce the epigraph as Eliot typed it (facsimile p. 3; I could not reach the facsimile today and give this from memory of it). Either is defensible, but say which is being quoted: "as Eliot typed it" or Conrad's text.
5. "Pound wrote in January 1922": right, but this is the same letter that the dedication note dates "either 24 December 1921 or 24 January 1922" ("Complimenti, you bitch" and "I doubt if Conrad is weighty enough" are in one letter, dated "24 Saturnus, An 1"). The site should date it once. Paige printed it as 24 December 1921; Valerie Eliot's facsimile introduction and The Letters of T. S. Eliot vol. 1 give 24 January 1922, which fits the sequence (Eliot left Paris for London about 16 January and the letter answers a revised typescript sent from London).
6. "the vulgar millionaire host": Trimalchio is a freedman, a freed slave grown rich. The word tells a reader more than "vulgar".
7. Missing and worth one sentence: the Petronius epigraph first appeared in the Boni & Liveright edition (December 1922); the Criterion printing of October 1922 had neither epigraph nor notes (King's College Cambridge centenary page, kcctreasures.com, says the Boni & Liveright edition "included the epigraph and the 'Notes' to the poem for the first time"). This is what "and then replaced it anyway" leaves out: when.
8. "Her wish is the poem's first word on the subject of living on after the point of living has gone": a reading. Mark it or drop it.
Draft:
Body: The speaker is Trimalchio, a freed slave grown enormously rich, host of the dinner party that fills the longest surviving stretch of Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i> (first century AD). In chapter 48 he is boasting to his guests of what he has read and seen. The Sibyl's story is in Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV, 130–153: Apollo offered her whatever she wished; she picked up a heap of dust and asked for as many years as it had grains, and forgot to ask for youth. Ovid has her foretell that she will shrink until only her voice is left. The jar is Petronius's detail. Ovid's word is dust, "pulvis," and line 30 of the poem ends in "a handful of dust."

This epigraph was not the first. The typescript of Part I carried Kurtz's death from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, from "Did he live his life again in every detail of desire, temptation, and surrender" to "'The horror! The horror!'" Pound wrote on 24 January 1922: "I doubt if Conrad is weighty enough to stand the citation." Eliot answered that it was "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," and gave it up. The Petronius epigraph first appeared in the Boni & Liveright edition of December 1922; the <i>Criterion</i> printing of October 1922 had none.

### dedication
Verdict: correct
Checked: Purgatorio XXVI.115–17 and 148 at it.wikisource: the quoted tercet is exact; line 148 reads "Poi s'ascose nel foco che li affina" (Eliot's "gli"). Guinizzelli speaks; Arnaut is named at 142. Eliot's 1922 note "427. V. Purgatorio, XXVI, 148" (archive.org scan); every-line count 428. The inscription in Pound's copy: Wikipedia and the King's College Cambridge page confirm the autograph inscription in Pound's copy of the first edition and the first printing in Poems 1909–1925; the date January 1923 is Ricks and McCue's (their textual headnote prints the inscription with "Jan. 1923"), which I could not reach today. Pound's Spirit of Romance chapter title confirmed by web search (Wikipedia, Arnaut Daniel).
Substance:
1. "Pound worked over the typescript in Paris in January 1922 and by post afterwards": Pound saw the typescript twice in Paris. Eliot showed him what existed (Parts I–III) in November 1921 on the way to Lausanne, and the two went through the whole poem in January 1922 on Eliot's way back; the letters followed (Valerie Eliot's introduction to the facsimile; Rainey, Revisiting The Waste Land). Add November.
2. "roughly halved it": Eliot's own figure. In "Ezra Pound" (Poetry, September 1946) he said the manuscript "left his hands, reduced to about half its size." Give the source; the phrase is within the rights rule.
3. The letter date: see epigraph 5. "read as either 24 December 1921 or 24 January 1922" is honest, but the site's two notes should agree. Recommend: "24 January 1922 ('24 Saturnus, An 1' in Pound's private calendar; D. D. Paige's 1950 edition of the letters printed it as 24 December 1921)".
4. "It credits the cutting, not the writing": a reading, though Eliot's 1946 account supports it. Fine if kept plain.
5. Missing, one sentence: "Il Miglior Fabbro" was already Pound's phrase, the title of his chapter on Arnaut Daniel in <i>The Spirit of Romance</i> (1910). Ricks and McCue note it; a reader of a dedication to Pound would want it.
6. "not in the 1922 printings": right; nor in the Hogarth Press edition of September 1923. Worth adding so no one goes looking there.
7. "Complimenti, you bitch. I am wracked by the seven jealousies": exact.
Draft:
Body (second paragraph only): The dedication is not in the printings of 1922, nor in the Hogarth Press edition of 1923. Eliot wrote it by hand in Pound's copy of the Boni & Liveright edition (Ricks and McCue date the inscription January 1923) and first printed it in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>. Pound had seen the typescript in Paris in November 1921, on Eliot's way to Lausanne, and gone through the whole poem with him there in January 1922 on his way back; further comments came by post. Eliot said in 1946 that the poem left Pound's hands "reduced to about half its size." Pound's letter of 24 January 1922, dated "24 Saturnus, An 1" in a calendar of his own devising, contains the verdict: "Complimenti, you bitch. I am wracked by the seven jealousies." (D. D. Paige's 1950 edition of Pound's letters printed it as 24 December 1921.) "Il miglior fabbro" was already Pound's phrase: it is the title of his chapter on Arnaut Daniel in <i>The Spirit of Romance</i> (1910).

### april
Verdict: correct
Checked: Chaucer, General Prologue 1–4 and 12 (Skeat, Gutenberg #22120; the site's spelling is Riverside's, also fine). Lines 1–18 of the poem are one verse paragraph in Gutenberg #1321 and in the 1922 setting on archive.org; the first break is after line 18. Hermit-thrush line: 357 in the every-line count. Brooke, "The Old Vicarage, Grantchester," Gutenberg #262: headed "(Cafe des Westens, Berlin, May 1912)", opens "Just now the lilac is in bloom, / All before my little room". Brooke died 23 April 1915. Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" (Sequel to Drum-Taps, 1865) from knowledge: the poem names spring, not April.
Substance:
1. "so that the verbs never quite arrive at their objects": false. Each participle's object is the first thing on the next line: lilacs, memory and desire, dull roots, earth, a little life. The effect is a delay of one line, not a missing object. The count of five participles (breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding) is right.
2. "the leisured Europeans of the next paragraph": same paragraph. Lines 1–18 run without a break in 1922; the Munich lines begin at 8 with no break. Say "of lines 8–18".
3. "Behind Chaucer stand two other Aprils": neither poem names April. Whitman's says "ever-returning spring" (Lincoln was shot on 14 April 1865, which is the April); Brooke's is dated Berlin, May 1912, and the only April is his death. Rephrase as two other lilacs, which is what the poems share.
4. The Brooke connection is a suggestion and is not in Eliot's notes; I cannot attribute it to a standard commentary. Mark it as a suggestion. The Whitman connection is generally accepted (Ricks and McCue), and Eliot's own note to line 357 names the hermit-thrush.
5. "or you": second person; a reading. The plain fact is that "us" is not identified.
6. Title "Chaucer's April, turned inside out" and "Eliot's first line answers it": the Chaucer echo is accepted by every commentary; fine.
Draft:
Body (from "Read the first seven lines" to the end): Five of the first seven lines end on a participle: breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding. Each object is held over to the next line (lilacs, memory and desire, dull roots, earth, a little life), so each verb waits a line for what it governs.

Two other lilac poems are usually mentioned. Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" (1865) mourns Lincoln, shot in April, with lilacs, "ever-returning spring" and a hermit thrush; Eliot's own note to line 357 names the hermit-thrush. Rupert Brooke's "The Old Vicarage, Grantchester," dated "Café des Westens, Berlin, May 1912," opens "Just now the lilac is in bloom"; Brooke died in April 1915. The Whitman connection is generally accepted; the Brooke one is a suggestion. "Us" in line 5 is never identified. The voice that goes on to Munich at line 8 belongs to the same verse paragraph, which runs to line 18.

### starnbergersee
Verdict: correct
Checked: Ludwig II (Wikipedia): found dead in Lake Starnberg, then called the Würmsee, on 13 June 1886; official verdict suicide by drowning; the autopsy found no water in his lungs; he attended the Munich premiere of Tristan (1865) as Wagner's patron; he built Neuschwanstein. The typescript's "Königsee" for line 8: reported from the facsimile by several sources (web search); the facsimile itself not reachable. Eliot's Munich dates: Jean Verdenal wrote to him in Bavaria in mid-July 1911 (Letters vol. 1), so he was there by July; Crawford's Young Eliot has July and August. Hofgarten arcades on two sides (north and west): knowledge.
Substance:
1. "Eliot spent August 1911 in Munich": July and August. He was in Munich by mid-July (Verdenal's letter), and left for northern Italy in late August before returning to Paris and America.
2. "was found drowned": found dead in the shallows of the lake. The official verdict was suicide by drowning; the autopsy found no water in the lungs, and the cause has been disputed ever since. "Found dead in the lake" is what is known.
3. "the king who paid for Tristan und Isolde": Ludwig paid for its first performance (Munich, 10 June 1865); Wagner had composed it in 1857–59, before Ludwig came to the throne. Say "paid for the first performance of".
4. The Ludwig connection is a reading, a common one (Southam's Guide notes the drowning at the lake). Mark it. It has a second support the note does not give: Countess Marie Larisch, whose memoir lies behind lines 8–18 (see marie), was Elisabeth of Austria's niece, and the Wittelsbach house at Possenhofen on this lake was Elisabeth's family home; Larisch's book mentions the lake (G. L. K. Morris, 1954).
5. Missing: in the typescript the lake was the Königssee, in the far south-east of Bavaria; Eliot altered it to the Starnbergersee (facsimile). A reader of a "place" note would want this.
6. "which is the 'colonnade' the speakers shelter in": a plausible identification, not a fact. "Probably".
7. "The poem never says so. It only stops for coffee.": the second sentence is a flourish; cut. The title "Munich, August 1911" states the biographical reading as fact and gets the month wrong.
Draft:
Title: The lake and the Hofgarten
Body: The Starnberger See is the long lake about twenty-five kilometres south-west of Munich; the Hofgarten is the court garden beside the Residenz in the city centre, with arcades along two sides, which are probably the "colonnade." In the typescript the lake was the Königssee, in the far south-east of Bavaria; Eliot changed it (facsimile, 1971). Eliot was in Munich in July and August 1911, at the end of his year in Paris, and finished "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" there. King Ludwig II of Bavaria, Wagner's patron and the builder of Neuschwanstein, was found dead in the lake on 13 June 1886; the verdict was suicide by drowning, though the autopsy found no water in his lungs. Commentators since Southam have set that death beside the poem's "death by water"; the poem itself says nothing of it. The lake also belongs to Countess Marie Larisch, whose memoir lies behind lines 8–18: her aunt the Empress Elisabeth spent her childhood summers at Possenhofen on its shore.

### marie
Verdict: correct

Draft:
Body: Lines 8–18 draw on Countess Marie Larisch (1858–1940), a niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria. G. L. K. Morris pointed out in 1954 ("Marie, Marie, Hold On Tight," <i>Partisan Review</i>) that her memoir <i>My Past</i> (1913) has the archduke who was her cousin, the Starnbergersee, the mountains where one feels free, the reading late at night and the winters in the south. ### litauen

4. "The poem's first foreign sentence": the epigraph is Latin and Greek and the dedication Italian. "The first line of the poem proper not in English."

Title: The German line

### son-of-man
Verdict: correct
Checked: KJV Ezekiel 2:1, 2:3, 3:1–3, 6:4, 6:6 and Ecclesiastes 12:5 at en.wikisource (all quotations exact). Eliot's notes 20 and 23 in the archive.org scan. "Dry bones can harm no one" is line 391 in the every-line count. Ash-Wednesday 1930, The Rock 1934: knowledge.
Substance:
1. "Eliot's second note, on line 23": his first note is the headnote on Weston, so this is his third; say "Eliot's note on line 23".
2. "'A heap of broken images' is Ezekiel too": the usual identification (Southam; Ricks and McCue), not a certainty. "Is usually traced to Ezekiel 6:4–6, where the images of Israel are to be broken."
3. "Cricket, grasshopper: the same small dry noise": the link in Ecclesiastes is weight, not sound. "The grasshopper shall be a burden" is part of the chapter's picture of old age, in which even so light a thing becomes heavy. Eliot's cricket gives "no relief". The note's sentence asserts a connection by noise that the source does not make.
4. "It is the one Eliot would use again in 'Ash-Wednesday' and the choruses of The Rock, the desert voice": a reading. Mark it ("readers have compared").
5. "with the same book in mind": widely shared, but a reading; "usually referred to Ezekiel 37".
6. "before sending him to a rebellious people": right (2:3, "a rebellious nation"). "The next chapter has the prophet eat a scroll": right (3:1–3; the roll is shown to him at 2:9–10).
Draft:
Body: Eliot's note sends the reader to Ezekiel 2:1, where God addresses the prophet as "son of man" and sends him to "a rebellious nation" (2:3). The address runs through the book; in chapter 37 the prophet is set down in a valley of dry bones and told to prophesy until they stand up. "Dry bones can harm no one" (line 391) is usually referred to the same chapter. "A heap of broken images" is usually traced to Ezekiel 6:4–6, where the idols and images of Israel are to be broken. The voice here is unlike anything in the first eighteen lines; readers have compared it to "Ash-Wednesday" (1930) and the choruses of <i>The Rock</i> (1934).

Eliot's note on line 23 points to Ecclesiastes 12:5, in the chapter on old age: "the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail: because man goeth to his long home, and the mourners go about the streets." The grasshopper there is a weight, not a sound: in age even so light a thing is a burden. Eliot's cricket gives "no relief."

### red-rock
Verdict: correct
Checked: Isaiah 32:2, en.wikisource raw text: "as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land." Tennyson, Maud, Part II section V, stanza 1, Gutenberg #56913: "Dead, long dead, / Long dead! / And my heart is a handful of dust," exact. Ovid XIV.136–37 (see epigraph). "The Death of Saint Narcissus": first line "Come under the shadow of this gray rock" (Wikipedia); Valerie Eliot dates it early 1915; set in type for Poetry for October 1915 and withdrawn; first printed in Poems Written in Early Youth (privately, 1950; Faber, 1967); in the facsimile (secondary sources; the poem itself I checked from memory). The paraphrase of its opening in the note is accurate and keeps within the rights rule.
Substance:
1. "the line that follows, 'I will show you fear in a handful of dust,' carries Isaiah's 'shadow of a great rock in a weary land' (32:2)": misattached. Isaiah 32:2 is cited (Southam and others) for the shelter under the rock, lines 25–26; it has nothing to do with the handful of dust. Move it.
2. "the Sibyl's handful of sand": dust in Ovid ("pulvis," XIV.136–37); see epigraph 1. The correction makes the note's point for it.
3. "The two shadows, morning and evening, are a sundial reading of a whole life": a reading, unattributed. Mark it as one.
4. Tennyson: a comparison editors make (Ricks and McCue), not a source Eliot acknowledged. "Editors cite".
5. "a poem Eliot wrote in 1915": Valerie Eliot's dating is early 1915; "about 1915" is safer. Add that it was set in type for Poetry and withdrawn, and first printed in 1950, so a reader knows why it is little known.
6. "lifted, almost word for word": right. Line 27, "And I will show you something different from either," is carried over unchanged; line 26 changes only "gray" to "red".
Draft:
Body: "Come in under the shadow of this red rock" comes almost unchanged from "The Death of Saint Narcissus," a poem Eliot wrote about 1915, had set in type for <i>Poetry</i> (Chicago) that autumn and then withdrew; it was first printed in 1950 and is reproduced in the 1971 facsimile. There the rock of shelter is grey, and the speaker promises to show something different from two shadows, one sprawling over the sand at daybreak, the other leaping behind a fire against a red rock. Line 27 is carried over word for word. For the shelter of lines 25–26 commentators cite Isaiah 32:2, "the shadow of a great rock in a weary land." The two shadows of lines 28–29, behind you in the morning and rising to meet you at evening, have been read as a life from birth to death; that is a reading. For "a handful of dust" editors cite Tennyson's <i>Maud</i> (Part II, section V): "Dead, long dead, / Long dead! / And my heart is a handful of dust." The Sibyl of the epigraph, in Ovid, asked for as many years as there were grains in a heap of dust.

### summary
1. april: two statements are false. The participles' objects do arrive, at the head of the next line; and the Munich lines are in the same verse paragraph as lines 1–7 (the first break is after line 18). Neither Whitman's nor Brooke's poem names April; the Brooke connection is an unattributed suggestion.
2. red-rock: Isaiah 32:2 is attached to line 30; it belongs to the shelter of lines 25–26.
3. epigraph and red-rock: Ovid's Sibyl holds a heap of dust, "pulvis," not sand (Met. XIV.136–37); the shrinking is Ovid's, the jar Petronius's. The Conrad quotation follows Eliot's typescript, not Conrad's punctuation; say which.
4. epigraph and dedication date the same Pound letter two ways. Use 24 January 1922 ("24 Saturnus, An 1"), noting Paige's 24 December 1921.
5. dedication: Pound saw the typescript in Paris in November 1921 as well as January 1922; "halved" is Eliot's own 1946 figure; "Il Miglior Fabbro" was Pound's chapter title in The Spirit of Romance (1910); the dedication is also absent from the Hogarth 1923 edition.
6. marie: "not from any book" reverses the evidence. Morris (1954) found most of lines 8–18 in My Past; Valerie Eliot says only that the sledding came from conversation and that when and where Eliot met Larisch is unknown.
7. starnbergersee: Munich was July and August 1911; Ludwig was found dead, and drowning is disputed; he paid for Tristan's first performance, not the opera; the typescript read Königssee.
8. boston-cut: attribute the cancellation to Valerie Eliot's facsimile and the absence of Pound's marks; the summary of the passage omits Myrtle's, and "burlesque" and "run from a policeman" are unverified.
9. son-of-man: "second note" should be "note on line 23"; the Ecclesiastes grasshopper is a burden, not a noise.
10. litauen: mostly reading; the one hard fact, that "Russin" makes the speaker a woman, is missing.
11. Line numbers in this batch (30, 357, 391, 428) are right in the every-line count. Aside for the README: the 1922 marginal numbers slip between 341 and 351 (the Liveright setting prints "350" beside "A spring", every-line 351), but Eliot's own notes 357 (hermit-thrush) and 360 match the every-line count, so "one behind from 346" does not describe the note numbers between 346 and 360. Worth a check against the 1922 book before Part V is edited.
