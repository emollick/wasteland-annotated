### hyacinth-girl
Verdict: not yet
1. "Frazer gives him a short chapter at the end of the Attis book of <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, the volumes Eliot's headnote names, as a figure who "has been interpreted as ..."" — the "as a figure who" clause hangs off "gives him a short chapter" across an appositive, and I read it twice to find what it belonged to. Put: "In <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, the volumes Eliot's headnote names, Frazer gives him a short chapter at the end of the Attis book. He writes there that Hyacinth "has been interpreted as the vegetation which blooms in spring and withers"."
2. "Sparta kept a three-day festival for him at Amyclae, the Hyacinthia, a day of mourning followed by rejoicing." — "three-day" and "a day" trip over each other; I stopped to count. Put: "Sparta kept a three-day festival for him at Amyclae, the Hyacinthia, which began with a day of mourning and ended in rejoicing."
3. ""The heart of light" has been read against the title of Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>" — "read against" is critic's shorthand; a reader can take it as "read in opposition to". Put: ""The heart of light" has been read as an inversion of the title of Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, the book the typescript's epigraph came from (see the note on the epigraph)."
For the scholar: "<i>Burnt Norton</i> (1935)" — a bracketed date reads as the date of publication; 1935 is the year of writing, and the poem was first printed in <i>Collected Poems 1909–1935</i> in April 1936.

### oed-und-leer
Verdict: not yet
1. "Kurwenal has sent to Cornwall for Isolde" — a name with nobody attached to it; a reader who does not know the opera cannot use it. Put: "his servant Kurwenal has sent to Cornwall for Isolde" (the scholar to choose the word: servant, squire or retainer).
2. "This quotation and the one at line 31 stand on either side of the hyacinth garden, that one from the opening of the opera, before Tristan and Isolde have fallen in love, this one from its last act, when they are apart and Tristan is dying." — one sentence with two tails hung on commas; I lost where "that one" ended and "this one" began. Put: "This quotation and the one at line 31 stand on either side of the hyacinth garden. That one comes from the opening of the opera, before Tristan and Isolde have fallen in love; this one from its last act, when they are apart and Tristan is dying."

### sosostris
Verdict: not yet
1. "the Sailor and the Merchant "appear later", so do the crowds, and "Death by Water is executed in Part IV"" — a comma splice in the middle ("appear later, so do the crowds") that I had to reread. Put: "Eliot's note ties her cards to what follows: the Sailor, the Merchant and the crowds "appear later", and "Death by Water is executed in Part IV"."
2. "is usually read as a fortune-teller's caution" — caution about what? The reader has to wait for the next sentence to find out. Put: "is usually read as a fortune-teller's caution about the law."
3. "Mr Scogan" — the tarot note has "Mr. Eugenides" and the poem prints "Mrs. Equitone"; one form on the site. Put: "Mr. Scogan".

### tarot
Verdict: not yet
1. "Eliot's note associates him with "the Hanged God of Frazer", the chapter of that name in <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, on Attis, Marsyas and Odin, gods hanged on trees, and with the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V." — the second "with" arrives after a nested appositive and I had to go back to find what it paired with. Put: "Eliot's note associates him with "the Hanged God of Frazer" and with the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. "The Hanged God" is a chapter in <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, on Attis, Marsyas and Odin, gods hanged on trees."
2. "Weston, in her chapter on the Grail symbols, says the Tarot was first used "to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land"." — standing last in the Hanged Man entry, after "Death by Water is executed in Part IV", it has nothing to attach to; the reader cannot tell why it is there. It belongs with Eliot's own remark about the pack, before the list. Put the opening paragraph as: "Eliot's note says he was "not familiar with the exact constitution of the Tarot pack" and had "obviously departed" from it "to suit my own convenience". Weston, in her chapter on the Grail symbols, says the Tarot was first used "to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land". The cards, in the order Madame Sosostris names them, are these." and strike the Weston sentence from the Hanged Man entry. ("Madame Sosostris" for "she" so that the pronoun does not fall on Weston.)
3. "The bracketed line 48 is from a song telling Ferdinand that his father has drowned; see the note there." — "the bracketed line 48" made me pause to work out what was bracketed. Put: "Line 48, the line in brackets, is from a song telling Ferdinand that his father has drowned; see the note there."

### pearls
Verdict: not yet
1. "The poem quotes the line again at 125" — the same note says "at line 257"; the bare number reads as a page or a note. Put: "The poem quotes the line again at line 125".
2. "Compare Part IV, where Phlebas drowns." — Part IV opens with Phlebas "a fortnight dead"; nobody drowns in it, and a reader who turns to it will look for a drowning that is not there. Put: "Compare Part IV, on the drowned Phlebas." (For the scholar: the tarot note's "Part IV, "Death by Water", is the drowning of Phlebas the Phoenician" is loose in the same way; "is about the drowned Phlebas" would be exact.)

### ring
Verdict: not yet
1. "Eliot's note on line 46 lists "the 'crowds of people'" with the Sailor and the Merchant among the figures that "appear later"." — quotation marks inside quotation marks, and "figures" for a crowd; I read it twice. Put: "Eliot's note on line 46 says that the "crowds of people", like the Sailor and the Merchant, "appear later"."
2. "The ring has been read against Dante's neutrals in <i>Inferno</i> III, who run for ever behind a whirling banner" — "neutrals" is a label a reader may not know, and "read against" is the same shorthand as in the hyacinth note. Put: "The ring has been compared with Dante's neutrals, the souls in <i>Inferno</i> III who were neither good nor evil and run for ever behind a whirling banner; Eliot's note on line 63 cites the same canto." (The gloss on "neutrals" is mine, from the canto; the scholar to confirm the phrase.)
