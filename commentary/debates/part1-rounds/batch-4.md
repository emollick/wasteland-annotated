### source chaucer  (lines 1)
TITLE: The Canterbury Tales, General Prologue
AUTHOR: Geoffrey Chaucer   DATE: c. 1387   LANGUAGE: Middle English   KIND: poem
WHAT: The kind April that Eliot's first line contradicts.
PASSAGE: Whan that Aprill with his shoures soote / The droghte of March hath perced to the roote, / And bathed every veyne in swich licour / Of which vertu engendred is the flour; / Whan Zephirus eek with his sweete breeth / Inspired hath in every holt and heeth / The tendre croppes, and the yonge sonne / Hath in the Ram his half cours yronne, / And smale foweles maken melodye, / That slepen al the nyght with open ye / (So priketh hem Nature in hir corages), / Thanne longen folk to goon on pilgrimages.
TRANSLATION: When April with its sweet showers has pierced the drought of March to the root and bathed every vein in the liquid whose power engenders the flower; when Zephyrus with his sweet breath has quickened the tender shoots in every wood and heath, and the young sun has run half his course in the Ram, and small birds make melody that sleep all night with open eye (so Nature pricks them in their hearts), then people long to go on pilgrimages.
NOTE:
English poetry's first great spring, in which rain ends drought, sap rises and the whole country wants to travel. Eliot's poem also ends in a kind of pilgrimage, to the Chapel Perilous, but its April breeds lilacs out of dead land and its people would rather have stayed under the snow.

### source ecclesiastes  (lines 23)
TITLE: Ecclesiastes 12
AUTHOR: The Bible (King James Version)   DATE: 3rd century BC; translation 1611   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: scripture
WHAT: "the cricket no relief": the chapter on old age and death.
PASSAGE: Also when they shall be afraid of that which is high, and fears shall be in the way, and the almond tree shall flourish, and the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail: because man goeth to his long home, and the mourners go about the streets: or ever the silver cord be loosed, or the golden bowl be broken, or the pitcher be broken at the fountain, or the wheel broken at the cistern. Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it.
NOTE:
Eliot's note sends the reader to verse 5, where "the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail." The rest of the chapter, with its broken pitcher, its dry cistern and its dust, could be a plan for Part V.

### source tristan  (lines 31, 42)
TITLE: Tristan und Isolde
AUTHOR: Richard Wagner   DATE: 1865 (first performed Munich)   LANGUAGE: German   KIND: opera
WHAT: The sailor's song (lines 31–34) and the shepherd's "Öd und leer das Meer" (line 42).
PASSAGE: Westwärts / schweift der Blick; / ostwärts / streicht das Schiff. / Frisch weht der Wind / der Heimat zu: / mein irisch Kind, / wo weilest du? / Sind’s deiner Seufzer Wehen, / die mir die Segel blähen? / Wehe, wehe, du Wind! / Weh, ach wehe, mein Kind! / Irische Maid, / du wilde, minnige Maid! /  / . . . /  / HIRT: Öd und leer das Meer!
TRANSLATION: Westward the gaze wanders; eastward the ship runs. Fresh blows the wind toward home: my Irish child, where do you linger? Is it the breath of your sighs that fills my sails? Woe, woe, you wind! Woe, ah woe, my child! Irish maid, you wild, lovely maid! . . . SHEPHERD: Desolate and empty the sea!
NOTE:
Eliot's note cites the opera by act and verse as if it were a book, and it was: the libretto was sold and read. The young sailor's song is the first thing sung in Act I, from the masthead, unseen, above the deck where Isolde lies; the shepherd's four words in Act III answer Kurwenal's question whether any ship is in sight while Tristan lies dying. Between the two quotations the poem puts its hyacinth garden, which is to say that the garden happens between a ship setting out and a ship not arriving. Eliot heard *Tristan* in Boston in 1909, as a Harvard senior, and wrote a poem called "Opera" about it; Stravinsky, who met him in 1956, came away thinking that *Tristan* "must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life." Ludwig II, who paid for the first performance, drowned in the Starnberger See of line 8.

### source baudelaire-sept-vieillards  (lines 60)
TITLE: Les Sept Vieillards (from Les Fleurs du mal)
AUTHOR: Charles Baudelaire   DATE: 1859; in the 1861 edition of Les Fleurs du mal   LANGUAGE: French   KIND: poem
WHAT: "Unreal City."
PASSAGE: Fourmillante cité, cité pleine de rêves, / Où le spectre en plein jour raccroche le passant! / Les mystères partout coulent comme des sèves / Dans les canaux étroits du colosse puissant.
TRANSLATION: Swarming city, city full of dreams, where the ghost in broad daylight accosts the passer-by! Mysteries everywhere flow like sap in the narrow channels of the mighty giant.
NOTE:
The poet, walking through a yellow fog in a Paris morning, meets a hideous old man, then another exactly like him, and another, seven in all, and flees home half mad. Eliot's note quotes only the first two lines, and the whole "Unreal City" passage is built on their method: a real street, and a ghost on it at nine in the morning.

### source baudelaire-au-lecteur  (lines 76)
TITLE: Au Lecteur (the preface poem of Les Fleurs du mal)
AUTHOR: Charles Baudelaire   DATE: 1857   LANGUAGE: French   KIND: poem
WHAT: "hypocrite lecteur!—mon semblable,—mon frère!"
PASSAGE: C’est l’Ennui! — l’œil chargé d’un pleur involontaire, / Il rêve d’échafauds en fumant son houka. / Tu le connais, lecteur, ce monstre délicat, / — Hypocrite lecteur, — mon semblable, — mon frère!
TRANSLATION: It is Boredom! Its eye charged with an involuntary tear, it dreams of scaffolds while smoking its hookah. You know it, reader, this delicate monster, hypocrite reader, my double, my brother!
NOTE:
Baudelaire opens his book by listing the sins of mankind, saving the worst for last, boredom, and then turning on whoever is reading. Eliot closes his first section the same way. He said in 1930 that Baudelaire was "the greatest exemplar in modern poetry in any language," and in 1950 that he had learned from him "the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric."

### source death-of-st-narcissus  (lines 25)
TITLE: The Death of Saint Narcissus
AUTHOR: T. S. Eliot   DATE: 1915 (withdrawn from publication)   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: poem
WHAT: "Come in under the shadow of this red rock."
PASSAGE: Come under the shadow of this gray rock— / Come in under the shadow of this gray rock,
NOTE:
Set in type for *Poetry* (Chicago) in 1915 and withdrawn at proof stage, the poem about a saint who wanted to be a tree, a fish and a girl, and died pierced with arrows, survived among the manuscripts Eliot sent to John Quinn in 1922 along with the *Waste Land* drafts. Its opening goes on, in the cadence the later poem keeps, to promise the listener something other than his own shadow on the sand or against the fire: the saint's bloody cloth and limbs. The gray rock's shadow, made red, became the poem's first prophecy. The poem is still in copyright, so only its first two lines are given here.

### source huxley-crome-yellow  (lines 43)
TITLE: Crome Yellow, chapter 27
AUTHOR: Aldous Huxley   DATE: 1921   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: novel
WHAT: "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana": the name behind Madame Sosostris.
PASSAGE: Mr. Scogan had been accommodated in a little canvas hut. Dressed in a black skirt and a red bodice, with a yellow-and-red bandana handkerchief tied round his black wig, he looked—sharp-nosed, brown, and wrinkled—like the Bohemian Hag of Frith’s Derby Day. A placard pinned to the curtain of the doorway announced the presence within the tent of “Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana.”

### source dante-inf3  (lines 62, 63, 56)
TITLE: Inferno, canto III
AUTHOR: Dante Alighieri   DATE: c. 1308   LANGUAGE: Italian   KIND: poem
WHAT: The crowd on London Bridge: "I had not thought death had undone so many."
PASSAGE: E io, che riguardai, vidi una ’nsegna / che girando correva tanto ratta, / che d’ogne posa mi parea indegna; / e dietro le venìa sì lunga tratta / di gente, ch’i’ non averei creduto / che morte tanta n’avesse disfatta.
TRANSLATION: And I, who looked, saw a banner which, whirling, ran so fast that it seemed to scorn all pause; and behind it came so long a train of people that I would never have believed death had undone so many.
NOTE:
Inside the gate ("Abandon all hope") but before the river Acheron, Dante finds the souls who were neither good nor bad, "who lived without infamy and without praise," rejected by Heaven and refused by Hell, running forever after a banner and stung by wasps. Eliot's commuters, going round in a ring, are these. He said in 1950 that Dante's poetry was still, "after forty years," "the most persistent and deepest influence" on his own verse.

### source dante-inf4  (lines 64)
TITLE: Inferno, canto IV
AUTHOR: Dante Alighieri   DATE: c. 1308   LANGUAGE: Italian   KIND: poem
WHAT: "Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled."
PASSAGE: Quivi, secondo che per ascoltare, / non avea pianto mai che di sospiri / che l’aura etterna facevan tremare.
TRANSLATION: Here, as far as I could tell by listening, there was no weeping but only sighs, which made the eternal air tremble.
NOTE:
Limbo, the first circle, home of the virtuous who died unbaptised: Homer, Virgil, Aristotle, the "great-hearted" souls who are not tormented but live in desire without hope. One line of the poem, one canto of Dante: the office crowd sigh like the noble pagans, not like the damned.

### source ezekiel  (lines 20, 22, 391)
TITLE: Ezekiel 2 and 37
AUTHOR: The Bible (King James Version)   DATE: 6th century BC; translation 1611   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: scripture
WHAT: "Son of man"; "a heap of broken images"; the valley of dry bones.
PASSAGE: And he said unto me, Son of man, stand upon thy feet, and I will speak unto thee. (2:1) /  / The hand of the LORD was upon me, and carried me out in the spirit of the LORD, and set me down in the midst of the valley which was full of bones, and caused me to pass by them round about: and, behold, there were very many in the open valley; and, lo, they were very dry. And he said unto me, Son of man, can these bones live? And I answered, O Lord GOD, thou knowest. (37:1–3)
NOTE:
Ezekiel, prophet of the Babylonian exile, is addressed by God ninety-odd times as "son of man," which is to say mortal. Eliot's note points to the moment of his commission; the poem's dry stone, broken images and dry bones come from the same book (chapters 6 and 37). In the valley the bones stand up. In the poem they do not.

TITLE: Isaiah 32 and 38

### source tennyson  (lines 30, 429)
TITLE: Maud, and The Princess
AUTHOR: Alfred Tennyson   DATE: 1855; 1847   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: poem
WHAT: "a handful of dust"; "O swallow swallow."
PASSAGE: Dead, long dead, / Long dead! / And my heart is a handful of dust, / And the wheels go over my head, / And my bones are shaken with pain, / For into a shallow grave they are thrust, / Only a yard beneath the street . . . (Maud, II.v) /  / O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South, / Fly to her, and fall upon her gilded eaves, / And tell her, tell her, what I tell to thee. (The Princess)
NOTE:
The mad speaker of *Maud*, who imagines himself buried alive under a city street with the traffic going over him, is a Victorian ancestor of the man in rats' alley; the swallow song in *The Princess* gives Eliot the English half of a Latin line. Eliot's respect for Tennyson's ear ("the finest ear of any English poet since Milton," he wrote in 1936) shows in what he took.

### source whitman-lilacs  (lines 2, 357)
TITLE: When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd
AUTHOR: Walt Whitman   DATE: 1865   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: poem
WHAT: Lilacs, spring, death, and a hermit thrush.
PASSAGE: When lilacs last in the dooryard bloom’d, / And the great star early droop’d in the western sky in the night, / I mourn’d, and yet shall mourn with ever-returning spring. . . . /  / In the swamp in secluded recesses, / A shy and hidden bird is warbling a song. / Solitary the thrush, / The hermit withdrawn to himself, avoiding the settlements, / Sings by himself a song. / Song of the bleeding throat, / Death’s outlet song of life . . .
NOTE:
Whitman's elegy for Lincoln, shot in April 1865, binds three things together: the lilac, the evening star, and the hermit thrush singing in the swamp. Eliot's poem opens on lilacs out of dead land and, three hundred and fifty lines later, longs for the hermit-thrush's "water-dripping song." He rarely admitted to reading Whitman. The lilacs and the thrush say otherwise.

### source webster-white-devil  (lines 74, 408)
TITLE: The White Devil
AUTHOR: John Webster   DATE: 1612   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: play
WHAT: Cornelia's dirge ("keep the wolf far thence") and Flamineo on the spider and the winding-sheet.
PASSAGE: Call for the robin-redbreast and the wren, / Since o’er shady groves they hover, / And with leaves and flowers do cover / The friendless bodies of unburied men. / Call unto his funeral dole / The ant, the field-mouse, and the mole, / To rear him hillocks that shall keep him warm, / And (when gay tombs are robb’d) sustain no harm; / But keep the wolf far thence, that’s foe to men, / For with his nails he’ll dig them up again. (V.iv) /  / O men, / That lie upon your death-beds, and are haunted / With howling wives! ne’er trust them; they’ll re-marry / Ere the worm pierce your winding-sheet, ere the spider / Make a thin curtain for your epitaphs. (V.vi)
NOTE:
Webster's tragedy of Vittoria Corombona, the "white devil," and her murderous lover Brachiano. Cornelia sings the dirge over her son Marcello, killed by his brother Flamineo; Flamineo gives the speech about widows as he rises from a feigned death, having just learned that his sister would have let him die. Eliot's essays on the Elizabethan dramatists and his poem "Whispers of Immortality" ("Webster was much possessed by death / And saw the skull beneath the skin") explain why these two speeches were in his head.

### source shakespeare-tempest  (lines 48, 125, 191, 257)
TITLE: The Tempest, Act I scene ii
AUTHOR: William Shakespeare   DATE: c. 1611   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: play
WHAT: Ariel's song, "Those are pearls that were his eyes"; Ferdinand on the bank; "This music crept by me upon the waters."
PASSAGE: FERDINAND: Where should this music be? i’ th’ air or th’ earth? / It sounds no more: and sure, it waits upon / Some god o’ th’ island. Sitting on a bank, / Weeping again the king my father’s wreck, / This music crept by me upon the waters, / Allaying both their fury and my passion / With its sweet air . . . /  / ARIEL (sings): Full fathom five thy father lies; / Of his bones are coral made; / Those are pearls that were his eyes: / Nothing of him that doth fade / But doth suffer a sea-change / Into something rich and strange. / Sea-nymphs hourly ring his knell: / Ding-dong. / Hark! now I hear them,—Ding-dong, bell.
NOTE:
Ferdinand, washed ashore after the shipwreck, believes his father Alonso drowned; Ariel's song tells him so, beautifully and falsely. Alonso is alive, and by the end of the play everyone has suffered a sea-change for the better. Eliot quotes the song twice and Ferdinand's speech twice, and never allows the change. The drowned father with pearls for eyes becomes the drowned Phoenician and Phlebas; Ferdinand weeping on the bank becomes the Fisher King by the gasworks.

### source frazer  (lines 46, 71, 312)
TITLE: The Golden Bough (Adonis, Attis, Osiris)
AUTHOR: Sir James George Frazer   DATE: 1906–15 (third edition); one-volume abridgement 1922   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: anthropology
WHAT: The dying and reviving god; the buried corpse that sprouts; the Hanged God; the effigy thrown into the sea.
NOTE:
Frazer's twelve-volume comparative study of magic and religion, which Eliot's headnote says "has influenced our generation profoundly," argued that behind the myths of Adonis, Attis, Osiris and, by implication, Christ lay a single agricultural drama: the god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped along by ritual (a sacrificed king, an effigy buried or drowned). The volumes Eliot names describe the "gardens of Adonis," pots of quick-sprouting seed thrown into the sea, and the effigies of Osiris "made of earth and corn," buried to sprout. Stetson's planted corpse, Phlebas in the current, the Hanged Man and the whole question of whether April can bring anything back are Frazer's. The book's title and its opening page come from Turner's painting *The Golden Bough* (1834), which Frazer took for a view of Nemi; it shows the Sibyl at Lake Avernus.

### source weston  (lines 0, 46, 386, 424)
TITLE: From Ritual to Romance
AUTHOR: Jessie L. Weston   DATE: 1920   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: anthropology
WHAT: The title, the plan, the Fisher King, the Chapel Perilous, the Syrian merchants.
NOTE:
Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance who came to believe, under the influence of Frazer, that the Grail stories preserved the remains of an ancient fertility cult: a wounded king, a land gone waste, a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics, and a ritual of initiation whose secrets travelled Europe with Syrian merchants. Almost no medievalist now accepts the argument. It does not matter. Eliot's note says the book suggested "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism," and thirty-four years later he said he regretted "having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail." Read chapters II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel) and you have the poem's skeleton; read the rest and you have a very good bad book.

### source petronius  (lines 0)
TITLE: Satyricon, chapter 48
AUTHOR: Petronius   DATE: 1st century AD   LANGUAGE: Latin, with Greek   KIND: prose
WHAT: The epigraph: the Sibyl who wanted to die.
PASSAGE: Nam Sibyllam quidem Cumis ego ipse oculis meis vidi in ampulla pendere, et cum illi pueri dicerent: Σίβυλλα τί θέλεις; respondebat illa: ἀποθανεῖν θέλω.
TRANSLATION: For I myself, with my own eyes, saw the Sibyl at Cumae hanging in a jar, and when the boys said to her, "Sibyl, what do you want?" she answered, "I want to die."
NOTE:
The speaker is Trimalchio, the freed slave turned millionaire whose dinner party is the surviving centre of the *Satyricon*, showing off between courses. The Sibyl of Cumae, in Ovid's telling, asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand and forgot to ask for youth, and shrank with age until only her voice was left. A prophetess kept as a curiosity; a poem introduced by a fat man's boast.

### source dante-purg26  (lines 0, 428)
TITLE: Purgatorio, canto XXVI
AUTHOR: Dante Alighieri   DATE: c. 1315   LANGUAGE: Italian and Provençal   KIND: poem
WHAT: "il miglior fabbro" (the dedication) and "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina" (line 428).
PASSAGE: «Tan m’abellis vostre cortes deman, / qu’ieu no me puesc ni voill a vos cobrire. / Ieu sui Arnaut, que plor e vau cantan; / consiros vei la passada folor, / e vei jausen lo joi qu’esper, denan. / Ara vos prec, per aquella valor / que vos guida al som de l’escalina, / sovenha vos a temps de ma dolor!». / Poi s’ascose nel foco che li affina.
TRANSLATION: "So much does your courteous question please me that I cannot and will not hide myself from you. I am Arnaut, who weep and go singing; in thought I see my past folly, and I see with joy, ahead, the joy I hope for. Now I pray you, by that power which guides you to the top of the stair, be mindful in time of my pain." Then he hid himself in the fire that refines them.
NOTE:
On the seventh terrace of Purgatory, where the lustful walk in flame, Dante meets Guido Guinizelli, who points out Arnaut Daniel as "miglior fabbro del parlar materno," the better craftsman of the mother tongue (line 117). Arnaut answers in his own Provençal, the only passage in the *Comedy* in a vernacular other than Italian, and steps back into the fire. Eliot took the dedication to Pound from line 117, the title of his 1920 collection *Ara Vos Prec* from line 145, and line 428 of the poem from line 148. Modern texts read "li affina"; Eliot's "gli" follows the editions he owned.

### source dickens-omf  (lines 0)
TITLE: Our Mutual Friend, Book I chapter 16
AUTHOR: Charles Dickens   DATE: 1865   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: novel
WHAT: The typescript's title: "He Do the Police in Different Voices."
PASSAGE: “I do love a newspaper. You mightn’t think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices.”
NOTE:
Betty Higden's praise of the foundling Sloppy, who reads her the police-court reports doing all the parts. Eliot wrote the sentence across the top of the typescript of Parts I and II. It is the plainest statement of what the poem does that anyone has made, including its author, and Dickens's novel, which opens with a body fished out of the Thames, is the poem's river in prose.

### source conrad-hod  (lines 0)
TITLE: Heart of Darkness
AUTHOR: Joseph Conrad   DATE: 1899   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: novella
WHAT: The epigraph the poem almost had.
PASSAGE: Did he live his life again in every detail of desire, temptation, and surrender during that supreme moment of complete knowledge? He cried in a whisper at some image, at some vision,—he cried out twice, a cry that was no more than a breath— / “The horror! The horror!”
NOTE:
Kurtz's last words, as Marlow reports them, stood at the head of the typescript. Pound doubted whether Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation"; Eliot defended it as "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," then replaced it with Petronius. The "heart of light" at line 41 still answers to it. The book's opening, a boat on the Thames at Gravesend at dusk with London behind it, is worth rereading with Part III in mind.

### source eliot-frontiers  (lines 0)
TITLE: The Frontiers of Criticism (Eliot on his own notes)
AUTHOR: T. S. Eliot   DATE: 1956   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: essay
WHAT: What Eliot later said about the notes.
PASSAGE: . . . the remarkable exposition of bogus scholarship that is still on view to-day. . . . I regret having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail.
NOTE:
A lecture given at the University of Minnesota in 1956 to an audience of nearly fourteen thousand, in which Eliot, thirty-four years on, described the notes as padding that had got out of hand: he had meant only to list his references, to spike the guns of critics who had called him a plagiarist, and then, when the poem proved too short to make a book on its own, expanded them to fill pages. He also admitted they could not now be removed: readers had "bought" the notes with the poem. Take the notes as part of the poem and as a joke at its expense; both are true.

## C. Drafts records (the Drafts page and the Drafts lens). The first is in Part I’s own file; the other two are in the shared site file (proposals only).

---
### draft boston  (part 1, line 1)
TITLE: Before "April": a night in Boston
BODY:
The typescript of Part I opens with fifty-four lines in a loose, chatty verse about a night out with the boys in Boston: drinks at "Tom's place," a burlesque show, a girl, a run from a policeman, a sunrise walk home. The whole passage is struck through in pencil, apparently by Eliot before Pound saw it. What remains begins already tired, and "April is the cruellest month" is the second first line the poem had. Pound's comments on the rest of Part I are light; he queried a few words and let the section stand.

---
### draft title-draft  (part 0, line 0)
TITLE: The title that was crossed out
BODY:
Across the top of the typescript of the first two parts Eliot typed "He Do the Police in Different Voices," from Dickens's *Our Mutual Friend*. "The Waste Land" replaced it, with Weston's Grail country and Malory's "waste land" of the Dolorous Stroke behind it. The Dickens title survives in the notes only as a habit of mind: the poem reads the world's news aloud in every voice it can do.

### draft epigraph-draft  (part 0, line 0)
TITLE: The epigraph that was replaced

Kurtz's "The horror! the horror!" from Conrad's *Heart of Darkness* stood at the head of the typescript. Pound doubted Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation"; Eliot called it "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," and then substituted the Sibyl from Petronius. The change turns a dying man's verdict on his life into an immortal's wish to be done with hers.
