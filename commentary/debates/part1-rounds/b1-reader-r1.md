# Batch 1, reader, round 1

Drafts below use `<i>…</i>` for titles and straight quotes, as the batch text does; the editor matches the site. Anything in square brackets inside a draft is a flag to the editor or scholar, not proposed text. I have kept every date, name and claim as the notes give them; where I softened a claim into "usually read as", I say so, and the scholar can put it back.

### title
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: in places
Problems:
1. "The waste land is the country, the king, and the reader who has not yet asked anything." A triad that ends by making me the subject of the poem. It is a reading given as a fact, and it does not help me with the words "The Waste Land"; it wants to be quoted.
2. "written over the first two parts" I first read this as crossed out, written on top of. If the title stood at the head of the typescripts of Parts I and II, say that.
3. "A poem that reads the news of the world aloud in as many voices as it can do." A fragment, and the note admiring its own idea. It describes the poem instead of the title.
4. "Keep that title in your head; it explains more than the Grail does." An instruction to me, then a verdict on the whole poem as a closing line. Whose view is that? If it is a common one, name it; if it is the note's, it does not belong.
5. Small: "under a Christian skin" is a figure where "beneath their Christian story" would do. I would let it pass on its own; with the rest it is part of the manner.
Keep: "Eliot's first note says it plainly ... *From Ritual to Romance* (1920)." "Weston's book argues that ... asks the right question." The three sentences on *He Do the Police in Different Voices* and Dickens, with "written over" fixed.
Draft:
TITLE: Where the title comes from
BODY:
Eliot's first note credits the title, "the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism" to Jessie L. Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920). Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve, beneath their Christian story, a much older fertility rite: a land lies waste because its king is wounded or impotent, and is healed only when a stranger arrives and asks the right question.

The typescript carried a different working title at the head of the first two parts [scholar: confirm this is what "written over" meant]: <i>He Do the Police in Different Voices</i>. The words are Betty Higden's praise of the boy Sloppy in Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (1865): "You mightn't think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices." The working title was dropped before publication.

### epigraph
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: in places
Problems:
1. "Her wish is the poem's first word on the subject of living on after the point of living has gone." "The poem's first word on the subject" treats the poem as a speaker with opinions, and "living on after the point of living has gone" is a phrase built to be admired. It also states the poem's theme as fact. What I wanted was plainer: she has long life without youth and wants only to die.
2. "Latin, then Greek, then the poem's English: three languages before the first line, and a handful of sand before the "handful of dust" at line 30." This is the sentence-shape Ethan quoted: a colon, a count, a tidy echo. The sand-and-dust link is worth a plain sentence of its own. The count of languages tells me nothing; if the Greek matters, tell me why it is there (is it Petronius's own Greek?).
3. "hanging in a jar" in the translation, "bottle" in the title and body. Same object, two words; I stopped to wonder whether they were the same. Pick one (the scholar's call which).
4. "the vulgar millionaire host ... boasting between courses" Colour I can live with, but with "vulgar millionaire" it is a small performance. "The rich freedman who hosts the dinner, and the story is one of his boasts" gives me the same.
5. What I wanted and did not get: where the grains-of-sand story comes from (Ovid, I think; scholar to supply), and which chapter of the *Satyricon* this is.
6. "and then replaced it anyway" is how a person would say it. Keep.
Keep: "The Sibyl of Cumae had asked Apollo ... forgot to ask for youth. She shrank until she could be kept in a bottle." The whole second paragraph.
Draft:
TITLE: The Sibyl in the bottle [or "jar", to match the translation]
BODY:
The speaker is Trimalchio, the rich freedman who hosts the dinner in Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i> (first century AD), and the story is one of his boasts. The Sibyl of Cumae had asked Apollo for as many years of life as there were grains in a handful of sand, and forgot to ask for youth [scholar: add the source of the story]. She shrank until she could be kept in a bottle. Her handful of sand is echoed by the "handful of dust" at line 30. [If true, one sentence: The boys' question and her answer are in Greek in Petronius's Latin text.]

This was not the first epigraph. [second paragraph unchanged]

### dedication
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: in places
Problems:
1. "*Purgatorio* XXVI, line 117." A bare fragment as an opener. Fine in a footnote, but the rest of the note is in sentences, so make it one.
2. "The same canto supplies the poem's last page" "Supplies" and "last page" are a little pleased with themselves. "The same canto is quoted near the end of the poem" says it.
3. "It credits the cutting, not the writing:" The antithesis with a colon, and it decides for me what the dedication means. Say what Pound did and I will draw the line myself.
4. "in a letter dated in his own invented calendar and read as either 24 December 1921 or 24 January 1922" I stumbled twice: "invented calendar" with no explanation, and "read as" by whom? Give the calendar one plain clause and say that editors differ on the date.
5. "and roughly halved it" Plain and useful. Keep.
Keep: "The poet Guido Guinizelli ... the better smith of his native speech." "The dedication is not in the 1922 printings. Eliot wrote it by hand ... *Poems 1909–1925*." "Pound worked over the typescript in Paris in January 1922 and by post afterwards." The "Complimenti" quotation.
Draft:
TITLE: The better craftsman
BODY:
The line is <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, 117. The poet Guido Guinizelli, doing penance among the lustful in the refining fire, points Dante toward Arnaut Daniel, the Provençal troubadour, and calls him the better smith of his native speech. The same canto is quoted near the end of the poem: "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina" at line 428 is Arnaut stepping back into the flames.

The dedication is not in the 1922 printings. Eliot wrote it by hand in Pound's copy of the Boni & Liveright edition in January 1923 and first printed it in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>. Pound worked over the typescript in Paris in January 1922 and by post afterwards, and cut it by about half. His letter of congratulation is dated in a calendar of his own devising, and editors have read the date as 24 December 1921 or as 24 January 1922: "Complimenti, you bitch. I am wracked by the seven jealousies."

### april
Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: yes
Problems:
1. Title, "Chaucer's April, turned inside out". A figure for its own sake. "Chaucer's April, reversed" or plain "Chaucer's April" is enough.
2. "The most famous opening in English poetry says that April is kind: rain ends the drought, sap rises, people want to go on pilgrimages." The colon and the triad; and "most famous" is the writer's flourish, not a fact I can check.
3. "in something that would rather stay numb" Tells me what the speaker feels. It is a reading and is not marked as one.
4. "Winter was kinder: "forgetful snow," "a little life with dried tubers."" A colon and two quoted scraps standing in for a sentence.
5. "Read the first seven lines aloud and notice how five of them end on a present participle left hanging ... so that the verbs never quite arrive at their objects." An instruction to me, a count for effect, and, when I did read them, not true: each participle gets its object on the next line ("breeding / Lilacs", "stirring / Dull roots", "feeding / A little life"). The real point is that the object is held over the line break. That I can see for myself once it is stated.
6. "Growth that will not finish." A fragment tied as a bow.
7. "Behind Chaucer stand two other Aprils." Staging. Poems do not stand behind one another; commentators cite them.
8. "And Rupert Brooke, dead in April 1915, whose "The Old Vicarage, Grantchester" opens with the lilac in bloom." A fragment, and I am not told what the link is beyond the word lilac. If the opening line is the link, quote it.
9. "The line's "us" is a group that never gets named. It might be the leisured Europeans of the next paragraph, or the dead, or you." "Or you" is the bow, aimed at me; the list of three is for rhythm. And the "us" is in line 5, not line 1.
10. "that thrush turns up at line 357 of this poem" Useful. Keep.
Keep: The Whitman sentence. The dry-roots quotation and the "memory and desire" point, once they are one plain sentence.
Draft:
TITLE: Chaucer's April, reversed
BODY:
Chaucer's opening welcomes April. Its showers end the drought of March, the sap rises, and people set out on pilgrimages. Eliot's first line is usually read as an answer to it [the note says "answers it"; scholar may restore]. The rain is still there ("stirring / Dull roots with spring rain"), but it is unwelcome, since it stirs "memory and desire" where the winter snow was "forgetful". Five of the first seven lines end on a participle (breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding) whose object is held over to the next line.

Commentators also cite two other poems with lilacs. Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" (1865) mourns Lincoln with lilacs, spring and a hermit thrush; the thrush returns at line 357 of this poem. Rupert Brooke's "The Old Vicarage, Grantchester" opens "Just now the lilac is in bloom" [scholar: confirm the quotation]; Brooke died in April 1915. The "us" of line 5 is never identified.

### boston-cut
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: in places
Problems:
1. "in the voice of a young man out with his friends: a bar, a burlesque show, a drunken run from a policeman, a sunrise." The colon and the list of four. This is content I want, so write it as a sentence with verbs in it.
2. "What survived is the second beginning, a poem that starts already exhausted." The common brief's own example. The note stops describing the draft and starts describing the poem. Cut.
3. "apparently by Eliot himself before Pound saw it" A good hedge, but whose judgement is "apparently"? If it is Valerie Eliot's, say so; the source sentence at the end is doing that work already and could sit next to it.
4. "fifty-four lines" A count, but one I wanted: it tells me how much was cut. Keep.
Keep: "In the typescript, "April is the cruellest month" was not the first line." "The whole passage was struck through with a single pencil line, apparently by Eliot himself before Pound saw it." "The manuscript is at the New York Public Library's Berg Collection and is reproduced in the 1971 facsimile edited by Valerie Eliot."
Draft:
TITLE: What came before "April"
BODY:
In the typescript, "April is the cruellest month" was not the first line. Fifty-four lines came before it, describing a night on the town in Boston in the voice of a young man out with his friends, who drink in a bar, see a burlesque show, run drunk from a policeman and watch the sun rise. The whole passage was struck through with a single pencil line, apparently by Eliot himself before Pound saw it [scholar: whose judgement]. The manuscript is in the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library and is reproduced in the 1971 facsimile edited by Valerie Eliot.

### starnbergersee
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: in places
Problems:
1. "a death by water for the king who paid for *Tristan und Isolde*" The connection made for me with a wink. Lay out the facts (Ludwig drowned in this lake; he was Wagner's patron; the poem quotes *Tristan* twenty lines later) and I will see it. "Paid for" also sounds loose to me: what did he pay for, the opera or its staging? Scholar's call.
2. "The poem never says so. It only stops for coffee." The bow, and the poem behaving like a person. Cut both sentences.
3. Minor: the poem prints "Starnbergersee" as one word and the note has "Starnberger See". One clause saying so would spare a reader the flicker of doubt about whether they are the same place.
4. "which is the "colonnade" the speakers shelter in" An identification I am glad of. "Probably" would be honest unless it is certain.
Keep: The first two sentences. "The lake is also where King Ludwig II of Bavaria, Wagner's patron and the builder of Neuschwanstein, was found drowned in June 1886."
Draft:
TITLE: Munich, August 1911
BODY:
The Starnberger See (one word in the poem) is the long lake south of Munich; the Hofgarten is the court garden in the city centre, with an arcaded walk on two sides, which is probably the "colonnade" the speakers shelter in. Eliot spent August 1911 in Munich, at the end of his year in Paris, and finished "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" there. King Ludwig II of Bavaria, Wagner's patron and the builder of Neuschwanstein, was found drowned in the lake in June 1886. The poem does not mention him. It quotes Wagner's <i>Tristan und Isolde</i> at lines 31 to 34 and 42, and Madame Sosostris says "Fear death by water" at line 55.

### son-of-man
Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: in places
Problems:
1. Title, "The prophet's address". I read "address" as where he lives before I read it as a speech, and then it is God's speech, not the prophet's. ""Son of man" in Ezekiel" or "God's address to the prophet".
2. "The next chapter has the prophet eat a scroll" Nothing to do with this line. A detail for its own sake.
3. "says line 391, with the same book in mind" A line does not have a mind. Say that line 391 is usually referred to the same chapter.
4. "The voice that speaks here is not the café voice of Marie." Staging; the note steps back to narrate the poem. The point, that the speaker changes, takes four words.
5. "It is the one Eliot would use again in "Ash-Wednesday" and the choruses of *The Rock*, the desert voice, and it has been given a stony landscape, a "red rock," and a question with no answer: what grows here?" "The desert voice" is a label the note invents; then a triad, a colon and a rhetorical question for a finish. The one fact in it, that Eliot used a similar manner later, is worth one plain sentence.
6. "sends you to" Second person; the first paragraph's "points to" is right.
7. "Cricket, grasshopper: the same small dry noise." The fragment from the common brief's list. The point is that Eliot changed the insect, which is a sentence.
Keep: "Eliot's note points to Ezekiel 2:1, where God addresses the prophet as "son of man" before sending him to a rebellious people." ""A heap of broken images" is Ezekiel too: the Lord promises to break the idols of Israel (6:4–6)." The Ecclesiastes quotation.
Draft:
TITLE: "Son of man" in Ezekiel
BODY:
Eliot's note points to Ezekiel 2:1, where God addresses the prophet as "son of man" before sending him to a rebellious people. Chapter 37 of the same book has the prophet prophesy over a valley of dry bones until they stand up as an army; line 391, "Dry bones can harm no one", is usually referred to it. "A heap of broken images" also recalls Ezekiel, where the Lord promises to break the idols of Israel (6:4–6). The speaker changes here, from Marie to a prophetic voice that Eliot used again in "Ash-Wednesday" and the choruses of <i>The Rock</i>.

Eliot's second note, on line 23, points to Ecclesiastes 12:5, the chapter on old age, where "the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail: because man goeth to his long home, and the mourners go about the streets." Eliot has a cricket where Ecclesiastes has a grasshopper.

### red-rock
Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: in places
Problems:
1. The first sentence is eighty words long. I lost it at "be shown something different from two shadows", and by "against a red rock" I had to go back to find which rock was which. Break it up.
2. "gray rock" and then "grey rock", two lines apart.
3. "The saint's grey rock has turned red." A dramatic little sentence, and by the note's own account the earlier poem already has a red rock (the one behind the fire), so "turned red" is not quite what happened. Say which rock the new poem kept.
4. "The two shadows, morning and evening, are a sundial reading of a whole life" A metaphor about a metaphor, asserted as fact. If it is a reading, mark it as one.
5. "the line that follows, "I will show you fear in a handful of dust," carries Isaiah's "shadow of a great rock in a weary land" (32:2), the Sibyl's handful of sand, and Tennyson's *Maud*" Isaiah's shadow of a rock belongs with lines 25 and 26 (shadow, rock), not with the handful of dust. Bundling all three into one triad on line 30 muddles which line echoes what, and I could not have told you afterwards.
6. Title is fine.
Keep: ""Come in under the shadow of this red rock" is lifted, almost word for word, from a poem Eliot wrote in 1915 and withdrew from publication, "The Death of Saint Narcissus"". The *Maud* quotation.
Draft:
TITLE: Eliot borrowing from himself
BODY:
"Come in under the shadow of this red rock" is lifted, almost word for word, from "The Death of Saint Narcissus", a poem Eliot wrote in 1915 and withdrew from publication. There the invitation is to come in under the shadow of a grey rock and be shown something different from two shadows: one sprawling over the sand at daybreak, the other leaping behind a fire against a red rock. In <i>The Waste Land</i> the rock of the invitation is the red one. "Shadow under this red rock" also recalls Isaiah 32:2, "the shadow of a great rock in a weary land". One reading takes the morning and evening shadows as the two ends of a life. "A handful of dust" at line 30 recalls the Sibyl's handful of sand in the epigraph, and Tennyson's <i>Maud</i>: "Dead, long dead, / Long dead! / And my heart is a handful of dust."

### summary
What I threw out most often was the last sentence. Six of the ten notes end on a line written to be quoted ("It only stops for coffee", "or you", "Keep that title in your head", "it is defensive", "the same small dry noise", "someone who is not sure they mean it"), and two more put one at the end of a paragraph ("Growth that will not finish", "a poem that starts already exhausted"); in every case the note was better without it: the facts before it were doing the work, and the bow told me what to feel about them. Next most common was the colon followed by a list of three or four, used as the default way to end a thought (epigraph, april, marie, son-of-man, boston-cut, dedication), and the formula "the poem's first X" (epigraph, litauen, marie), which makes the poem a person with opinions. Two clarity problems were also real ones: the april note's claim that the participles "never quite arrive at their objects" is contradicted by the lines themselves, and the red-rock note pins Isaiah's shadow of a rock on the wrong line. The good news is that the notes are well stocked with facts and sources; in most of them the first two-thirds can stand, and the fix is to cut the finish and let the fact be the last thing said.
