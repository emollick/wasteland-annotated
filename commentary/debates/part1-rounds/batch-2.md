Each item below is one piece of commentary exactly as it stands in the site's source files. Review every one. Keep the ids exactly.

### frisch-weht  (kind: echo; line 31–34; hangs on the words “Frisch weht der Wind”; library source: tristan)
TITLE: The sailor's song from the masthead
QUOTE (source passage shown above the note): Frisch weht der Wind / der Heimat zu: / mein irisch Kind, / wo weilest du?
TRANSLATION (shown under the quote): Fresh blows the wind toward home: my Irish child, where do you linger?
BODY:
*Tristan und Isolde* opens with this song, sung by a young sailor high in the rigging, unseen, as Tristan's ship carries Isolde from Ireland to Cornwall to marry a king she does not love. Isolde hears it as mockery. The wind blows toward the sailor's home and away from hers. Eliot's note gives the reference and nothing else; the four short lines sit in the poem like a snatch of song heard through a wall, and their subject, love that has not happened yet and will end in death, opens the hyacinth garden that follows. The reply comes at line 42.

### hyacinth-girl  (kind: gloss; line 36–41; hangs on the words “hyacinth girl”)
TITLE: The hyacinth garden
BODY:
Hyacinthus was the boy Apollo loved and killed by accident with a discus; the flower grew from his blood, and in Ovid its petals carry the letters of a cry of grief (*Metamorphoses* X). In the Greek world the hyacinth had its own festival, the Hyacinthia at Sparta, and Hyacinth is, in Frazer's reading, another dying and reviving god. All of that is available, and none of it explains the passage, which is one of the few places in the poem where a speaker admits to having felt something. "I could not / Speak, and my eyes failed, I was neither / Living nor dead, and I knew nothing, / Looking into the heart of light, the silence." The heart of light is Conrad's heart of darkness turned inside out, and it is also the mystical blank that Eliot's later poems circle.

Two lives have been read into the garden. Lyndall Gordon's biographies attach it to Emily Hale, the Bostonian Eliot loved before he sailed for Europe in 1914; his letters to her, unsealed at Princeton in 2020, show how long that attachment lasted. Others hear Jean Verdenal, the friend of Eliot's Paris year, killed at the Dardanelles in 1915, to whom *Prufrock and Other Observations* is dedicated. In 1934 Eliot remembered "a friend coming across the Luxembourg Gardens in the late afternoon, waving a branch of lilac, a friend who was later (so far as I could find out) to be mixed with the mud of Gallipoli." Lilacs, line 2. The poem does not choose.

### oed-und-leer  (kind: echo; line 42; hangs on the words “Oed’ und leer das Meer.”; library source: tristan)
TITLE: Empty and desolate the sea
QUOTE (source passage shown above the note): Öd und leer das Meer!
TRANSLATION (shown under the quote): Desolate and empty the sea!
BODY:
Act III of *Tristan*. Tristan lies dying in Brittany, waiting for Isolde's ship. A shepherd keeps watch and is to play a merry tune the moment a sail appears. Asked whether he sees anything, he answers with these four words. Between line 31 (the ship setting out, love ahead) and line 42 (the ship not coming, love behind), the hyacinth garden happens. Eliot's note, "Id. III, verse 24," is as brief as the shepherd.

### sosostris  (kind: gloss; line 43–46; hangs on the words “Madame Sosostris”)
TITLE: The clairvoyante with a cold
BODY:
The name is borrowed and bent. In Aldous Huxley's novel *Crome Yellow*, published in November 1921 while Eliot was drafting, the cynic Mr. Scogan dresses up at a village fair as "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana" to tell fortunes. Sesostris was a legendary Egyptian pharaoh. Eliot feminises the fake and gives her a head cold. "Clairvoyante," with the French feminine ending, is the trade term; "the wisest woman in Europe" is the trade puff. She is the poem's most fully drawn comic character, and she is also its first prophet, since everything she deals turns out to be true.

"One must be so careful these days": fortune-telling for money was an offence under the Vagrancy Act of 1824, and prosecutions of palmists and mediums were routine in the London of the 1920s. She is not being philosophical. She is afraid of the police.

### tarot  (kind: gloss; line 46–55; hangs on the words “a wicked pack of cards”; image: tarot-hanged-man)
TITLE: The cards, one by one
BODY:
Eliot's own note confesses that he did not know the Tarot well and "departed from it to suit my own convenience." What she deals:

**The drowned Phoenician Sailor.** Not a Tarot card. He is Phlebas, who gets Part IV to himself, and the merchant of Part III, and Ferdinand in *The Tempest*, whose father is the one lying "full fathom five" with pearls for eyes.

**Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks.** "Beautiful lady" in Italian, also the poisonous nightshade, also the eye-drops women used to dilate their pupils. Leonardo's *Virgin of the Rocks* stands behind the title; the society woman of "A Game of Chess" stands in front of it. "The lady of situations" sounds like a phrase from the gossip columns.

**The man with three staves.** The Three of Wands, a real card: a figure on a headland with his back to us, watching ships. Eliot says he associated it, "quite arbitrarily," with the Fisher King.

**The Wheel.** The Wheel of Fortune, card X. "O you who turn the wheel," line 320.

**The one-eyed merchant.** Not a card; Mr. Eugenides of Smyrna, seen in profile at line 209, with his blank card of something carried on his back.

**The Hanged Man.** Card XII: a young man hanging serenely by one foot from a living tree, a halo round his head. Eliot links him to Frazer's hanged god, the sacrificed king whose death brings the rain, and to the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. Madame Sosostris cannot find him. No sacrifice, no resurrection, is in this reading. "Fear death by water" is the reading's one instruction, and it is the one thing the Grail legend says you should not fear: water is what the waste land needs.

The cards shown here are Pamela Colman Smith's designs for the pack published in London by William Rider & Son in 1909, the Tarot that an English buyer of the 1910s would have found in a shop.

### pearls  (kind: echo; line 48; hangs on the words “Those are pearls that were his eyes”; library source: shakespeare-tempest)
TITLE: Ariel's song
QUOTE (source passage shown above the note): Full fathom five thy father lies; / Of his bones are coral made; / Those are pearls that were his eyes: / Nothing of him that doth fade / But doth suffer a sea-change / Into something rich and strange.
BODY:
*The Tempest*, Act I scene ii. Ariel sings this to Ferdinand, who believes his father the king drowned in the wreck. The father is not dead; the song is a lie that turns out to be a description of the play, in which everyone is transformed by water. The poem quotes it twice (here and at line 125), works its "sea-change" into Phlebas in Part IV, and never lets the transformation happen. Eliot's note on line 218 says the Phoenician Sailor "is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples."

### ring  (kind: gloss; line 56; hangs on the words “walking round in a ring”)
TITLE: Crowds in a ring
BODY:
The clairvoyante's last card is not a card but a vision, and it comes true four lines later on London Bridge. Dante's neutrals in *Inferno* III run forever after a whirling banner; Frazer's villagers dance in rings around the maypole; the commuters go round and round between the station and the office. Eliot's note on the Tarot says "the 'crowds of people'" appear later. They do, at line 62, and again as the "hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" at line 369.
