# Batch 3, reader, round 1: glosses on lines 60–76

### unreal-city

Verdict: trim

Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "That is the recipe for the next sixteen lines: a real bridge, a real street, a real church clock, and the dead." — the colon-and-list aphorism from the brief; "recipe" is a metaphor for the poem, "sixteen" is a count that exists to be quoted, and "and the dead" is a drumroll. The point underneath (real London, Dante's dead) is worth one plain sentence.
2. "Eliot said in 1950 that Baudelaire had taught him..." — said where? I want the talk named. Not mine to rule on, but the site's own rule for Eliot's later texts is a phrase or two, and this is a full sentence; the scholar should say whether it stays at this length.
3. "stripped to one word" — a small flourish that made me stop and work out which word. Say the word.
4. The first sentence is a long fragment ("The opening of ... in which the poet ... meets ...") of the kind editors do use, but it reads more easily as two sentences.

Keep: "The fog is coal smoke. Eliot went into the City through it every working morning from 1917, to Lloyds Bank in Lombard Street." Also the title.

Draft:
TITLE: Baudelaire's swarming city
BODY:
The opening of “Les Sept Vieillards” (“The Seven Old Men”), in the <i>Tableaux parisiens</i> section of <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i>. The poet, walking in a yellow fog, meets the same hideous old man seven times over. In a talk in 1950 [scholar to name it] Eliot said that Baudelaire had taught him “the poetical possibilities . . . of the more sordid aspects of the modern metropolis, of the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric.” The lines that follow do this. London Bridge, King William Street and St Mary Woolnoth are real places, and the crowd on them is taken from Dante’s dead.

The fog is coal smoke. Eliot walked through it into the City every working morning from 1917, to Lloyds Bank in Lombard Street. “Unreal City” returns at line 207, at a winter noon, and at line 377 as the single word “Unreal.”

### london-bridge-dead

Verdict: rewrite

Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "So the commuters ... are damned twice over, once as people who never chose anything, once as people who were merely born too early." — this is the brief's own example of the portentous pairing, and it is a reading given as a fact. "Born too early" stopped me: it means born before Christ, which fits Dante's Limbo and not a clerk on London Bridge, so the clever parallel does not survive a second look.
2. "“Each man fixed his eyes before his feet” is what a crowd on a bridge in a fog actually looks like." — the writer vouching for the poem; "actually" stands in for an argument that is not there, and the sentence is a bow to end on.
3. "between half past eight and nine" — a precise time with no source. The poem gives nine, which is enough.
4. "Eliot worked among them." — true and to the point, but staged as a beat of its own; it can go inside the sentence about the crowd.

Keep: "<i>Inferno</i> III, lines 55–57. Just inside the gate, before the river, Dante sees the neutrals, the souls who lived “without infamy and without praise,” and is astonished at how many there are. Line 64, “Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled,” is from the next canto, the Limbo of the virtuous unbaptised, where “there was no weeping but only sighs, which made the eternal air tremble” (IV, 25–27)." Also the title.

Draft:
TITLE: The crowd at the gate of Hell
BODY:
<i>Inferno</i> III, lines 55–57. Just inside the gate, before the river, Dante sees the neutrals, the souls who lived “without infamy and without praise,” and is astonished at how many there are. Line 64, “Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled,” comes from the next canto, the Limbo of the virtuous unbaptised, where “there was no weeping but only sighs, which made the eternal air tremble” (IV, 25–27). Eliot’s crowd is the morning rush from London Bridge station into the City, which he walked in himself on his way to Lloyds Bank. Its size comes from the first passage and its sighs from the second. One reading is that the commuters are like Dante’s neutrals, people who never chose anything.

### woolnoth

Verdict: trim

Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "the poem's driest joke about its own apparatus" — an opinion in jargon ("apparatus"), and it tells me to find the note funny. Quote the note and let me.
2. "There is a literal death under this church too:" — a tour-guide set-up with a colon. The fact that follows is good and needs no drumroll.
3. "The commuters come up out of it every morning." — the bow. If the point is that the station entrance under the church is still in use, say that plainly, or stop at the booking hall.
4. "heavily rusticated" — I had to guess at it. A few words of plain English would help, unless the image beside the note does the job.
5. Title "The dead sound at nine" — I clicked on the church's name and expected the note to be about the church. Title it by the church.

Keep: the whole first paragraph, from "King William Street" to "his working day". In the second paragraph, the sentence from "when the Bank station" to "booking hall".

Draft:
TITLE: St Mary Woolnoth
BODY:
King William Street runs from London Bridge up to the Bank, and at its top, on the corner of Lombard Street, stands Nicholas Hawksmoor’s St Mary Woolnoth (1716–27), a squat church with two little turrets and heavily rusticated stone, cut in deep bands. Lloyds Bank, where Eliot worked in the Colonial and Foreign Department from 1917 to 1925, was across the road, and the church’s clock struck the start of his working day. Eliot’s note on line 68 says only: “A phenomenon which I have often noticed.”

When the Bank station of the City and South London Railway was built beneath the church in 1897–1900, the crypt was cleared, the bodies were reburied at Ilford, and the crypt became the station’s booking hall.

### stetson

Verdict: rewrite

Reads as a model wrote it: yes

Problems:
1. "the financial centre of another commercial empire four years after another war" — the doubled "another" made me read it twice, and the whole analogy is squeezed into one subordinate clause.
2. "The war dead and the office crowd are the same people, and all wars are the one war." — a pronouncement given as fact. It is a reading, a well-known one, and should be marked as one.
3. "The name is a hat, an American hat, on a bridge in London." — a bow to end on. The useful part, that Stetson is an American name and the name of a hat, should be said plainly.
4. "Stetson has often been taken for Pound" — by whom? One name would do (for the scholar).
5. "a trade war between two commercial empires" — a characterisation added to set up the parallel; it reads as loading the dice. Fine if the scholar stands behind it, but keep it short.

Keep: "Mylae is the sea battle of 260 BC in which Rome first beat Carthage at sea, early in the First Punic War"; "nothing in the poem needs him to be anyone in particular." Also the title.

Draft:
TITLE: Stetson, and the ships at Mylae
BODY:
Mylae was a sea battle of 260 BC, early in the First Punic War, in which Rome beat Carthage at sea for the first time. Rome and Carthage were trading empires, and the war was a war over trade. Stetson is met in the City of London, the financial centre of the British Empire, four years after the end of the war of 1914–18. The usual reading is that the ancient war stands for the recent one, and that the man who was “in the ships” at Mylae had been in that war too. Stetson is an American name and the name of an American hat. He has often been taken for Pound, who wore wide-brimmed hats, but nothing in the poem needs him to be anyone in particular.

### corpse

Verdict: rewrite

Reads as a model wrote it: yes

Problems:
1. The first sentence carries two definitions on colons and appositives; I lost the thread at "and the “Osiris beds,”". Three sentences would carry the same facts.
2. "that ritual gone domestic and gone wrong" — a pairing for effect, and a reading given as fact.
3. "Will it bloom? Has the frost got at it?" — the poem's own questions staged back at me.
4. "it is the question of the whole poem: can anything come back?" — the bow. A claim about what the poem is, and it tells me what to feel.
5. "Stetson's suburban garden" — nothing in the lines says suburban; it is an assumption dressed as a detail.

Keep: the facts of the first sentence (the volumes, the gardens of Adonis, the Osiris beds). Also the title.

Draft:
TITLE: The buried god
BODY:
In <i>The Golden Bough</i>, in the volumes Eliot’s headnote names (<i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>), Frazer describes two rites of a god who dies and returns. The “gardens of Adonis” were pots of quick-sprouting seed, carried in the god’s funeral procession and then thrown into the sea. The “Osiris beds” were effigies of the god filled with earth and corn and buried, so that the sprouting grain showed his resurrection. The corpse planted in Stetson’s garden is usually read as a version of these rites, a body buried so that something will grow from it. The questions about blooming and frost are the ones a man asks about his bulbs.

### dog

Verdict: trim

Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. Title "with one word changed" against the body's "swaps the wolf for a dog and “foe” for “friend”" — the title says one, the note counts two, and the quote above the note shows more ("hence" for "thence", "it" for "them"). I noticed at once.
2. "The domestic pet, man's friend, becomes the thing that will not let the buried god stay buried long enough to sprout." — a long sentence carrying a reading as fact, and "the buried god" assumes I have read the note before this one.
3. "Readers who want more have found the Dog Star" — a wink. Say who proposed Sirius (for the scholar).
4. "The change of one word is the kind of thing Eliot meant when he wrote..." — repeats the one-word error and tells me what Eliot meant. The quotation stands well enough on its own.

Keep: "Cornelia's mad song over her murdered son in <i>The White Devil</i> (1612), Act V scene iv. Eliot swaps the wolf for a dog and “foe” for “friend,” and capitalises the Dog."

Draft:
TITLE: Webster's dirge, altered
BODY:
Cornelia’s mad song over her murdered son in <i>The White Devil</i> (1612), Act V scene iv. Eliot swaps the wolf for a dog and “foe” for “friend,” and capitalises the Dog. In Webster the wolf digs up the unburied dead. In Eliot the danger to the corpse in the garden is the dog, man’s friend. Some commentators [scholar to name one] take the capital D to point to the Dog Star, Sirius, whose rising announced the flooding of the Nile and the return of Osiris. In “Philip Massinger” (1920) Eliot wrote that “immature poets imitate; mature poets steal,” and that good poets make what they take “into something better, or at least something different” [scholar to confirm the second phrase].

### hypocrite-lecteur

Verdict: trim

Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "Eliot ends the first section by doing the same thing to you." — the "you" is the poem's, so it is arguable, but from here the note keeps addressing me.
2. "the Frenchness of the line is part of the accusation: you were meant to recognise it." — the colon flourish and the bow; a reading given as fact, and it tells me what I was meant to do.
3. "whoever is holding the book" — dramatised. "The reader" says it.

Keep: "The last line of “Au Lecteur,” the poem that opens <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i> (1857). Baudelaire's catalogue of vices ends with the worst, boredom, and then turns on the reader and makes him an accomplice." Also the title.

Draft:
TITLE: The reader accused
BODY:
The last line of “Au Lecteur,” the poem that opens <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i> (1857). Baudelaire’s catalogue of vices ends with the worst of them, boredom, and then turns on the reader and makes him an accomplice. Eliot ends Part I with the same turn. The “you” who has been Stetson becomes the reader of the poem. One reading is that the line is left in French on purpose, since only a reader who knows Baudelaire will recognise the charge.

### summary

What I threw out most often was the last sentence. Six of the seven notes end on a quotable line ("and the dead"; "actually looks like"; "come up out of it every morning"; "an American hat, on a bridge in London"; "can anything come back?"; "you were meant to recognise it"), and in every case the sentence before it was the useful one. Second, readings presented as facts, usually in pairs: "damned twice over", "gone domestic and gone wrong", "all wars are the one war". I asked for these to be marked as readings or dropped. The facts in the batch are good and I kept all of them; the notes on Dante, Woolnoth and Webster are at their best where they say what the source is and what happens in it, and the drafts mostly cut back to that. Two small things for the editor: the title of "dog" says one word changed when the body counts two; and three notes in a row (unreal-city, london-bridge-dead, woolnoth) each tell me that Eliot worked at Lloyds Bank, so woolnoth should carry the full fact and the other two a clause.
