### voice poem
Verdict: correct
Checked: The 1922 text of Part I (work/poem-part1.md). The claims about other parts (the room in Part II, Phlebas, the thunder's glosses) are consistent with the poem; the note gives no line numbers, so none to check.
Substance:
1. "The voice that opens the poem" — where this voice ends and Marie begins (7/8) is an editorial choice, not a datum. The "us" of line 5 ("Winter kept us warm") recurs at line 8 ("Summer surprised us"), which is why many readers take 1–18 as a single voice. One sentence, here or in the lens intro, saying the divisions are the site's own would cover all ten labels.
2. "speaks the thunder's glosses" — an assignment, not a fact; and the glosses speak as an "I" with a "friend" ("My friend, blood shaking my heart", Part V), which sits oddly with "It is never quite a person".
3. Internal inconsistency: the sosostris label has "the poem's voice" speaking 43–46, which this note's list of appearances omits. Either add the introduction of Sosostris or drop the enumeration.
Draft: NOTE: The unassigned narrating voice: it opens the poem, introduces Madame Sosostris (43–46), describes the room in Part II, elegises Phlebas, and is given the thunder's glosses here. The divisions between it and the named voices are the site's own.

### voice marie
Verdict: correct
Checked: Lines 8–18 against the 1922 text. The Larisch identification cited from memory of Valerie Eliot's note in the 1971 facsimile (not consultable online).
Substance:
1. "A displaced Austrian aristocrat" — an inference given as fact. The text supplies the archduke cousin (13), German speech and a rootless routine (18); "Austrian" and "displaced" rest on those and on the identification of the speaker with Countess Marie Larisch, niece of Empress Elisabeth (proposed from her memoir <i>My Past</i> by G. L. K. Morris, 1954; Valerie Eliot's facsimile note records that Eliot had met Larisch and that the sledding lines followed her conversation). Defensible, but the legend should either lean on the documented identification or claim only what the text gives ("an aristocrat of the old Austrian circle — the archduke of line 13 was her cousin").
2. "talking over coffee" — the coffee is inside the memory (11: "And drank coffee, and talked for an hour", in the remembered Hofgarten). That she speaks over coffee now is an inference from the conversational run of 8–18, not something the poem says.
3. "with one stranger's sentence at line 12" — that line 12 is another speaker is a reading, stated here as fact; the poem marks no change of speaker (see voice stranger).
Draft: NOTE: An aristocrat of the old Austrian circle — the archduke of line 13 was her cousin — remembering a summer and a childhood. Lines 8–18; the site gives the German sentence at 12 to another voice.

### voice stranger
Verdict: correct
Checked: Line 12 against the 1922 text.
Substance:
1. "A voice in the café" — there is no café in the text; the remembered scene is the colonnade and the Hofgarten (9–10). If the label needs a place, the Hofgarten is the poem's.
2. "overheard" — the poem marks no new speaker at 12; hearing the sentence as another party's talk rather than Marie's own is a reading (it follows from taking Marie for an Austrian, since the line claims Lithuanian birth and true Germanness). Both readings are current; the legend should own the choice as the site's.
Draft: LABEL: A voice in the Hofgarten
NOTE: One sentence of German, line 12. The poem marks no new speaker; the site hears it as overheard talk rather than Marie's own.

### voice prophet
Verdict: correct
Checked: Lines 19–30 against the 1922 text and Eliot's notes (20: Ezekiel II.i; 23: Ecclesiastes XII.v); KJV wording of Ezekiel 2:1, Ecclesiastes 12:5 and Isaiah 32:2 verified at gutenberg.org/ebooks/10.
Substance:
1. "Ezekiel's and Isaiah's voice" — Eliot's own notes cite Ezekiel (II.i) and Ecclesiastes (XII.v). Isaiah is a commentators' parallel for the rock (XXXII.2, "the shadow of a great rock in a weary land"; so Southam, Rainey, Ricks–McCue), not Eliot's. The legend asserts the uncited prophet as fact and omits a book Eliot cites.
2. "Ezekiel's… voice, addressing the 'son of man'" — in Ezekiel the voice that says "Son of man, stand upon thy feet" (II.i) is the Lord's, addressing the prophet. The passage speaks as that commanding voice; it is not Ezekiel who says "son of man".
Draft: NOTE: A voice out of the Old Testament, addressing the hearer as 'Son of man' — the address the Lord uses to Ezekiel (Eliot's note to line 20 cites Ezekiel II.i; his note to 23, Ecclesiastes XII.v; commentators add Isaiah XXXII.2 for the rock's shadow). Lines 19–30.

### voice lover
Verdict: correct
Checked: Lines 37–41 against the 1922 text; the poem's other first-person admissions.
Substance:
1. "the poem's one admission of feeling" — false as a count and a reading passed off as fact. "And I was frightened" (15) stands seven lines above this passage, and Part V has "My friend, blood shaking my heart / The awful daring of a moment's surrender". The superlative is the tidy-flourish habit the site is purging, and here it misstates.
Draft: NOTE: The one who came back from the Hyacinth garden and could not speak. Lines 37–41.

### voice sosostris
Verdict: correct
Checked: Lines 43–59 against the 1922 text.
Substance:
1. "Lines 46–59, framed by the poem's voice at 43–46" — line 46 is claimed for both voices, and "framed" implies enclosure on both sides. In the text her speech begins inside line 46 ("Here, said she" — "said she" being the narrator's tag), after an introduction in the narrating voice (43 to mid-46); nothing follows her at 59 but a new scene. Say "introduced by", and place the join inside 46.
2. The parenthesis at 48, "(Those are pearls that were his eyes. Look!)", is Ariel's song (The Tempest I.ii) and returns at line 125; some readers hear it as an interpolating voice rather than the fortune-teller's patter. Giving it to Sosostris is a defensible choice, but it is a choice the gloss could own.
Draft: NOTE: The clairvoyante, dealing the cards. Her speech runs from inside line 46 ("Here, said she") to 59, after an introduction in the poem's voice, 43–46.

### voice walker
Verdict: correct
Checked: Lines 60–76 against the 1922 text of Part I; the Part III ranges against the standard text, anchored by Eliot's own notes (210 currants; 257 "V. The Tempest, as before"; 264 St. Magnus Martyr) — site numbers agree with Eliot's below 346.
Substance:
1. "Lines … 207–211" — the Eugenides encounter runs to 214. Line 211 ends "C.i.f. London: documents at sight," and 212–214 — "Asked me in demotic French / To luncheon at the Cannon Street Hotel / Followed by a weekend at the Metropole." — are still the walker's narration ("Asked me"). As given, the range cuts its own speaker off mid-sentence. It should read 207–214.
2. "walks from the Strand to Lower Thames Street" — the text has the music creeping "along the Strand, up Queen Victoria Street" (258), and the walker "can sometimes hear" the mandoline "Beside a public bar in Lower Thames Street" (259–260). The named walk is Strand to Queen Victoria Street; Lower Thames Street is where he sometimes hears the music, not a stated terminus.
3. That the "I" of 257 resumes this walker rather than continuing Tiresias's sequence (215 ff.) is a reading — fine as the lens's assignment if the lens owns its divisions.
Draft: NOTE: The 'I' of the City: he watches the crowd flow over London Bridge and cries out to Stetson; Mr. Eugenides asks him to luncheon; he hears the mandoline by the bar in Lower Thames Street. Lines 60–76, 207–214, 257–265.

### draft boston
Verdict: correct
Checked: Edmund Wilson's review of the facsimile, NYRB, 18 Nov 1971 (nybooks.com/articles/1971/11/18/the-first-waste-landii/): the excision of the opening was "made by Eliot himself"; the narrator is picked up by the police and rescued by an acquaintance; Myrtle refuses him but gives him bed, bath and breakfast. Draft lines as quoted in criticism ("First we had a couple of feelers down at Tom's place"; "When we got into the show, up in Row A"). Accounts derived from the facsimile give 54 lines, struck lightly in pencil, with "April" at the top of the second typescript page. I could not consult the 1971 facsimile itself.
Substance:
1. "a burlesque show" — the draft says only "the show" ("When we got into the show, up in Row A", where the narrator kicks at the drum and the girl squeals). No account I can reach calls it a burlesque; "a show" is what the page supports.
2. "a run from a policeman" — there is no run. The narrator is picked up by a plain-clothes man (a "fly cop") and got off when a well-connected acquaintance intervened (Wilson's account of the facsimile). "A brush with a policeman" would be accurate.
3. "struck through in pencil, apparently by Eliot before Pound saw it" — the pencil cancellation is generally credited to Eliot (so Wilson, reviewing the facsimile in 1971), and the leaf carries no comment by Pound; but nothing records whether Pound had read the lines first. "Before Pound saw it" is conjecture stated as near-fact; say what is known.
4. No source is named. The cancelled page is known from Valerie Eliot's 1971 facsimile; a record about the drafts should say so once.
5. "What remains begins already tired" — an interpretation in the register the site is purging (compare the brief's "a poem that starts already exhausted"), stated as description; and "the second first line the poem had" is the counting flourish, true only trivially.
6. "fifty-four lines" — matches the published descriptions of the leaf; I could not count them against the facsimile. Worth one check against the book (pp. 4–5) before this ships.
Rights: the record describes and quotes only "Tom's place" — compliant; a rewrite should hold to that.
Draft: BODY: In Valerie Eliot's 1971 facsimile, the typescript of Part I opens with fifty-four cancelled lines: a night out in Boston told in a loose vernacular — drinks at "Tom's place", a show, a call at Myrtle's, a brush with a policeman smoothed over by a well-connected friend, a walk home at sunrise. The lines are struck through in pencil, generally taken to be Eliot's own cancellation; the leaf carries no comment by Pound, and whether he read it is not recorded. The published opening, "April is the cruellest month", stood at the top of the typescript's second page. Pound's marks on the rest of Part I are few.

### draft title-draft
Verdict: correct
Checked: <i>Our Mutual Friend</i>, Book the First, ch. 16, at gutenberg.org/ebooks/883: "You mightn't think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices." Malory, <i>Le Morte d'Arthur</i>, Book XVII ch. iii, at gutenberg.org/ebooks/1252: "men call it the lands of the two marches, the waste land, for that dolorous stroke". Weston, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, at gutenberg.org/ebooks/4090 (the Waste Land motif "the very heart of our problem"). Eliot's 1922 headnote (work/poem-part1.md) crediting the title to Weston. Wikipedia and centenary accounts confirm the heading on the typescripts of the first two parts only.
Substance:
1. "The Dickens title survives in the notes only as a habit of mind" — false on its face: the 1922 Notes nowhere mention Dickens. The working title was not public at all until the drafts — sold to John Quinn in 1922, resurfaced in the Berg Collection and published in 1971. The rest of the sentence ("the poem reads the world's news aloud in every voice it can do") is a reading given as fact, with the bow tied.
2. TITLE "The title that was crossed out" — I find no account that the typed heading was itself struck through; what is reported is that the title was dropped. Unless the facsimile shows a cancellation stroke through the heading, call it the title that was dropped.
3. "with Weston's Grail country and Malory's 'waste land' of the Dolorous Stroke behind it" — Weston is documented: Eliot's own headnote says "Not only the title, but the plan…" were suggested by her book. Malory is not cited by Eliot anywhere. The phrase is genuinely Malory's (Book XVII ch. iii — and there of King Hurlame's stroke; Balin's "dolorous stroke" is Book II), and commentators adduce it as the Grail tradition's term; but as written the note makes Malory a source of the title, which is an editorial inference.
4. Missing: the Dickens sentence itself, which a reader would want and which is freely quotable (1865) — Betty Higden praising the foundling Sloppy's newspaper reading.
Draft: BODY: The typescripts of the first two parts are headed "He Do the Police in Different Voices". The phrase is Betty Higden's, in Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (1865), praising the foundling Sloppy: "You mightn't think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices." Eliot dropped the heading before publication. His 1922 headnote credits the title he kept to Jessie L. Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, whose subject is the Grail legend's Waste Land; the phrase itself is as old as Malory — "the waste land, for that dolorous stroke".

### draft epigraph-draft
Verdict: correct
Checked: Conrad's text at gutenberg.org/ebooks/219: "Did he live his life again in every detail of desire, temptation, and surrender during that supreme moment of complete knowledge? He cried in a whisper at some image, at some vision—he cried out twice, a cry that was no more than a breath: 'The horror! The horror!'" The Pound–Eliot exchange as quoted in accounts of the facsimile and the Letters ("weighty enough to stand the citation"; "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative"; Pound's "Do as you like about Conrad: who am I to grudge him his laurel crown?"), confirmed at towntopics.com/2022/11/09/soundings-and-echoes-the-waste-land-at-100/. The Sibyl's myth from Ovid, Metamorphoses XIV (from knowledge; not fetched).
Substance:
1. "Kurtz's 'The horror! the horror!' … stood at the head of the typescript" — what stood there was the fuller passage: Marlow's account of Kurtz's death, from "Did he live his life again in every detail of desire, temptation, and surrender…" down to the cry. Conrad (1899) is out of copyright, so the record can give the passage or its close rather than the two words alone.
2. Sequence: after Eliot's defence Pound gave way ("Do as you like about Conrad: who am I to grudge him his laurel crown?") and Eliot changed it anyway. As written, the note implies Eliot yielded to Pound; the half-sentence restores what happened. The exchange should also carry its source: letters of January 1922, quoted in Valerie Eliot's 1971 facsimile.
3. "an immortal's wish to be done with hers" — the Sibyl is not an immortal. Apollo granted her as many years as the grains of sand in her hand, and she did not ask for youth (Ovid, Metamorphoses XIV); she is withering toward nothing, which is why she wants to die. The closing antithesis, besides being in the manner under purge, misstates the myth. Cut or correct.
Rights: Conrad may be quoted freely (1899); the phrases from Pound's and Eliot's letters are within the phrase-or-two rule.
Draft: BODY: On the typescript the epigraph was from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>: Marlow's account of Kurtz's death — "Did he live his life again in every detail of desire, temptation, and surrender…?" — ending in the cry "The horror! the horror!" Pound demurred: Conrad, he wrote, might not be "weighty enough to stand the citation". Eliot defended the passage as "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative"; Pound gave way ("who am I to grudge him his laurel crown?"); and Eliot replaced it all the same with the Sibyl of Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i>, who, asked by the boys what she wants, answers that she wants to die (letters of January 1922, quoted in Valerie Eliot's 1971 facsimile).

### summary
The corrections that matter most:
1. voice walker: 207–211 must be 207–214 — line 211 ends mid-sentence and 212–214 ("Asked me in demotic French…") is still the walker speaking.
2. voice shepherd: "Four words" — the line has five ("Oed' und leer das Meer").
3. voice lover: "the poem's one admission of feeling" is false ("And I was frightened", line 15; Part V's "blood shaking my heart").
4. voice prophet: Eliot's notes cite Ezekiel and Ecclesiastes; Isaiah is a commentators' addition; and in Ezekiel the voice saying "Son of man" is the Lord's, not Ezekiel's.
5. voice marie / voice stranger: giving line 12 to an overheard stranger is a reading stated as fact, and the scene is the Hofgarten, not a café.
6. draft boston: the draft says "the show", not a burlesque; the policeman picks the narrator up and is talked round — no run; the pencil cancellation is credited to Eliot (Wilson, 1971) but "before Pound saw it" is conjecture; name the facsimile as source; verify "fifty-four" against the book.
7. draft title-draft: the Dickens title does not "survive in the notes" — the 1922 Notes never mention Dickens; Weston is Eliot's stated source for the title and Malory an editorial inference; "crossed out" is unattested — the heading was dropped.
8. draft epigraph-draft: the typescript epigraph was the full Conrad passage ending in the cry, not the two words; and the Sibyl is no immortal (Ovid: years like grains of sand, without youth) — the closing antithesis misstates the myth.
Sailor and hyacinth stand as written.
