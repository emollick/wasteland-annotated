# Scholar report, batch 6, round 1

### card-note phoenician-sailor
Verdict: correct
Checked: Eliot's notes 46 ("The Phoenician Sailor and the Merchant appear later… Death by Water is executed in Part IV") and 218 (the merchant "melts into the Phoenician Sailor, and the latter is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples"); The Tempest I.ii (Ariel's song, sung to Ferdinand about his father); "Fear death by water" at 55. "Not in any Tarot pack" is right — Eliot's note 46 says he departed from the pack to suit his own convenience.
Substance:
1. "He is Phlebas of Part IV, the merchant of Part III, and Ferdinand's drowned father" — three flat identities of unequal warrant. Phlebas has Eliot's note behind it. The merchant has the note's own, more careful verb: "melts into" — worth quoting rather than flattening to "is". "Ferdinand's drowned father" is wrong twice over: Eliot's note ties the Sailor to Ferdinand himself ("not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples"), not to the father; and in the play the father is not drowned — the pearls are Ariel's fiction, sung to Ferdinand. If the card wants the Tempest link, it is the song, and the song is a false report.
2. "Part IV obliges" — the substance is Eliot's own ("Death by Water is executed in Part IV"); the quip is the manner. The note's phrase does the work better.
Draft (NOTE): Not in any Tarot pack. He is Phlebas of Part IV; the one-eyed merchant, Eliot's note says, "melts into" him. The pearls are from Ariel's song in <i>The Tempest</i> — sung to Ferdinand about a father who has not in fact drowned. "Fear death by water," says the clairvoyante, and Eliot's note confirms it: "Death by Water is executed in Part IV."

### card-note belladonna
Verdict: correct
Checked: bella donna, the plant (Atropa belladonna), and the cosmetic use of the drops — all sound. The Leonardo gloss is the standard one; Part II's woman sits in "The Chair… like a burnished throne" (77).
Substance:
1. "Leonardo's Virgin of the Rocks is behind the title" — the universal gloss, but a gloss: Eliot names no painting. "Is usually traced to" (the register the site's model notes use) states it exactly.
2. "the woman in the chair of Part II is in front of it" — a reading passed as fact. Eliot's note 46 lists which cards reappear, and Belladonna is not among them; the identification with the woman of "A Game of Chess" is critical tradition (standard since Grover Smith), and should be marked as the usual reading.
3. "'The lady of situations' is gossip-column English." — an assertion about idiom with no source I can find. The guides guess variously (social intrigue; the "situations" of the newspaper columns); no one knows. Mark it as a guess or cut it.
Draft (NOTE): "Beautiful lady": the deadly nightshade, and the drops women used to widen their pupils. The title is usually traced to Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>, and the woman in the chair of Part II is often read as this card come true. Nobody has settled what "the lady of situations" means.

### card-note one-eyed-merchant
Verdict: correct
Checked: Mr. Eugenides at site line 209; the bundle at 52–54; Eliot's note 46 (the Merchant reappears) and 218 ("seller of currants").
Substance:
1. "One-eyed because seen in profile" — stated as the reason, but the poem never explains the eye; the profile explanation is the guides' conjecture (Southam offers it), here adopted as the deck's design rationale. The DECK text frames it rightly as the print's necessity; the NOTE should mark it as the usual guess, not the poem's fact.
Draft (NOTE): Not a card. He is Mr. Eugenides of Smyrna, with his currants and his invitation, at line 209; one-eyed, on the usual guess, because a card shows its figure in profile. Something on his back the clairvoyante is forbidden to see.

### card-note blank
Verdict: correct
Checked: the quotation of 52–54 (exact). Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. XII ("Mithra and Attis"): the Mysteries of Attis and Mithra travelled the Roman Empire by three channels, "First, commercial, through the medium of Syrian merchants… 'founding their chapels at the same time as their counting-houses'" (Weston citing Cumont; text checked on Gutenberg). The application of that passage to this merchant is Cleanth Brooks's (1939) and has been standard since.
Substance:
1. "Weston's merchants carried the Grail mysteries along the trade routes" — three slips in one clause. They are Syrian merchants; what they carried, in her account, were the Mysteries of Attis and Mithra — the cults she argues lie behind the Grail — not "the Grail mysteries"; and the whole is her thesis (built on Cumont), so it should be marked as her argument, not common fact. The reading that Mr. Eugenides therefore carries a mystery he knows nothing of is Brooks's.
Draft (NOTE): "This card, / Which is blank, is something he carries on his back, / Which I am forbidden to see." The one card the reading cannot open. Weston argued that the old Mysteries travelled the empire with the Syrian merchants, who founded "their chapels at the same time as their counting-houses"; read with her, the merchant carries a mystery he knows nothing of.

### card-note hanged-man
Verdict: correct
Checked: Waite's card XII (hanged by one foot from living wood, face serene, nimbed) — the description is right. Eliot's note 46: "associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer", and the hooded figure of the Emmaus passage — both quoted rightly in substance. Frazer's "Hanged God" is the chapter in Adonis Attis Osiris (the volumes Eliot names in his headnote): Attis, the god hanged in effigy on the pine, whose death and return renew vegetation.
Substance:
1. "the sacrificed king whose death brings the rain" — not Frazer's Hanged God. The Hanged God's office is the renewal of the year's growth; rain-making kings are a different part of The Golden Bough. The poem's drought makes the slide natural, but the clause states as Frazer what is the poem's own economy. "Whose death renews the year" (or "the land") is both true to Frazer and truer to Eliot's note, which claims only an association "in my mind".
2. "No sacrifice, no resurrection, in this deal." — the standard reading of the missing card (Brooks's line of argument) delivered as a verdict, in the fragment manner. Mark it as the reading it is, or let the fact ("Madame Sosostris does not find him") carry it.
Draft (NOTE): Card XII: a young man hanging by one foot from a living tree, serene, haloed. Eliot's note associates him with "the Hanged God of Frazer" — the god whose death renews the year — and with the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus. Madame Sosostris does not find him; on the usual reading, the one death that would redeem the rest is missing from her pack.

### card-note crowd-in-a-ring
Verdict: correct
Checked: the vision is line 56; "Unreal City" is 60; the crowd flows over London Bridge at 62; the hooded hordes are Part V (site 369). Inferno III: the neutrals wheel after the whirling banner, and Eliot's note 63 cites that canto for the bridge crowd. Frazer's May-dances exist (The Magic Art), but no note of Eliot's points the ring there.
Substance:
1. "the commuters on London Bridge four lines later" — six lines later. Line 56 to 62 is six; four lines later is "Unreal City" (60), which has no commuters in it yet. The count is the checkable kind and it is off.
2. "Frazer's villagers round the maypole" — an association with no citation behind it, listed level with two links Eliot's own notes support (Dante at 63; the crowds at 46). Keep it marked as an association, or cut it.
Draft (NOTE): Not a card but what she sees. The crowd comes true six lines later, flowing over London Bridge — Eliot's note sends it to Dante's neutrals — and again in the hooded hordes of Part V.

### summary
1. phoenician-sailor: "Ferdinand's drowned father" is doubly wrong — Eliot's note ties the Sailor to Ferdinand himself, and in The Tempest the father is not drowned; the pearls are Ariel's fiction. Quote the note's "melts into" for the merchant rather than flat identity.
2. blank: Weston's merchants are Syrian merchants carrying the Mysteries of Attis and Mithra (ch. XII, citing Cumont) — not "the Grail mysteries" — and it is her thesis; the application to this merchant is Brooks's.
3. hanged-man: Frazer's Hanged God renews vegetation; "whose death brings the rain" hands the poem's drought-logic to Frazer. "No sacrifice, no resurrection" is a reading delivered as verdict.
4. crowd-in-a-ring: the bridge crowd is six lines after the vision, not four; the maypole is nobody's citation.
5. one-eyed-merchant: "one-eyed because seen in profile" is the guides' conjecture (and the deck's design rationale), not the poem's fact — mark it as the usual guess.
6. belladonna: the Leonardo gloss should be "usually traced to"; the Part II identification is a standard reading, not fact; "gossip-column English" is an unsourced assertion about idiom.
Verified and sound: "not in any Tarot pack" / "not a card"; Eliot's "quite arbitrarily" and line 424; trump numbers X and XII and the Waite iconography; lines 209, 320, and the quotations throughout.
