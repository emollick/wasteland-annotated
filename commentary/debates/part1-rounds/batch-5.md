# Batch 5: the ten ways into Part I, the pathway stops in Part I, and the notes on the tarot cards drawn for Part I

Each item below is one piece of commentary exactly as it stands in the site's source files. Review every one. Keep the ids exactly.

## A. The list at the head of Part I. Its title: “Ten ways into The Burial of the Dead” (this title is a recurring heading, shared across parts, not yours to change; each item’s heading and paragraph are)

---
### way-1  (kind: listen; opens: listen)
HEADING: Hear it before you read it
BODY: Virginia Woolf, who heard Eliot read the poem at dinner in June 1922, wrote in her diary that "he sang it & chanted it rhythmed it." Start with his own voice: the listening room has his recordings, and the first section is where the chant is plainest. Then read the seventy-six lines silently and notice where his stresses fell.

---
### way-2  (kind: lens; opens: voices)
HEADING: Count the speakers
BODY: Switch on Voices and the section splits into at least nine: the poem's own voice, Marie, a stranger in a café, a prophet, a sailor in Wagner, a girl with hyacinths, someone who could not speak, a clairvoyante, a commuter. The typescript's title was "He Do the Police in Different Voices." Ask, of each, whether it is quoting someone else.

---
### way-3  (kind: do; opens: cards)
HEADING: Lay out the cards
BODY: Madame Sosostris deals seven cards and one vision. Turn them over one at a time on the table and read the note on each. Then ask what she leaves out: no Hanged Man, no water except the kind that drowns.

---
### way-4  (kind: map; opens: places)
HEADING: Walk the commute
BODY: London Bridge, King William Street, St Mary Woolnoth, the stroke of nine. It is a route of about six hundred yards, the one Eliot walked to Lloyds Bank. Follow it on the map, then read lines 60–68 as a man who has just walked it in fog.

---
### way-5  (kind: source; opens: chaucer)
HEADING: Put Chaucer beside it
BODY: Read the first twelve lines of the General Prologue, then Eliot's first seven. Same month, same rain, same roots. Decide what has changed between 1387 and 1922, and whether it is the weather or the people.

---
### way-6  (kind: lens; opens: water)
HEADING: Follow the water
BODY: Spring rain, forgetful snow, a shower of rain, dry stone, no sound of water, a handful of dust, a drowned sailor, "Fear death by water." The section's weather is its argument. The Water lens tints every line by what it is made of.

---
### way-7  (kind: path; opens: dead)
HEADING: Meet the dead
BODY: Dante's crowd at the gate of Hell, Limbo's sighs, Webster's dirge, the corpse in the garden, Frazer's buried god. A short walk through the section's underworld, with the source passages open.

---
### way-8  (kind: draft; opens: drafts)
HEADING: Read what was cut
BODY: The section did not begin with April. It began with fifty-four lines of a night out in Boston. The drafts view shows where the cut fell, and what the poem gained by starting exhausted.

---
### way-9  (kind: lens; opens: tongues)
HEADING: Leave the German alone
BODY: Five lines of Wagner, one café sentence and a line of Baudelaire are left untranslated. Switch on Tongues to see the translations, then switch it off and read the section as someone in 1922 without German would have read it: as sound, and as a wall.

---
### way-10  (kind: ask; opens: nothing)
HEADING: Three questions with no answer
BODY: When were you "neither living nor dead"? What corpse have you planted in a garden, and have you checked on it? Whose shadow at evening rises to meet you? The section asks them; it does not answer. Neither need you, yet.

## B. Pathway stops in Part I. A stop's text appears beside its line when the reader walks that pathway. Each pathway's title and intro are shared (they live on the Pathways page) and are given for context only.

---
#### pathway “A first reading” (id first). INTRO, for context: Twelve stops for someone meeting the poem for the first time. Each stop is a place to stand, not an explanation. Read the lines, read the note, move on.

### first@1
STOP at line 1: Start with the sound. Read the first seven lines aloud. Five of the seven lines end on a hanging participle: breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding. The poem is about things that will not finish.

### first@19
STOP at line 19: A new voice. Nobody announces the change; the tone does. This is the poem's method for four hundred lines: voices arrive and leave without introduction.

### first@35
STOP at line 35: The one moment of feeling in Part I. Between two lines of Wagner, someone remembers being unable to speak. Hold on to "the heart of light, the silence."

### first@43
STOP at line 43: A comic fortune-teller who is also the poem's prophet. Everything she deals comes true. Note the card she cannot find.

### first@60
STOP at line 60: The real London: a bridge, a street, a clock. And the dead, from Dante, walking over it to work. The poem's trick, learned from Baudelaire, is that both are true at once.

---
#### pathway “Water” (id water). INTRO, for context: The poem's hydrology, from spring rain to the last rain. Follow the water and you follow the argument: water is what the waste land needs, water is what drowns you, and the two are never told apart.

### water@4
STOP at line 4: "Dull roots with spring rain." The first water is unwelcome.

### water@24
STOP at line 24: "the dry stone no sound of water." The first drought, and the first of the poem's many sounds of water that are not there.

### water@42
STOP at line 42: "Oed' und leer das Meer." The sea, empty. Wagner's shepherd sees no ship.

### water@47
STOP at line 47: The drowned Phoenician Sailor, and Ariel's pearls. The first death by water is a card.

### water@55
STOP at line 55: "Fear death by water." The reading's one instruction, and the reverse of what the Grail legend advises.

---
#### pathway “The dead” (id dead). INTRO, for context: Burials, drownings, bones and the crowd that death has undone. The poem's first section is called The Burial of the Dead, from the Book of Common Prayer, and the dead never quite stay in it.

### dead@0
STOP at line 0: The Sibyl, who cannot die, in her jar.

### dead@2
STOP at line 2: "Lilacs out of the dead land." The dead land is the first thing named after April.

### dead@23
STOP at line 23: "the dead tree gives no shelter." Ezekiel's valley, Ecclesiastes' long home.

### dead@39
STOP at line 39: "I was neither / Living nor dead." The hyacinth garden.

### dead@62
STOP at line 62: Dante's crowd on London Bridge: "I had not thought death had undone so many."

### dead@71
STOP at line 71: "That corpse you planted last year in your garden." Frazer's buried god, in a suburb.

### dead@74
STOP at line 74: Webster's dirge for unburied men, with the wolf turned into a dog.

---
#### pathway “The women” (id women). INTRO, for context: Eliot's note on Tiresias says "all the women are one woman." Here they are, in order of appearance, so that you can test the claim. Most of them speak; several are quotations; two are queens; one is a bird.

### women@0
STOP at line 0: The Sibyl, kept in a bottle, asked by boys what she wants.

### women@13
STOP at line 13: Marie, on the sled, frightened and free.

### women@35
STOP at line 35: The hyacinth girl. Two lines, in quotation marks, and a garden.

### women@43
STOP at line 43: Madame Sosostris, with a cold, afraid of the police, right about everything.

### women@49
STOP at line 49: Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks: a card, a poison, a painting.

---
#### pathway “Cities” (id cities). INTRO, for context: The poem's geography, in the order the poem gives it. It begins in Munich and ends on the Ganges, with London at its centre and five capitals falling in two lines.

### cities@8
STOP at line 8: The Starnberger See and the Hofgarten: Munich, where Eliot spent August 1911.

### cities@12
STOP at line 12: Lithuania, by way of a sentence in German.

### cities@60
STOP at line 60: "Unreal City": London, from Baudelaire's Paris.

### cities@66
STOP at line 66: King William Street and St Mary Woolnoth. Eliot's route to work.

### cities@70
STOP at line 70: Mylae, the sea battle of 260 BC.

---
#### pathway “Birds” (id birds). INTRO, for context: Every bird in the poem is a musician, and every song is a complaint. Recordings, where they exist, are in the listening room.

### birds@23
STOP at line 23: "the cricket no relief." Not a bird, but the first singer, from Ecclesiastes.

---
#### pathway “Nothing” (id nothing). INTRO, for context: One word, followed through the poem. It is said by a lover, a woman with bad nerves, a man who will not answer, a Thames-daughter, and the poor on Margate sands.

### nothing@40
STOP at line 40: "I knew nothing, / Looking into the heart of light, the silence."

---
#### pathway “Eyes” (id eyes). INTRO, for context: Eyes that fail, eyes turned to pearls, eyes fixed on feet, lidless eyes, a one-eyed merchant, a blind seer, eyes that were holden on the road to Emmaus. The poem's people cannot see, or see too much.

### eyes@0
STOP at line 0: "oculis meis vidi": with my own eyes I saw the Sibyl.

### eyes@39
STOP at line 39: "my eyes failed."

### eyes@48
STOP at line 48: "Those are pearls that were his eyes."

### eyes@52
STOP at line 52: "the one-eyed merchant."

### eyes@65
STOP at line 65: "each man fixed his eyes before his feet."

---
#### pathway “The poem in Eliot's life” (id eliot). INTRO, for context: Places and days from the poet's own year, 1921 into 1922, that surface in the text. Not a key to the poem, but a map of where he was standing.

### eliot@8
STOP at line 8: Munich, August 1911: the Hofgarten's colonnade, the lake where a king drowned.

### eliot@35
STOP at line 35: The hyacinth garden: Emily Hale in Boston, or Jean Verdenal in the Luxembourg Gardens with a branch of lilac.

### eliot@66
STOP at line 66: Lloyds Bank, Lombard Street, across the road from St Mary Woolnoth. The clock struck nine as he arrived.

### eliot@0
STOP at line 0: Paris, January 1922: Pound's flat, the pencil, "il miglior fabbro."

## C. Tarot card notes (the “Turn the cards” tool: each card drawn for the site has a SHOWS text describing the drawing and an ANSWERS text tying it to the poem; written by the artist; these live in a shared file, so this debate produces proposals for them)

---
### card sibyl  (title on the card: The Sibyl)
SHOWS: A glass jar hung from a cord in a cave of layered rock; inside it, tiny, a crone crouched in profile with her hair down to her knees. Two boys on the cave floor, one pointing up at her.
ANSWERS: The epigraph, from Petronius: the Sibyl of Cumae, who asked for life without asking for youth, hanging in her jar while the boys ask "Sibyl, what do you want?" and she answers "I want to die."

---
### card marie  (title on the card: Marie)
SHOWS: A sled going down a steep white slope, seen from the side: Marie in front, bent forward, both hands on the rail, her scarf streaming back; her cousin behind her, upright, in a cap. Black firs, a hatched sky, far peaks with their sunward faces left as bare snow, and the sled's track behind.
ANSWERS: 13–18, "Marie, / Marie, hold on tight. And down we went. / In the mountains, there you feel free." (The Countess Marie Larisch, the archduke's cousin.)

---
### card hyacinth-girl  (title on the card: The Hyacinth Girl)
SHOWS: A girl standing in a garden with her arms full of hyacinth spikes, hair wet, looking straight out. Round her the hatching of the garden opens into a bare oval: the heart of light. Dark petals falling at the edges.
ANSWERS: 35–41, "You gave me hyacinths first a year ago; / They called me the hyacinth girl" and "Looking into the heart of light, the silence."

---
### card madame-sosostris  (title on the card: Madame Sosostris)
SHOWS: The clairvoyante at her table in a fringed shawl and headscarf, spectacles on, a handkerchief to her nose (the bad cold), one hand dealing a card. On the table five cards face down showing the pack's back, one face up and blank, and a teacup. A curtain behind.
ANSWERS: 43–59, "Madame Sosostris, famous clairvoyante, / Had a bad cold, nevertheless / Is known to be the wisest woman in Europe, / With a wicked pack of cards." The back on her cards is back.svg in miniature.

---
### card phoenician-sailor  (title on the card: The Drowned Phoenician Sailor)
SHOWS: A sailor in a striped jersey and bell-bottoms sinking head first through the water, hair streaming with the current, a gull above the surface, a ship's wheel already on the bottom. His eyes are two gold pearls.
ANSWERS: 47–48, and Part IV (312–321). "Those are pearls that were his eyes" is Ariel's song from *The Tempest*. He is drawn inverted on purpose: he is the Hanged Man the reading cannot find, gone into the water instead.

---
### card belladonna  (title on the card: Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks)
SHOWS: A woman seated in a grotto of stone, in a black mantle and gown that leave only her face and hands bare; the pupils of her eyes are wide (belladonna drops); her lips are violet; she holds up a sprig of deadly nightshade, leaves, bells and berries. Sea visible through the mouth of the cave.
ANSWERS: 49–50. "The Lady of the Rocks" is Leonardo's *Virgin of the Rocks* (and Pater's Mona Lisa, older than the rocks among which she sits). Belladonna is the plant, the poison, the cosmetic and "beautiful lady".

---
### card three-staves  (title on the card: The Man with Three Staves)
SHOWS: A man seen from behind on a headland of cracked earth, looking out over water toward a low red sun and two barges. His three staves are a crutch under his arm, a fishing rod held out over the water with its line down, and a staff planted in the ground that has begun to sprout. A dead tree at the left.
ANSWERS: 51, with Eliot's note tying him to the Fisher King (so: a wound to lean on, a line to fish with, a rod of office set in the waste ground). The composition follows Pamela Colman Smith's Three of Wands.

---
### card wheel  (title on the card: The Wheel)
SHOWS: Fortune's wheel drawn as a ship's wheel, hub in gold, the air blasted with lines. The same man in a bowler hat at its four stations: enthroned on top, falling on the right, crushed beneath, climbing on the left.
ANSWERS: 51; the ship's wheel looks ahead to "you who turn the wheel and look to windward" (320). The four stations are the medieval *regno, regnavi, sum sine regno, regnabo*.

---
### card one-eyed-merchant  (title on the card: The One-Eyed Merchant)
SHOWS: Mr. Eugenides in profile: a profile shows one eye, which is the only kind of one-eyed man a printed card can have. Bowler, astrakhan collar, long overcoat, unshaven, a pocket full of currants and a handful held out in invitation. On his back a large roped bundle whose contents are not shown. A City wall and a doorway behind.
ANSWERS: 52, and 207–214 (the Smyrna merchant, unshaven, currants, the Cannon Street Hotel). Eliot's note: the merchant "melts into" the Phoenician Sailor.

---
### card blank  (title on the card: The Blank Card)
SHOWS: Bare paper inside the frame. Faintly, at 13% ink, the merchant's bundle shows through from the other side of the card, reversed, as if the print on the back had bled: something is there, and cannot be read. The show-through is in a group id="showthrough" so the page can raise its opacity on hover if it likes.
ANSWERS: 52–54: "this card, / Which is blank, is something he carries on his back, / Which I am forbidden to see."

---
### card hanged-man  (title on the card: The Hanged Man)
SHOWS: A dead tree with one long bough for a beam; a rope tied to it, its noose hanging open. The whole air is dense cross-hatching, and where the Hanged Man should hang, inverted, one leg crooked, the paper is left bare in his shape: the card shows the place he is missing from. Round the head that is not there, a gold halo. Far off at the bottom, on a road, two walkers and a hooded third. A green shoot on the dead bough.
ANSWERS: 54–55: "I do not find / The Hanged Man." Eliot's note ties him to Frazer's hanged god and to the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus (360–366), which is why the three walkers are there, and why the bough sprouts.

---
### card crowd-in-a-ring  (title on the card: The Crowd in a Ring)
SHOWS: On a bare plain under a high horizon, a ring of walkers in bowlers and cloche hats, seen from above, going round clockwise; the path is worn into the ground under their feet. The centre of the ring is empty.
ANSWERS: 56, "I see crowds of people, walking round in a ring", and ahead to the crowd flowing over London Bridge (62–65) and Dante's dead.

---
### card crowd-on-london-bridge  (title on the card: The Crowd on London Bridge)
SHOWS: London Bridge from the river side under the brown fog: two rows of bowler-hatted silhouettes flowing over the parapet, the three open arches with the river through them, a barge with a black sail, and above the skyline the tower of St Mary Woolnoth with its clock at nine.
ANSWERS: 60–68, "A crowd flowed over London Bridge, so many" and the dead sound on the final stroke of nine.

---
### card stetson  (title on the card: Stetson)
SHOWS: A man with a spade in a walled garden bed: paling fence, the earth in clods, a low mound with one lilac bud just showing, and a black dog digging at the corner.
ANSWERS: 69–76, "Stetson! / You who were with me in the ships at Mylae! / That corpse you planted last year in your garden, / Has it begun to sprout?" and the Dog, friend to men, who will dig it up again.
