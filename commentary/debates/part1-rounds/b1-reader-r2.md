Read as a site reader would, against the editor's rewrites in editor/r1-b1.md. Each replacement keeps every fact the rewrite has; anything for the scholar is marked as such. Where I am satisfied I say in one line why, and propose nothing.

### title
Verdict: not yet
1. "Eliot's first note credits the title, "the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism" of the poem to Jessie L. Weston's..." The comma after "title" makes the quotation read as what the title is, and "of the poem" is left hanging outside the quotation marks; I read it twice. Put instead: Eliot's first note says that "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem" were suggested by Jessie L. Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920).
2. "the rites of a much older fertility cult: a king wounded or impotent, his land laid waste with him, and a healing that depends on a stranger who arrives and asks the right question." The colon promises rites and delivers a plot, in three phrases with no verb. It is the shape from Ethan's example, carrying real content this time, so it needs only to become a sentence. Put instead: Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve, beneath their Christian story, the rites of a much older fertility cult. In her account the king is wounded or impotent, his land lies waste with him, and it is healed only when a stranger arrives and asks the right question.
The second paragraph is right as it stands.

### april
Verdict: not yet
1. "but the month is called cruel, because what the rain stirs is "memory and desire", and the snow of winter was "forgetful". Five of the first seven lines end on a participle (breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding), and each object is held over to the next line, so each verb waits a line for what it governs." The "and" after "desire" makes the forgetful snow a second reason for April's cruelty, which it is not; and the participle sentence makes its point twice, the second time as a flourish. Put instead: The rain is still there ("stirring / Dull roots with spring rain"), but the month is called cruel because what the rain stirs is "memory and desire". Winter's snow, by contrast, was "forgetful". Five of the first seven lines end on a participle (breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding) whose object is held over to the next line.
2. "mourns Lincoln, shot in April, with lilacs, "ever-returning spring" and a hermit thrush" On first reading Lincoln is shot with lilacs. Put instead: Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" (1865) mourns Lincoln, who was shot in April; it has lilacs, "ever-returning spring" and a hermit thrush, and Eliot's own note to line 357 names the hermit-thrush.
3. ""Us" in line 5 is never identified, and the voice that goes on to Munich at line 8 belongs to the same verse paragraph, which runs to line 18." A voice belonging to a paragraph is a roundabout way of saying the lines are continuous. Put instead: "Us" in line 5 is never identified. The same verse paragraph runs on without a break through the Munich lines, which begin at line 8, to line 18.
