# Batch 2, scholar, round 1 (lines 31–59)

### frisch-weht

Verdict: stands

Checked: Wagner's libretto, Act I sc. 1 and Isolde's reply (murashev.com German libretto, Act 1; projekt-gutenberg.org returned only an index page). Eliot's note against the 1922 text in `poem-part1.md`.

Substance:
1. Everything checkable is right. The stage direction is "Stimme eines jungen Seemanns (aus der Höhe, wie vom Mast her, vernehmbar)", so "from the masthead" and "unseen" are exact. The quote matches the libretto word for word. Isolde's first words after the song are "Wer wagt mich zu höhnen?" (Who dares to mock me?), so "hears it as mockery" is exact. Eliot's "I, verses 5–8" is exact: the song's lines 1–4 are "Westwärts / schweift der Blick; / ostwärts / streicht das Schiff" (westward roams the gaze; eastward runs the ship), which is also what settles the direction of the wind: toward the sailor's Cornwall, away from Isolde's Ireland. The note could say this in a clause instead of asserting it.
2. "The reply comes at line 42" is the site's reading, not a fact about the opera. Line 42 is from Act III, sung by a different character; the juxtaposition is Eliot's. Say "Eliot's second quotation from the opera, at line 42, is from Act III".
3. "its subject, love that has not happened yet and will end in death" is an anticipation of the whole opera laid on four lines. Not wrong, but a reading; harmless if cut.
4. Small addition a reader would want: the king is Marke of Cornwall, Tristan's uncle.

Draft:
TITLE: The sailor's song
BODY: <i>Tristan und Isolde</i> opens with this song, sung from the masthead by a young sailor who is heard but not seen, as Tristan's ship carries Isolde from Ireland to Cornwall, where she is to marry King Marke, Tristan's uncle. The four lines before the ones Eliot quotes give the ship's course ("westward roams the gaze; eastward runs the ship"), so the wind that blows "toward home" blows toward the sailor's Cornwall and away from Isolde's Ireland. She takes the song as mockery; her first words in the opera are "Who dares to mock me?" Eliot's note gives the act and line numbers and nothing else. His second quotation from the opera, at line 42, comes from Act III.

### hyacinth-girl

Verdict: correct

Checked: Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> X 162–219 (thelatinlibrary.com, ovid.met10). Frazer, <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, 3rd ed. 1914, vol. I, Book II ch. VII "Hyacinth", pp. 313–17 (archive.org, in.ernet.dli.2015.174186). Hyacinthia (Wikipedia; Frazer p. 314). Emily Hale, Jean Verdenal (Wikipedia); Princeton's announcement of the letters (research.princeton.edu). The 1917 dedication of <i>Prufrock</i> (Gutenberg #1459). John Peter 1952 and James E. Miller 1977 (Essays in Criticism archive; Wikipedia). The Criterion sentence (LRB, Mark Ford, "Hyacinth Boy"; other secondary copies agree on the wording). <i>Burnt Norton</i> I (poets.org). <i>Inferno</i> XXXIV 25 (it.wikisource). Eliot's 1929 <i>Dante</i>, the paragraph on Canto XXXIV, in a scan of <i>Selected Essays</i> 1932 (archive.org, dli.ernet.544072).

Substance:
1. "Hyacinth is, in Frazer's reading, another dying and reviving god." Frazer is more tentative. His chapter opens: Hyacinth "has been supposed to belong to the class of gods here discussed" and "has been interpreted as the vegetation which blooms in spring and withers under the scorching heat of the summer sun"; Frazer's own suggestion is that he was an aboriginal deity, "perhaps a dead king". The chapter is the last of the Attis book in <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, the volumes Eliot's headnote names, which is worth saying. (Frazer also notes the flower is "usually supposed" not to be our hyacinth but "a little purple iris" with AI on its petals.)
2. "the Hyacinthia at Sparta": a Spartan festival, held at Amyclae; three days, the first of mourning, then rejoicing (Frazer p. 314; Wikipedia). "at Amyclae, near Sparta" is exact.
3. Ovid checks out: discus rebounds from the ground (X 182–85), the flower springs from the blood (209–12), "AI AI / flos habet inscriptum" (215–16).
4. "one of the few places in the poem where a speaker admits to having felt something": a reading, and a shaky one; Marie says "I was frightened" at line 15. Cut.
5. "The heart of light is Conrad's heart of darkness turned inside out": a reading, unattributed. What is documented: the typescript's epigraph was a passage from <i>Heart of Darkness</i> ending "The horror! the horror!"; Pound wrote on 24 January 1922 that he doubted Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation", and Eliot gave it up (Valerie Eliot's notes in the 1971 facsimile; <i>Letters</i> vol. 1). Give the fact and mark the inversion as a reading.
6. "the mystical blank that Eliot's later poems circle": vague where a fact exists. <i>Burnt Norton</i> I (1935) reuses the phrase: "The surface glittered out of heart of light". A three-word quotation is within the rights rule.
7. "Emily Hale, the Bostonian": she was born in East Orange, New Jersey, and grew up in Chestnut Hill outside Boston (Wikipedia). "of Boston" is safe. Gordon's attachment of the garden to Hale runs from <i>Eliot's Early Years</i> (1977) to <i>The Hyacinth Girl</i> (2022). The letters: 1,131 of them, opened at Princeton on 2 January 2020 (Princeton's announcement).
8. "Others hear Jean Verdenal": name them. John Peter, "A New Interpretation of The Waste Land", <i>Essays in Criticism</i> 1952, withdrawn at the demand of Eliot's solicitors and reprinted in 1969; James E. Miller, <i>T. S. Eliot's Personal Waste Land</i> (1977). Verdenal, a medical officer, was killed at the Dardanelles on 2 May 1915. The 1917 dedication reads "To Jean Verdenal 1889–1915" (Gutenberg text); "mort aux Dardanelles" and the Purgatorio epigraph were added in 1925.
9. Rights: the Criterion sentence (April 1934, "A Commentary") is quoted in full, forty-odd words. The common brief allows the 1934 commentary "a phrase or two". Cut to "waving a branch of lilac" and "the mud of Gallipoli" and give the source. The wording as quoted is otherwise accurate.
10. Missing that matters: "I was neither / Living nor dead" is regularly set beside Dante's words at the sight of Satan, <i>Inferno</i> XXXIV 25, "Io non mori' e non rimasi vivo" (I did not die and did not stay alive), text verified at Wikisource. I could not check which commentary first drew the parallel (Southam and Ricks–McCue, from memory). One caution: Eliot's 1929 <i>Dante</i> discusses Canto XXXIV but does not quote this line (checked in the scan), so the note must not say he did.
11. "Lilacs, line 2. The poem does not choose." The lilac link is a reading; the last sentence is the brief's own example of the bow. Both go.

Draft:
TITLE: The hyacinth garden
BODY: Hyacinthus was the boy Apollo loved and killed by accident with a discus. The flower sprang from his blood, and in Ovid its petals carry the letters of a cry of grief, AI AI (<i>Metamorphoses</i> X, 162–219). Sparta kept a three-day festival for him at Amyclae, the Hyacinthia, a day of mourning followed by rejoicing. Frazer gives him a short chapter at the end of the Attis book of <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, the volumes Eliot names in his headnote, as a figure who "has been interpreted as the vegetation which blooms in spring and withers". None of this is needed to read the lines, which describe a moment of speechlessness and blankness. "Neither / Living nor dead" is often set beside Dante's words at the sight of Satan, <i>Inferno</i> XXXIV 25: "Io non mori' e non rimasi vivo" (I did not die and did not stay alive). "The heart of light" has been read against Conrad's title; the typescript's epigraph was a passage from <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, which Pound talked Eliot out of in January 1922. Eliot used the phrase again in <i>Burnt Norton</i> (1935): "The surface glittered out of heart of light".

Two lives have been read into the garden. Lyndall Gordon, from <i>Eliot's Early Years</i> (1977) to <i>The Hyacinth Girl</i> (2022), attaches it to Emily Hale of Boston, to whom Eliot declared his love before sailing for Europe in 1914; his 1,131 letters to her, opened at Princeton on 2 January 2020, show how long the attachment lasted. John Peter (1952) and James E. Miller (1977) hear Jean Verdenal, the friend of Eliot's Paris year, killed at the Dardanelles in May 1915, to whom <i>Prufrock and Other Observations</i> is dedicated. In the <i>Criterion</i> for April 1934 Eliot recalled that friend crossing the Luxembourg Gardens "waving a branch of lilac", and his death in "the mud of Gallipoli".

### oed-und-leer

Verdict: stands

Checked: Wagner's libretto, Act III sc. 1 (murashev.com German libretto, Act 3), counting the sung lines from the shepherd's "Kurwenal! He!". Eliot's note in `poem-part1.md`.

Substance:
1. Eliot's "Id. III, verse 24" is exact: "Öd und leer das Meer!" is the twenty-fourth sung line of the act (stage directions and speakers' names not counted). The note calls the reference "brief"; it could say it is right.
2. The sequence is slightly compressed. Kurwenal asks "Sahst du noch nichts? Kein Schiff noch auf der See?"; the shepherd says Kurwenal would hear a merry tune if he had; Kurwenal tells him to keep watch and play "lustig und hell" if a ship appears; the shepherd turns, shades his eyes, looks out to sea and says the line. "Asked whether he sees anything, he answers with these four words" is close enough.
3. The scene: Tristan lies wounded at Kareol, his castle in Brittany; Kurwenal has sent to Cornwall for Isolde, "die Ärztin ... die einz'ge, die uns hilft" (the physician, the only one who can help us). "dying" and "waiting for Isolde's ship" are right.
4. Title: "Empty and desolate the sea" reverses the German. <i>Öd</i> is desolate or waste (as in <i>Ödland</i>, waste land), <i>leer</i> is empty; the translation line under the quote has them in the right order. Match the title to it. The word <i>öd</i> being the German for "waste" is a dictionary fact a reader might want; I know of no critic to attribute it to.
5. Quote: editions differ between "Öd' und leer" (with the elision mark, as in Eliot's "Oed'") and "Öd und leer". No change needed; the apostrophe form would sit closer to Eliot's text.
6. "(the ship setting out, love ahead) ... (the ship not coming, love behind)" is a reading. Isolde's ship does come in Act III; Tristan dies as she lands. Fine if marked, better cut.

Draft:
TITLE: Desolate and empty the sea
BODY: Act III of <i>Tristan</i>. Tristan lies wounded at Kareol, his castle in Brittany, and Kurwenal has sent to Cornwall for Isolde, the only one who can heal him. A shepherd keeps watch from the wall and is to play a merry tune the moment a sail appears; told to look, he looks out to sea and answers with these four words. Eliot's note, "Id. III, verse 24", is exact: the line is the twenty-fourth sung in the act. <i>Öd</i> is the German for desolate or waste (<i>Ödland</i>, waste land). Between the sailor's song of Act I at line 31 and this line from Act III, the poem sets the hyacinth garden.

### sosostris

Verdict: correct

Checked: <i>Crome Yellow</i> ch. XXV and XXVII (Gutenberg #1999). Grover Smith, "The Fortuneteller in Eliot's Waste Land", <i>American Literature</i> 25 (Jan. 1954), 490–92, and Eliot's letter to him of 10 March 1952, as reported in a 2019 article on Sosostris and Scogan (Garmian University journal; the PDF would not parse, so this rests on the search summary) and in Rainey. Rainey, <i>Revisiting The Waste Land</i> (2005), ch. 1, on the dating of the Parts I–II typescript (ebin.pub copy). Vagrancy Act 1824 s. 4 wording (Hansard, Lords, 3 May 1951); the 1921 ruling (Encyclopedia of Occultism and Parapsychology via encyclopedia.com). Sesostris in Herodotus II 102–10, from knowledge.

Substance:
1. "The name is borrowed" from <i>Crome Yellow</i>: the novel checks out (ch. XXVII: Mr Scogan "in a little canvas hut", "dressed in a black skirt and a red bodice" and a black wig, a placard announcing "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana", reading palms), but the borrowing is disputed on chronology. Eliot told Grover Smith in 1952 that he was "almost certain" he had taken the name from the novel without being aware of it. Lawrence Rainey, from the paper and typewriter of the typescript, dates Parts I and II to 9–22 May 1921, and Lyndall Gordon had already pointed out that Huxley finished the novel only in August 1921 (published November). If Rainey is right the name cannot come from the book unless the line was added later. The brief's own "usually traced to" is the right verb; add the caveat in a sentence.
2. "at a village fair": it is "Crome's yearly Charity Fair", a Bank Holiday fair in the park of the house, in aid of the local hospital (ch. XXV).
3. "Sesostris was a legendary Egyptian pharaoh": fine (Herodotus II 102–10).
4. "she is also its first prophet": the Sibyl of the epigraph comes first. And "everything she deals turns out to be true" is a reading. What Eliot's note says is the fact to give: the Sailor and the Merchant "appear later", so do the crowds, and "Death by Water is executed in Part IV". Not everything is dealt true: she does not find the Hanged Man, and the blank card is never read.
5. "prosecutions of palmists and mediums were routine in the London of the 1920s": unsourced overstatement. What can be shown: s. 4 of the Vagrancy Act 1824 made "every person pretending or professing to tell fortunes, or using any subtle craft, means, or device, by palmistry or otherwise, to deceive and impose on any of his Majesty's subjects" a rogue and vagabond; prosecutions of palmists and mediums under it continued through the 1920s (a 1921 appeal held that "the act of fortune-telling is an offence in itself"), and in 1951 the Lords were told such prosecutions "have been confined to palmists, fortune-tellers, and the like". "still prosecuted" is safe; "routine" is not.
6. "She is not being philosophical. She is afraid of the police." The Vagrancy Act gloss is the usual one in the commentaries, but it is a reading of the line, and should be marked as one.
7. "'Clairvoyante,' with the French feminine ending, is the trade term": plausible, unsourced; harmless.

Draft:
TITLE: Madame Sosostris
BODY: The name is usually traced to Aldous Huxley's <i>Crome Yellow</i> (November 1921), where Mr Scogan, dressed as a gipsy woman, reads palms at the Bank Holiday charity fair in the grounds of Crome under a placard reading "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana". Sesostris was a legendary Egyptian king. Eliot, asked by Grover Smith in 1952, said he was "almost certain" he had taken the name from the novel without knowing it; Lawrence Rainey has since dated the typescript of Parts I and II to May 1921, before Huxley wrote the book, so the debt is in doubt. The spelling and the head cold are Eliot's own. His note ties her cards to what follows: the Sailor and the Merchant "appear later", so do the crowds, and "Death by Water is executed in Part IV".

"One must be so careful these days" is usually read as a fortune-teller's caution. Telling fortunes for money was an offence under section 4 of the Vagrancy Act 1824 ("every person pretending or professing to tell fortunes, or using any subtle craft, means, or device, by palmistry or otherwise, to deceive"), and palmists and mediums were still being prosecuted under it in the 1920s.

### tarot

Verdict: correct

Checked: Eliot's note 46 in `poem-part1.md`; his notes 218, 126 and 192 in the 1922 text (Gutenberg #1321). Line numbers 209, 320 and 369 by count against the Gutenberg text. Waite, <i>The Pictorial Key to the Tarot</i> (1911), Three of Wands and Hanged Man (archive.org scan). Rider–Waite publication (Wikipedia). Frazer, <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i> vol. I, Book II ch. V "The Hanged God", pp. 288–97 (archive.org, contents summary). Weston, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, ch. VI "The Symbols" (Gutenberg #4090).

Substance:
1. Misquotation. Eliot's note reads "from which I have obviously departed to suit my own convenience", not "departed from it to suit my own convenience". Use his words.
2. "He is Phlebas ... and the merchant of Part III, and Ferdinand": Eliot's note on 218 is hedged, and the hedges are the point: the merchant "melts into the Phoenician Sailor, and the latter is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples". Keep them.
3. "Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i> stands behind the title": a reading, though the usual one; "usually taken to echo". "'The lady of situations' sounds like a phrase from the gossip columns" is a guess with no source; cut or mark.
4. Three of Wands: right. Waite's own description, quotable: "A calm, stately personage, with his back turned, looking from a cliff's edge at ships passing over the sea. Three staves are planted in the ground." Eliot's "an authentic member of the Tarot pack" and "quite arbitrarily" are quoted correctly.
5. The Wheel of Fortune is X; line 320 is right in the site's count.
6. Mr. Eugenides at 209 is right. "seen in profile" is one reading of "one-eyed" (as court cards are shown), not a fact; mark it.
7. Hanged Man: the card is right. Waite: "The gallows from which he is suspended forms a Tau cross ... There is a nimbus about the head ... the tree of sacrifice is living wood, with leaves thereon ... the face expresses deep entrancement, not suffering." But "Frazer's hanged god, the sacrificed king whose death brings the rain" misdescribes Frazer. His chapter "The Hanged God" (Book II ch. V of <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>) is about Attis and his doubles: Marsyas hanged on a pine, Odin on the gallows-tree, the hanged Artemis, human and animal victims hanged, skins used "to effect the resurrection". Rain is not in it; the rain is the poem's, in Part V. Fix.
8. "'Fear death by water' ... is the one thing the Grail legend says you should not fear: water is what the waste land needs." The Grail legend says no such thing. Two facts can replace it: Eliot's note, "Death by Water is executed in Part IV"; and Weston's claim, in her chapter on the Grail symbols, that the Tarot's original use "would seem to have been, not to foretell the Future in general, but to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land".
9. "No sacrifice, no resurrection, is in this reading": a reading (Cleanth Brooks's, in outline, 1939), fine if marked.
10. Rider & Son, London, 1909 is right (December 1909; Pamela Colman Smith drawing to A. E. Waite's instructions). "the Tarot that an English buyer of the 1910s would have found in a shop" is unsourced; "the English pack of Eliot's time" is safe. Whether Eliot ever handled one is not known; his note says his knowledge of the pack was slight.
11. Bold card names: the batch shows Markdown asterisks; the site's rule is plain text with a little HTML. The editor will know the convention; the draft below uses <b>.

Draft:
TITLE: The cards
BODY: Eliot's note says he was "not familiar with the exact constitution of the Tarot pack" and had "obviously departed" from it "to suit my own convenience". The cards she deals:

<b>The drowned Phoenician Sailor.</b> Not a Tarot card. Eliot's note on line 218 says the one-eyed merchant "melts into the Phoenician Sailor", who "is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples"; Part IV, "Death by Water", is Phlebas the Phoenician's.

<b>Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks.</b> "Beautiful lady" in Italian; also deadly nightshade, and the drops made from it that women used to widen the pupils. "The Lady of the Rocks" is usually taken to echo Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>.

<b>The man with three staves.</b> The Three of Wands, which Eliot calls "an authentic member of the Tarot pack". In the Rider pack a man stands at a cliff's edge with his back to us, watching ships. Eliot "quite arbitrarily" associated him with the Fisher King.

<b>The Wheel.</b> The Wheel of Fortune, card X. Compare "O you who turn the wheel", line 320.

<b>The one-eyed merchant.</b> Not a card. He returns as Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant, at line 209. One reading of "one-eyed" is that he is seen in profile, as court cards are.

<b>The Hanged Man.</b> Card XII. In the Rider pack a young man hangs by one foot from a T-shaped tree of living wood, a halo round his head, his face calm. Eliot's note associates him with "the Hanged God of Frazer" (the chapter of that name in <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, on Attis, Marsyas and Odin, gods hanged on trees) and with the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. Madame Sosostris does not find him. Her one instruction, "Fear death by water", is answered by Eliot's note: "Death by Water is executed in Part IV." Weston, in her chapter on the Grail symbols, says the Tarot was first used "to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land".

The cards shown here are Pamela Colman Smith's designs for the pack published in London by William Rider & Son in December 1909, the English pack of Eliot's time. By his own note, his knowledge of the pack was slight.

### pearls

Verdict: correct

Checked: <i>The Tempest</i> I. ii, Ariel's song (Gutenberg #23042). Line 125 by count against the 1922 text (Gutenberg #1321), and Eliot's notes numbered 126, 192, 218 and 257 there.

Substance:
1. Quote and scene are right; the song's wording matches the text.
2. "The father is not dead; the song is a lie that turns out to be a description of the play, in which everyone is transformed by water": the first clause is fact, the rest is a reading, and "everyone is transformed by water" is not so (no one in the play drowns). Mark or cut.

That is the most useful fact missing from this note and the hyacinth one.
4. Missing: two more passages come from the same scene. Ferdinand's "the king my father's wreck" is behind lines 191–2 (Eliot's note 192: "V. The Tempest, I, ii"), and "This music crept by me upon the waters" is quoted at 257 (note: "V. The Tempest, as above").
5. "works its 'sea-change' into Phlebas in Part IV, and never lets the transformation happen": a reading; Part IV does not use the phrase. Mark it as one.
6. Eliot's note on 218 is quoted correctly.

Draft:
TITLE: Ariel's song
BODY: <i>The Tempest</i>, I. ii. Ariel sings this to Ferdinand, who believes his father, the King of Naples, drowned in the wreck; the father is alive. The poem quotes the line again at 125, and Eliot's note there (numbered 126 in the 1922 notes) points back to lines 37 and 48, the hyacinth garden and this line. Two more passages come from the same scene: Ferdinand's "the king my father's wreck" behind lines 191–2, and "This music crept by me upon the waters" at 257, both with Eliot's notes. ### ring

Verdict: correct

Checked: line count against the 1922 text (Gutenberg #1321): the crowd on London Bridge is line 62, "Unreal City" is 60; "Who are those hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" is 369–70 in the site's count (Eliot's 368–69). <i>Inferno</i> III 52–57 (it.wikisource). Frazer, <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1922 abridgement), ch. X, the Swedish midsummer May-pole (Gutenberg #3623). Eliot's note 46 in `poem-part1.md`.

Substance:
1. "it comes true four lines later on London Bridge": wrong count. The crowd flows over London Bridge at line 62, six lines on; four lines on is "Unreal City" (60). Say "at line 62".
2. "Dante's neutrals in <i>Inferno</i> III run forever after a whirling banner": right ("una 'nsegna / che girando correva tanto ratta", III 52–53), and Eliot's note on 63 cites III 55–57. The link to line 56 is a reading, though a natural one.
3. "Frazer's villagers dance in rings around the maypole": the passage exists (ch. X, the Swedish midsummer May-pole: "the people flock to it from all quarters, and dance round it in a great ring"), but its bearing on this line is the note's own association, not Frazer's or Eliot's. Mark it as a reading or cut.
4. "the commuters go round and round between the station and the office": a reading with nothing behind it. Cut.
5. Eliot's note is quoted correctly ("also the 'crowds of people,'"); 62 and 369 are right in the site's count. "at line 369" for a two-line quotation should be "lines 369–70".

Draft:
TITLE: Crowds in a ring
BODY: Not a card. Eliot's note lists "the 'crowds of people'" with the Sailor and the Merchant among the figures that "appear later". They do, at line 62, the crowd flowing over London Bridge, and again as the "hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" at lines 369–70. The ring has been read against Dante's neutrals in <i>Inferno</i> III, who run forever after a whirling banner; Eliot's note on line 63 cites the same canto.

### summary

Nothing in the batch is badly wrong; the facts to fix are these. (1) tarot: Eliot's note is misquoted ("obviously departed to suit my own convenience" is his wording); Frazer's "Hanged God" chapter is about Attis, Marsyas and Odin hanged on trees, not "the sacrificed king whose death brings the rain"; and "the one thing the Grail legend says you should not fear" is not a thing the Grail legend says (Weston's actual claim, that the Tarot was first used "to predict the rise and fall of the waters", and Eliot's "Death by Water is executed in Part IV" can replace it). (2) hyacinth-girl: the 1934 Criterion sentence is quoted in full against the rights rule (cut to "waving a branch of lilac", "the mud of Gallipoli"); Frazer only reports the dying-god reading of Hyacinth, in the Attis book of the volumes Eliot cites; the Conrad inversion and "mystical blank" are readings, and the facts behind them (the discarded Conrad epigraph; "heart of light" in Burnt Norton) are better; name Peter (1952) and Miller (1977); add Inferno XXXIV 25 for "neither / Living nor dead", without claiming Eliot quoted it in 1929 (he did not). (3) sosostris: the Crome Yellow debt is disputed on chronology (Rainey dates the Parts I–II typescript to May 1921; Huxley wrote the novel June–August); Eliot's own 1952 "almost certain" is the evidence for it; "first prophet" overlooks the Sibyl; "routine" prosecutions is unsourced, "still prosecuted" is not. (4) ring: "four lines later" is six. (5) pearls: add Eliot's note 126 (for line 125), "Cf. Part I, l. 37, 48", which ties the pearls to the hyacinth garden, and the other Tempest passages at 191–2 and 257. (6) frisch-weht and oed-und-leer stand on substance; Eliot's line references to the libretto are exact in both, "reply" at line 42 is the site's reading, and the oed title reverses the German word order.
