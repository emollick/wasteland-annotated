Answer to the bracketed question: the policeman stops the speaker in the street — the draft's words for the charge are "committing a nuisance" (Wilson glosses it: "apparently for urinating in the street") — and a man the speaker knows gets him off; they go off in a cab, whose other occupants get out to run a hundred yards on a bet, and the speaker gets out to see the sunrise and walks home. Sources: Wilson, NYRB, 18 November 1971 ("Leaving the brothel, he is picked up by 'a fly cop,' ... but he is rescued by a man he knows, with whom he goes off in a cab"), and the draft lines as quoted there and in the 2025 post ("a fly cop came along, / Looking for trouble; committing a nuisance, he said"; "So I got out to see the sunrise, and walked home"). "Committing a nuisance" is two words of the facsimile, within the rights rule.

The same sentence has two small slips the answer can fix at a stroke: the call at Myrtle's is the speaker's alone, not the party's (the draft is first person singular there: he turns up at two in the morning and is told he is too drunk; Wilson's summary agrees), and the night does not end "with a cab home" — he leaves the cab and walks. Replace the sentence "They tell of a night out in Boston, in the voice of one of a party of young men: they drink at "Tom's place," see a show, and call at "Myrtle's place" after two in the morning, where the speaker is not welcome; a policeman appears, and the night ends with a cab home at sunrise." with:
"They tell of a night out in Boston, in the voice of one of a party of young men: drinks at "Tom's place," a show, then a call alone at "Myrtle's place" at two in the morning, where he is told he is too drunk; a policeman picks him up for "committing a nuisance," a man he knows gets him off, and he leaves their cab at sunrise to walk home."
