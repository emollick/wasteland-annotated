# Batch 4b, scholar, round 1: Library notes, Tennyson to Eliot's 1956 lecture

### source tennyson
Verdict: correct

Checked: <i>Maud</i> Part II, section V, 1 (Gutenberg, <i>Maud, and Other Poems</i>): the seven lines as quoted. <i>The Princess</i>, Part IV (Gutenberg #791): "O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South" is sung by the Prince ("one myself had made"); it was in the first edition of 1847 (the songs between the parts were added in 1850). Eliot, "In Memoriam" (1936): "the finest ear of any English poet since Milton" (text at uwyo.edu). Conrad, "Youth" (Gutenberg #525): "the heat of life in the handful of dust". Site HTML: line 30, line 115 ("rats' alley"), line 429 ("Quando fiam ceu chelidon—O swallow swallow"; Eliot's 428).

Substance:
1. "And my heart is a handful of dust" presented as Eliot's source. Eliot's notes give none for line 30, and the attribution is the annotators', one parallel among several: Conrad's "Youth" (1898; printed with <i>Heart of Darkness</i> in the 1902 volume) has "the heat of life in the handful of dust", and the phrase is proverbial. Say "annotators cite", not "gives".
2. "the swallow song in <i>The Princess</i> gives Eliot the English half of a Latin line": same point. Eliot's note on 429 cites only the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i>; Tennyson is the usual source given (Southam), and Swinburne's "Itylus" (1866), which addresses "sister swallow", is sometimes cited too.
3. "who imagines himself buried alive under a city street": in II.v the speaker, in a madhouse (Tennyson's own gloss), imagines himself dead and buried in a shallow grave a yard under the street, hearing the wheels and hoofs above. "Dead and buried, still hearing the traffic" is what the text says; "buried alive" is the annotator's summary. "A Victorian ancestor of the man in rats' alley" (line 115) is a reading; mark it as one or drop it.
4. "'the finest ear of any English poet since Milton,' he wrote in 1936": exact. Name the essay ("In Memoriam", 1936, the introduction to a Tennyson selection, reprinted in <i>Essays Ancient and Modern</i>); one phrase from a post-1929 text is within the rule.
5. Dates 1855 and 1847 are right; the swallow song was in the 1847 text, which is worth a word since the best-known songs of <i>The Princess</i> were added in 1850. Passage: both quotations exact. Line 429 is right in the site's count.

Draft:
TITLE: Maud, and The Princess
BODY: "A handful of dust" is in <i>Maud</i> (1855), Part II, section V, where the speaker, in a madhouse, imagines himself dead and buried a yard beneath a city street with the traffic going over him. Eliot's notes give no source for line 30; annotators cite <i>Maud</i> among other parallels, and Conrad's "Youth" (1898), printed with <i>Heart of Darkness</i> in the 1902 volume, has "the heat of life in the handful of dust". "O swallow swallow" at line 429 is usually traced to the song the Prince sings in Part IV of <i>The Princess</i> (1847), "O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South"; Eliot's note gives only the Latin source, the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i>. In his essay "In Memoriam" (1936) Eliot said Tennyson "had the finest ear of any English poet since Milton".

### source whitman-lilacs
Verdict: correct

Checked: <i>Leaves of Grass</i>, 1891–92 text (Gutenberg #1322): sections 1, 4 and 16 of "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" as quoted ("Lilac and star and bird twined with the chant of my soul", 16); Lincoln is not named in the poem. Eliot, introduction to Pound's <i>Selected Poems</i> (1928): "I did not read Whitman until much later in life, and had to conquer an aversion to his form, as well as to much of his matter". Eliot, "Whitman and Tennyson", <i>Nation and Athenaeum</i>, 18 December 1926 (a review of Emory Holloway's biography): "When Whitman speaks of the lilacs or of the mocking-bird, his theories and beliefs drop away like a needless pretext" (wording from secondary quotation, Whitman Archive and Project MUSE). Eliot's note 357; site line 357 is the hermit-thrush line, as the header says.

Substance:
1. "He rarely admitted to reading Whitman. The lilacs and the thrush say otherwise." Misleading as it stands. What is on record: in 1928 Eliot wrote that he "did not read Whitman until much later in life" and had had to overcome "an aversion to his form"; but in 1926, four years after the poem, he had already written of "the lilacs" and the mocking-bird as the places where Whitman's theories fall away. Neither text says whether he knew "Lilacs" before 1922. The parallel between the two poems is the annotators'; Eliot's own note on the thrush cites Chapman's bird handbook and his own hearing of the bird in Quebec County, not Whitman. Give the two statements and mark the parallel as a reading.
2. "Whitman's elegy for Lincoln, shot in April 1865": right (14 April; he died the next day). Worth adding that the poem never names him, and that it appeared that autumn in <i>Sequel to Drum-Taps</i>.
3. "three hundred and fifty lines later": 355 (line 2 to line 357). Give the line number.
4. "longs for the hermit-thrush's 'water-dripping song'": the phrase is Eliot's note's, not the poem's ("Its 'water-dripping song' is justly celebrated"). Say so.
5. "binds three things together: the lilac, the evening star, and the hermit thrush": right; "the great star early droop'd in the western sky" is Venus as evening star. Passage: exact (the ellipsis after "Death's outlet song of life" drops Whitman's parenthesis).

Draft:
TITLE: When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd
BODY: Whitman's elegy for Lincoln, shot on 14 April 1865, printed that autumn in <i>Sequel to Drum-Taps</i>; Lincoln is not named in it. Its three recurring images are the lilac, the evening star and the hermit thrush singing in the swamp: "Lilac and star and bird twined with the chant of my soul" (section 16). <i>The Waste Land</i> opens on lilacs out of the dead land and at line 357 wishes for the hermit-thrush, whose "water-dripping song" Eliot's note praises, citing a bird handbook rather than Whitman; the parallel is the annotators'. Eliot wrote in 1928 that he "did not read Whitman until much later in life" (introduction to Pound's <i>Selected Poems</i>), but in 1926 he had already written that "when Whitman speaks of the lilacs or of the mocking-bird, his theories and beliefs drop away like a needless pretext" ("Whitman and Tennyson", <i>Nation and Athenaeum</i>, 18 December 1926).

### source webster-white-devil
Verdict: correct

Checked: <i>The White Devil</i> (Gutenberg #12915): the dirge in Act V scene iv, with the stage direction "Cornelia doth this in several forms of distraction"; "Flamineo runs Marcello through" in V.ii; V.vi: Vittoria and Zanche fire the pistols, Flamineo feigns dying, Zanche promises to "drive a stake / Through thy body" and to give out that he killed himself, then "[Flamineo riseth.] The pistols held no bullets; 'twas a plot / To prove your kindness to me", and the speech "O men, / That lie upon your death-beds..." follows. 1612 quarto title (Wikipedia): "The White Divel; or, The Tragedy of Paulo Giordano Ursini, Duke of Brachiano. With The Life and Death of Vittoria Corombona the famous Venetian Curtizan". Eliot's note 407 ("Cf. Webster, <i>The White Devil</i>, V, vi"), which the site attaches to line 408. "Whispers of Immortality" (<i>Poems</i>, 1920): couplet exact.

Substance:
1. "having just learned that his sister would have let him die": understates what happens. Flamineo hands Vittoria and Zanche pistols he has secretly left unloaded, proposes that all three die, and has them shoot him first; they fire, and while he feigns dying they gloat over him (Zanche: she will drive a stake through his body and say he killed himself). He rises, "The pistols held no bullets; 'twas a plot / To prove your kindness to me", and the widow speech is his answer. They shot him, as they thought; they did not merely let him die.
2. "Cornelia sings the dirge over her son Marcello, killed by his brother Flamineo": right (V.ii). "Vittoria Corombona, the 'white devil,' and her murderous lover Brachiano": right; the 1612 title page names both.
3. "explain why these two speeches were in his head": the essays and the poem show that Eliot knew Webster well; they do not explain a choice. A reading; "show how well he knew Webster" is what the evidence bears. Note that Eliot's essays on the dramatists before 1922 are on Marlowe (1919), Jonson and Massinger (1920); his piece on Webster is a review of 1928.
4. Passage: a modern edited text, correct in wording. The stage direction is worth adding to the (V.iv) tag.

Draft:
TITLE: The White Devil
BODY: Webster's tragedy (1612) of Vittoria Corombona, the "white devil" of the title, and her lover Brachiano. Cornelia's dirge is sung, the stage direction says, "in several forms of distraction", over her son Marcello, whom his brother Flamineo has run through in the scene before. Flamineo's speech on widows comes in the last scene: he has given Vittoria and Zanche pistols, secretly unloaded, and had them shoot him first; they fire and gloat over him as he feigns dying; he rises ("The pistols held no bullets; 'twas a plot / To prove your kindness to me") and turns on them. Eliot quotes that passage in his note to line 408, and had written of Webster two years earlier in "Whispers of Immortality": "Webster was much possessed by death / And saw the skull beneath the skin".

### source shakespeare-tempest

4. "and never allows the change": a reading (the annotator's). Mark it or give it to someone. Phlebas's bones are picked by the current (lines 315–316); whether that is a sea-change refused is interpretation.

Draft:
TITLE: The Tempest, Act I scene ii
BODY: Ferdinand, cast ashore, believes his father Alonso drowned in the wreck; Ariel's song, "Full fathom five thy father lies", tells him so ("The ditty does remember my drown'd father"), and it is not true: Alonso is alive, and the play ends with father and son restored to each other. ### source frazer
Verdict: correct

Checked: <i>The Golden Bough</i>, 1922 abridgement (Gutenberg #3623): first sentence, "Who does not know Turner's picture of the Golden Bough? The scene ... is a dream-like vision of the little woodland lake of Nemi"; ch. XXXIII, "The Gardens of Adonis": "baskets or pots filled with earth, in which wheat, barley, lettuces, fennel ... were sown and tended for eight days ... carried out with the images of the dead Adonis, and flung with them into the sea or into springs"; the Osiris chapters: "the priests used to bury effigies of Osiris made of earth and corn ... the corn would be found to have sprouted from the body of Osiris". <i>Adonis Attis Osiris</i>, third edition, 2 vols, 1914 (archive.org scan of vol. I): "The Hanged God" is Book I chapter V, pp. 288–97 (Marsyas, Odin, Attis). Editions (Wikipedia): 2 vols 1890; 3 vols 1900; 12 vols 1906–15; abridgement 1922; <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i> separately 1906 and 1907, then as Part IV of the third edition, 1914; the crucifixion discussion of 1900 moved to an appendix in the third edition and dropped from the abridgement. Turner's <i>The Golden Bough</i>, exhibited RA 1834, Tate: Lake Avernus, the Sibyl with sickle and bough; Frazer used it as his frontispiece in 1890 and called it Nemi (Wikipedia, "The Golden Bough (painting)").

Substance:
1. "The book's title and its opening page come from Turner's painting": the opening page does; the title does not. It is Virgil's golden bough, the branch Aeneas must pluck before going down to the underworld (<i>Aeneid</i> VI), which Servius's commentary connected with the priesthood of Diana at Nemi; that connection is Frazer's starting point, and the painting is how he introduces it.
2. "which Frazer took for a view of Nemi; it shows the Sibyl at Lake Avernus": right, and worth keeping; the painting was Frazer's frontispiece in 1890.
3. "and, by implication, Christ": Frazer made the comparison explicit in the second edition (1900) and moved it to an appendix, "The Crucifixion of Christ", in the third; the 1922 abridgement leaves it out. Say that rather than "by implication".
4. "Stetson's planted corpse, Phlebas in the current, the Hanged Man and the whole question of whether April can bring anything back are Frazer's": a reading stated as fact. What Eliot's headnote claims is that a reader of Frazer "will immediately recognise in the poem certain references to vegetation ceremonies", and his note on the Tarot that the Hanged Man "is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer". "The Hanged God" is a chapter of the very volumes he names; give the chapter and let the rest stand as what readers have found.
5. "the 'gardens of Adonis,' pots of quick-sprouting seed thrown into the sea" and "effigies of Osiris 'made of earth and corn,' buried to sprout": both exact. "Twelve-volume": right for the third edition. The date line might add the first edition, 1890.

Draft:
TITLE: The Golden Bough (Adonis, Attis, Osiris)
BODY: Frazer's comparative study of magic and religion, two volumes in 1890 and twelve by its third edition (1906–15), argued that behind the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris lay one agricultural rite: a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by a sacrificed king or by an effigy buried or drowned. The second edition (1900) put the crucifixion into the same pattern; the third moved that discussion to an appendix, and the 1922 abridgement left it out. The two volumes Eliot's headnote names (he spells them "Atthis Adonis Osiris") describe the "gardens of Adonis", pots of quick-sprouting seed carried out with images of the dead god and flung into the sea, and the "effigies of Osiris made of earth and corn" buried at the festival of sowing so that the grain would sprout from the god's body; their chapter "The Hanged God" is what Eliot's note on the Tarot means by "the Hanged God of Frazer". Frazer's first page describes Turner's painting <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1834), which he took for a view of Nemi; it shows the Sibyl of Cumae, sickle and bough in hand, at Lake Avernus.

### source weston
Verdict: correct

Checked: <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (Gutenberg #4090): chapter list (I Introductory; II The Task of the Hero; III The Freeing of the Waters; IV Tammuz and Adonis; V Medieval and Modern Forms of Nature Ritual; VI The Symbols; VII The Sword Dance; VIII The Medicine Man; IX The Fisher King; X–XI The Secret of the Grail; XII Mithra and Attis; XIII The Perilous Chapel; XIV The Author). Ch. VI: Lance and Cup "are sex symbols of immemorial antiquity ..., the Lance, or Spear, representing the Male, the Cup, or Vase, the Female, reproductive energy", and "They exist to-day as the four suits of the Tarot". Ch. XII, after Cumont: the cults were carried across the Roman Empire by three channels, "Syrian merchants", Eastern slaves and officials, and "most important ... the soldiers, the foreign legions"; she then asks whether the cult "may have been known in Great Britain". Weston 1850–1928; the book 1920, Cambridge University Press (Wikipedia; the Gutenberg text has no imprint). Eliot's headnote; his Part V note, "the approach to the Chapel Perilous (see Miss Weston's book)"; note 424, "chapter on the Fisher King". Eliot, "The Frontiers of Criticism" (1956): "I regret having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail" (wording from secondary quotation). Later reception: rejected even by Roger Sherman Loomis (Wikipedia, citing Juliette Wood).

Substance:
1. "a ritual of initiation whose secrets travelled Europe with Syrian merchants": a simplification of chapter XII, where Weston, following Cumont, names three carriers, merchants, slaves and, "most important", the legions, and only asks whether the cult reached Britain. "Syrian merchants" is presumably kept for the sake of Mr Eugenides; fair, but say "with Syrian merchants and the legions" or "among others".
2. "Almost no medievalist now accepts the argument. It does not matter." The first sentence is right (Loomis, once sympathetic, gave it up). The second is a judgement; the point of substance is that Eliot's use of the book does not depend on the thesis being true. Say that.
3. "thirty-four years later he said he regretted...": right; 1956, "The Frontiers of Criticism". Name the lecture. One phrase from a post-1929 text: within the rule.
4. "Read chapters II ..., IX ... and XIII ... and you have the poem's skeleton": the numbers and titles are right. Missing is chapter VI, "The Symbols", where the four Grail hallows become the four suits of the Tarot, which is what Eliot's note on line 46 presupposes; and chapter III, "The Freeing of the Waters", is the drought. "A very good bad book" is a judgement.
5. "Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance", "under the influence of Frazer", "a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics": all right (chapter VI; chapter XIV on the Crusades' identification of the symbols with the Passion relics).

Draft:
TITLE: From Ritual to Romance
BODY: Weston (1850–1928), a scholar and translator of Arthurian romance, argued, following Frazer, that the Grail stories preserved an ancient fertility ritual: a wounded king, a land gone waste, a lance and a cup that were "sex symbols" before they were relics of the Passion (chapter VI), and a rite of initiation carried across the Roman Empire by Syrian merchants, Eastern slaves and the legions (chapter XII). Medievalists have not accepted the argument; Roger Sherman Loomis, once sympathetic, rejected it. That does not touch Eliot's use of the book. His headnote says it suggested "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism"; his notes send the reader to her chapters on the Fisher King (IX) and the Perilous Chapel (XIII), and her chapter on the symbols (VI) is where the Grail hallows become the suits of the Tarot. In 1956, in "The Frontiers of Criticism", he said he regretted "having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail".

### source petronius
Verdict: correct

Checked: <i>Satyricon</i> 48 (Latin Library): the sentence as quoted, with the Greek. Context: Trimalchio has asked the rhetorician Agamemnon what he declaimed on today, boasted of his own libraries, and asked whether Agamemnon knows the twelve labours of Hercules "or the story of Ulysses, how the Cyclops wrenched off his thumb with pincers; I used to read these things in Homer as a boy" ("Solebam haec ego puer apud Homerum legere"); the Sibyl follows at once. Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV.130–53 (Latin Library): "ostendens cumulum, quot haberet corpora pulvis, / tot mihi natales contingere vana rogavi; / excidit, ut peterem iuvenes quoque protinus annos" (136–39); "voce tamen noscar; vocem mihi fata relinquent" (153).

Substance:
1. "asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand": Ovid's Sibyl points to a heap ("cumulum") of sand and asks for as many birthdays as it has grains; there is no hand. "In her hand" makes Ovid echo line 30. Correct to "a heap of sand".
2. "shrank with age until only her voice was left": in Ovid she is seven hundred years old and foretells this, three hundred years more, then no body, only a voice. Future, not past.
3. "a fat man's boast": Petronius gives Trimalchio a bald head, rings, a scarlet cloak and a napkin at his neck (27, 32); he does not call him fat. The rest of the sentence is right: the anecdote caps a display of garbled learning ("showing off between courses" is exact; the pig has just gone out to the kitchen).
4. "whose dinner party is the surviving centre of the <i>Satyricon</i>": right; the Cena (chapters 26–78) is the longest continuous stretch to survive.
5. Missing: that the epigraph took the place of the Conrad quotation early in 1922 (see conrad-hod). Translation exact; "jar" for "ampulla" (a flask or bottle) is the conventional rendering.

Draft:
TITLE: Satyricon, chapter 48
BODY: The speaker is Trimalchio, the freed slave grown rich whose dinner party is the longest surviving stretch of the <i>Satyricon</i>. He has just asked the rhetorician Agamemnon about his declamation and offered his own learning in return, garbling Homer as he goes; the Sibyl is his clincher, something he claims to have seen with his own eyes. In Ovid (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV) the Sibyl of Cumae tells Aeneas that Apollo offered her whatever she wished: she pointed to a heap of sand and asked for as many years as it had grains, forgot to ask for youth, and will dwindle until nothing is left of her but a voice. Eliot put the passage in place of the Conrad epigraph early in 1922.

### source eliot-frontiers
Verdict: correct

Checked: "The Frontiers of Criticism", the Gideon Seymour Memorial Lecture, Williams Arena, University of Minnesota, 30 April 1956 (pamphlet, University of Minnesota Press, 1956; <i>Sewanee Review</i>, 1956; <i>On Poetry and Poets</i>, 1957). Wording verified in secondary quotation only (archive.org copies are lending-only): "with a view to spiking the guns of critics of my earlier poems who had accused me of plagiarism"; "it was discovered that the poem was inconveniently short, so I set to work to expand the notes"; "the remarkable exposition of bogus scholarship that is still on view to-day"; "now they can never be unstuck"; "anyone who bought my book of poems, and found that the notes to <i>The Waste Land</i> were not in it, would demand his money back"; "I regret having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail". Audience: 14,000 (Wikipedia); 13,523 in some accounts. Thirty-four years: right.

Substance:
1. "readers had 'bought' the notes with the poem": the quotation marks mark a word Eliot did not use in that sense. What he said is that the notes "can never be unstuck", because anyone who bought his collected poems and found them missing "would demand his money back". Rephrase without the false quotation.
2. "he had meant only to list his references, to spike the guns of critics who had called him a plagiarist": right ("critics of my earlier poems who had accused me of plagiarism"). "when the poem proved too short to make a book on its own, expanded them to fill pages": right ("inconveniently short"). "Padding that had got out of hand": a fair gloss on "bogus scholarship".
3. Missing, from the same lecture: the poem had no notes in its first two printings, the <i>Criterion</i> (October 1922) and the <i>Dial</i> (November 1922); they came with the Boni and Liveright book in December. A site that reproduces the notes should say so here.
4. "Take the notes as part of the poem and as a joke at its expense; both are true": an instruction and a judgement. What can be said: this is Eliot's account thirty-four years later, told for laughs, and the notes have been printed with the poem in every edition since 1922.
5. "nearly fourteen thousand": the usual figure. Passage: two phrases from a post-1929 text, within the rule; "to-day" is Faber's spelling.

Draft:
TITLE: The Frontiers of Criticism (Eliot on his own notes)
BODY: A lecture given at the University of Minnesota on 30 April 1956, in a sports arena, to an audience of about fourteen thousand. Eliot said that the poem had had no notes in the <i>Criterion</i> or the <i>Dial</i>; that he had first meant only to list his references, to spike the guns of critics who had accused his earlier poems of plagiarism; that when the poem was found "inconveniently short" for a book he expanded them to fill pages; and that they could not now be removed, since anyone who bought his poems and found the notes missing would want his money back. This is Eliot's account thirty-four years after the event, and it is told for laughs; the notes have been printed with the poem in every edition since 1922.

### summary
1. whitman-lilacs: "He rarely admitted to reading Whitman. The lilacs and the thrush say otherwise" misstates the record: Eliot's 1928 disclaimer ("did not read Whitman until much later in life") is real, but in 1926 he had already praised Whitman's "lilacs" in print (<i>Nation and Athenaeum</i>, 18 December 1926); the parallel is the annotators', not Eliot's note, which cites a bird handbook.
2. tennyson: <i>Maud</i> and <i>The Princess</i> are annotators' parallels, not Eliot's sources (his notes give the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i> only); Conrad's "Youth" has "the heat of life in the handful of dust". "Buried alive" is loose. The 1936 quotation is exact and should name the essay.
3. webster: Vittoria and Zanche shot Flamineo, as they thought, and gloated; "would have let him die" understates V.vi. The rest is right.
4. shakespeare-tempest: "gasworks" should be "gashouse" (line 190); the Fisher King at line 189 and "everyone has suffered a sea-change" are readings, the second overstated; Ferdinand's speech is altered at 191–192, quoted at 257.
5. frazer: the title is Virgil's, not Turner's (the painting supplies the first page, and Frazer did take Avernus for Nemi); the crucifixion was explicit in the 1900 edition, not "by implication"; "The Hanged God" is a chapter of the volumes Eliot names.
6. weston: "Syrian merchants" simplifies chapter XII (merchants, slaves, and "most important" the legions); chapter VI, the Tarot suits, is the chapter Eliot's note on line 46 presupposes.
7. petronius: Ovid's Sibyl points to a heap of sand, not sand "in her hand"; she foretells her shrinking; Petronius never calls Trimalchio fat.
8. dante-purg26: add that the Ovid Press title page reads "Ara Vus Prec", that "Il Miglior Fabbro" was already Pound's chapter title in <i>The Spirit of Romance</i> (1910), and that the dedication was added after 1922.
9. dickens-omf: the heading is typed over Parts I and II of the typescript, not "written across" it; "including its author" and "the poem's river in prose" should go.
10. conrad-hod: date and source the Pound–Eliot exchange (January 1922; the facsimile and <i>Letters</i> vol. 1), say Pound gave way and Eliot made the change, and mark "heart of light" as a reading; "Mistah Kurtz—he dead" (1925) is worth a clause.
11. eliot-frontiers: "'bought'" is a false quotation; add that the notes were absent from the <i>Criterion</i> and <i>Dial</i> printings; cut the closing instruction.
