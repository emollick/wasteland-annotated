# Batch 2, reader, round 1 (glosses on lines 31–59)

Drafts below use <i>…</i> for titles as the common brief asks; quotation marks are straight, matching the notes as they stand in the batch file. I have not changed any date, name or claim; where a draft adds a fact (a king's name, a festival's dedicatee, a later poem) I have flagged it for the scholar.

### frisch-weht

Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "the four short lines sit in the poem like a snatch of song heard through a wall" — a metaphor about how the lines feel, not an explanation of them. It tells me how to hear the poem, which I can do for myself, and gives me no fact.
2. "their subject, love that has not happened yet and will end in death, opens the hyacinth garden that follows" — I read this twice. The appositive is long, and a subject "opening" a garden is a stretch. The information underneath (where the song falls in the opera's story; that the garden comes next in the poem) is worth keeping in plainer words.
3. "The reply comes at line 42." — a short quotable closer. Line 42 is not a reply in the opera; it is a second quotation from a different act. "Reply" is the writer's pairing dressed as a fact. A plain cross-reference does the job.
4. "*Tristan und Isolde* opens with this song" — for the scholar: the opera opens with an orchestral prelude; the song opens Act I, and these are verses 5 to 8 of it, which is what Eliot's note says. "Act I opens with this song" would be exact. I have written it that way in the draft; the scholar should confirm.
5. "a king she does not love" — the king has a name, Mark of Cornwall, and a reader would rather have it. Scholar to confirm before it goes in.

Keep: "sung by a young sailor high in the rigging, unseen, as Tristan's ship carries Isolde from Ireland to Cornwall to marry a king she does not love. Isolde hears it as mockery. The wind blows toward the sailor's home and away from hers. Eliot's note gives the reference and nothing else". The title, and the quote and translation, are fine as they are.

Draft:
TITLE: The sailor's song from the masthead
BODY: Act I of Wagner's <i>Tristan und Isolde</i> opens with this song. A young sailor sings it from high in the rigging, unseen, as Tristan's ship carries Isolde from Ireland to Cornwall to marry King Mark, whom she does not love. Isolde hears it as mockery. The wind blows toward the sailor's home and away from hers. Eliot's note gives the reference, verses 5 to 8 of Act I, and nothing else. At this point in the opera Tristan and Isolde are not yet lovers; by its end both are dead. Eliot quotes the opera once more at line 42, from its last act, after the hyacinth garden.

### hyacinth-girl

Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: yes

Problems:
1. "the letters of a cry of grief" — coy. Ovid says which letters (AI AI). Tell me.
2. "All of that is available, and none of it explains the passage" — the writer lays out the learning and then waves it away, which is a performance. Either the myth bears on the lines or it does not; say which, plainly.
3. "which is one of the few places in the poem where a speaker admits to having felt something" — a confident claim about the whole poem, and it tells me what to make of the passage before I have read it. Whose observation is this? If it stays, mark it as a reading.
4. The four lines of the poem quoted in full ("I could not / Speak … the silence.") — the lines are beside the note already. Quoting them back to me is padding.
5. "The heart of light is Conrad's heart of darkness turned inside out" — "turned inside out" is a flourish. The fact underneath (the phrase inverts Conrad's title) is useful and can be said plainly.
6. "the mystical blank that Eliot's later poems circle" — the sentence that lost me. What is a mystical blank, and which poems circle it? If the point is that Eliot reused the phrase, name the poem: "Burnt Norton" has "the heart of light" in its opening section. Scholar to confirm.
7. "Two lives have been read into the garden" — arch. "Two people in Eliot's life have been proposed" says it.
8. "Others hear Jean Verdenal" — "hear" is precious, and "Others" is nobody. Gordon gets a name; whoever first proposed Verdenal should get one too. Scholar to supply.
9. The 1934 quotation ("a friend coming across the Luxembourg Gardens … the mud of Gallipoli") runs to about thirty-five words. The common brief says the 1934 Criterion commentary may be quoted only in a phrase or two. Paraphrase and keep one phrase.
10. "Lilacs, line 2." — a fragment I had to decode (the lilac in the memory, the lilacs in line 2). A plain cross-reference does the same work.
11. "The poem does not choose." — the bow at the end, and the exact sentence the common brief gives as an example of what has to go. The point (the text names no one) belongs at the start of the paragraph as a fact, not at the end as a line.
12. For the scholar: the original says "the hyacinth had its own festival"; the Hyacinthia honoured Hyacinthus, so I have written "a festival in his name". Confirm.

Keep: "Hyacinthus was the boy Apollo loved and killed by accident with a discus; the flower grew from his blood"; "Hyacinth is, in Frazer's reading, another dying and reviving god"; the whole Emily Hale sentence ("Lyndall Gordon's biographies attach it to Emily Hale … show how long that attachment lasted"); "the friend of Eliot's Paris year, killed at the Dardanelles in 1915, to whom *Prufrock and Other Observations* is dedicated". The title.

Draft:
TITLE: The hyacinth garden
BODY: Hyacinthus was the boy Apollo loved and killed by accident with a discus. The flower grew from his blood, and in Ovid its petals carry the letters AI AI, a cry of grief (<i>Metamorphoses</i> X). Sparta held a festival in his name, the Hyacinthia, and in Frazer's reading he is another of the dying and reviving gods. The passage itself takes nothing from the myth but the name. Its last phrase, "the heart of light", inverts the title of Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>; Eliot used it again in "Burnt Norton".

Two people in Eliot's life have been proposed for the figure in the garden, and the text names neither. Lyndall Gordon's biographies attach it to Emily Hale, the Bostonian Eliot loved before he sailed for Europe in 1914; his letters to her, unsealed at Princeton in 2020, show how long that attachment lasted. Other readers have proposed Jean Verdenal, the friend of Eliot's Paris year, killed at the Dardanelles in 1915, to whom <i>Prufrock and Other Observations</i> is dedicated. In a note in the <i>Criterion</i> in 1934 Eliot recalled a friend crossing the Luxembourg Gardens in the late afternoon "waving a branch of lilac", a friend who was afterwards killed at Gallipoli. Compare the lilacs of line 2.

(If the editor wants to keep the point that the passage records a felt experience, it can go in as a marked reading after the Ovid and Frazer sentences. I would leave it out. "Other readers have proposed" should become a name once the scholar supplies one.)

### oed-und-leer

Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "Act III of *Tristan*." — a fragment standing in for a sentence.
2. "Between line 31 (the ship setting out, love ahead) and line 42 (the ship not coming, love behind), the hyacinth garden happens." — the two bracketed pairs are a matched antithesis built for rhythm, and "the hyacinth garden happens" is arch. The point underneath is good and worth keeping: the two quotations stand either side of the garden, one from the start of the story and one from its end.
3. "Eliot's note, "Id. III, verse 24," is as brief as the shepherd." — a joke to close on. What I wanted from that sentence was to be told what "Id." means.
4. Title: "Empty and desolate the sea" reverses the order of the translation under the quote ("Desolate and empty the sea!"). One or the other. A title that says what the note is about ("The shepherd's answer") would match the other echo titles in this batch ("The sailor's song from the masthead", "Ariel's song").
5. For the scholar, not me: the quote reads "Öd und leer das Meer!"; some printings of the libretto have "Öd' und leer". Whichever matches the source the library cites.

Keep: "Tristan lies dying in Brittany, waiting for Isolde's ship. A shepherd keeps watch and is to play a merry tune the moment a sail appears. Asked whether he sees anything, he answers with these four words."

Draft:
TITLE: The shepherd's answer
BODY: The words are from Act III of <i>Tristan und Isolde</i>. Tristan lies dying in Brittany, waiting for Isolde's ship. A shepherd keeps watch and is to play a merry tune the moment a sail appears. Asked whether he sees anything, he answers with these four words. This quotation and the one at line 31 stand on either side of the hyacinth garden. That one comes from the opening of the opera, before Tristan and Isolde have fallen in love; this one from its last act, when they are apart and Tristan is dying. Eliot's note, "Id. III, verse 24," points back to the note on line 31: "Id." is short for <i>idem</i>, the same work.

### sosostris

Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: yes

Problems:
1. "The name is borrowed and bent." — a one-line opener that sounds good and tells me nothing until the next sentence does the work. The common brief quotes it as an example of what has to go.
2. "Eliot feminises the fake and gives her a head cold." — the brief's second example. Alliteration and a joke in place of the fact (the spelling and the cold are Eliot's own).
3. "the cynic Mr. Scogan" — harmless, but a label I do not need to follow the note.
4. "She is the poem's most fully drawn comic character, and she is also its first prophet, since everything she deals turns out to be true." — this is criticism, not a gloss: a ranking ("most fully drawn"), a title ("first prophet"), and a claim I am asked to take on trust ("everything … turns out to be true"). The useful fact behind it is that the cards reappear, and Eliot's note says so.
5. "She is not being philosophical. She is afraid of the police." — the two-beat punchline. The Vagrancy Act sentence before it already makes the point; these two lines exist to be quoted.
6. The second paragraph glosses line 59 under a note anchored at 43–46. That is fine if the site has no separate note on lines 57–59, but the body should say which line it is glossing.

Keep: the Huxley sentence, minus "the cynic"; "Sesostris was a legendary Egyptian pharaoh."; ""Clairvoyante," with the French feminine ending, is the trade term; "the wisest woman in Europe" is the trade puff." (this one explains something and I would keep it as it is); "fortune-telling for money was an offence under the Vagrancy Act of 1824, and prosecutions of palmists and mediums were routine in the London of the 1920s". The title.

Draft:
TITLE: The clairvoyante with a cold
BODY: The name is usually traced to Aldous Huxley's novel <i>Crome Yellow</i>, published in November 1921 while Eliot was drafting the poem. In it Mr. Scogan dresses up at a village fair as "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana" to tell fortunes. Sesostris was a legendary Egyptian pharaoh; Eliot's spelling and the head cold are his own. "Clairvoyante," with the French feminine ending, is the trade term; "the wisest woman in Europe" is the trade puff. The cards she deals reappear later in the poem; Eliot's note on line 46 says which.

Her parting line, "One must be so careful these days" (line 59), can be read as a practical worry. Fortune-telling for money was an offence under the Vagrancy Act of 1824, and prosecutions of palmists and mediums were routine in London in the 1920s.

### tarot

Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "Eliot's own note confesses that he did not know the Tarot well and "departed from it to suit my own convenience."" — "confesses" colours it, and the words inside the quotation marks are not Eliot's. His note says he is "not familiar with the exact constitution of the Tarot pack of cards, from which I have obviously departed to suit my own convenience." A quotation should be exact.
2. "What she deals:" — a fragment as a lead-in. A sentence costs nothing.
3. The bold **headings** are Markdown. The common brief says no Markdown in the final wording; whatever the site's convention for run-in headings is, the editor should apply it here.
4. "Not a Tarot card." / "Not a card;" — fragments.
5. "He is Phlebas, who gets Part IV to himself" — "gets Part IV to himself" is chatty. And "He is Phlebas … and the merchant … and Ferdinand" is stated as bare fact when the linkage is Eliot's own, in his notes on lines 46 and 218. Say so; it is a better sentence with the source in it.
6. "whose father is the one lying "full fathom five" with pearls for eyes" — clumsy ("is the one lying"), and the father is not in fact lying anywhere. There is a separate note on line 48 for this; a pointer will do.
7. "Leonardo's *Virgin of the Rocks* stands behind the title; the society woman of "A Game of Chess" stands in front of it." — the matched pair (behind / in front) made me stop and decode a metaphor when I wanted two facts: the title recalls the painting; the card has been identified with the woman of Part II. The second is a reading and should be marked as one, with a name if the scholar has one.
8. ""The lady of situations" sounds like a phrase from the gossip columns." — an impression with no source. If a commentator says this, cite them; if not, cut.
9. "seen in profile at line 209" — a neat explanation of "one-eyed" (a figure on a card shows one eye) slipped in as if it were in the poem. If it is somebody's reading, say whose; if it is the writer's, cut.
10. "with his blank card of something carried on his back" — I had to reread this. The poem says the blank card is something he carries on his back; the note's phrase garbles it.
11. "the sacrificed king whose death brings the rain" — a compressed summary of Frazer and Weston that sounds very sure of itself. Scholar to check that it describes Frazer's "Hanged God"; as a reader I would settle for "a god killed so that the land may revive".
12. "No sacrifice, no resurrection, is in this reading." — an inverted, quotable line. The plain fact: the card that would stand for the dying and reviving god is missing.
13. ""Fear death by water" is the reading's one instruction, and it is the one thing the Grail legend says you should not fear: water is what the waste land needs." — the paradox is the writer's, not the legend's, and it closes the list with a flourish. Eliot's note says "Death by Water is executed in Part IV"; that is the cross-reference I wanted.
14. Title "The cards, one by one" — "one by one" is a small tour-guide touch. "The cards she deals" says the same.

Keep: the Rider pack sentence at the end, entire; "The Three of Wands, a real card: a figure on a headland with his back to us, watching ships. Eliot says he associated it, "quite arbitrarily," with the Fisher King."; "The Wheel of Fortune, card X."; "Madame Sosostris cannot find him."; the three meanings of belladonna (beautiful lady; nightshade; eye-drops).

Draft:
TITLE: The cards she deals
BODY: Eliot's note says he was "not familiar with the exact constitution of the Tarot pack" and had departed from it "to suit my own convenience." The cards, in the order she names them, are these.

The drowned Phoenician Sailor. There is no such card in the Tarot. Eliot's note on line 46 says the Sailor "appears later": he is Phlebas the Phoenician, who drowns in Part IV. The note on line 218 adds that the one-eyed merchant "melts into" the Sailor, who "is not wholly distinct from" Ferdinand in <i>The Tempest</i>. The words in brackets at line 48 are from a song telling Ferdinand that his father has drowned; see the note there.

Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks. Belladonna is Italian for "beautiful lady". It is also the name of the poisonous nightshade, and of the eye-drops made from it, which women used to dilate their pupils. "The Lady of the Rocks" recalls Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>. The card has been identified with the woman of "A Game of Chess" in Part II.

The man with three staves. This is the Three of Wands, which is a card in the pack. In the design shown here a figure stands on a headland with his back to us, watching ships. Eliot's note says he associated it, "quite arbitrarily," with the Fisher King.

The Wheel. This is the Wheel of Fortune, card X. Compare "O you who turn the wheel" at line 320.

The one-eyed merchant. There is no such card in the pack. Eliot's note on line 46 says the Merchant appears later; he is Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant, at line 209. Neither the poem nor Eliot's note says what the blank card, or the thing on his back, is.

The Hanged Man. Card XII. In the design shown here a young man hangs by one foot from a living tree, his face calm and a halo round his head. Eliot's note links him to the Hanged God of Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i>, a god killed so that the land may revive, and to the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. Madame Sosostris cannot find him, so the card that on Eliot's account stands for the god's death and return is the one missing from her reading. "Fear death by water" is the only advice she gives. Eliot's note says that "Death by Water is executed in Part IV", where the Phoenician sailor drowns.

The cards shown here are Pamela Colman Smith's designs for the pack published in London by William Rider & Son in 1909, the Tarot that an English buyer of the 1910s would have found in a shop.

(The run-in headings above are plain text; the editor should mark them in whatever way the site marks them. "The card has been identified with the woman of Part II" wants a name from the scholar or should be cut. The "in profile" and "gossip columns" points can come back if the scholar can source them.)

### pearls

Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "*The Tempest*, Act I scene ii." — fragment.
2. "the song is a lie that turns out to be a description of the play, in which everyone is transformed by water" — the first half is a clever turn and the second is a claim about the whole of *The Tempest* that I am asked to accept in passing. The fact I needed is simply that the father is alive and the song untrue.
3. "The poem quotes it twice (here and at line 125), works its "sea-change" into Phlebas in Part IV, and never lets the transformation happen." — a triad ending with the poem "never letting" something happen, as if the poem were a person withholding a favour. The line 125 cross-reference is useful. "Works its 'sea-change' into Phlebas" is a reading (Part IV does not use the phrase) and should be marked as one or left out; "never lets the transformation happen" is the bow.
4. "his father the king" — the king is Alonso, King of Naples; Eliot's note, quoted in the next sentence, calls Ferdinand "Prince of Naples", so naming Naples helps me join the two up. Scholar to confirm.

Keep: "Ariel sings this to Ferdinand, who believes his father the king drowned in the wreck."; "Eliot's note on line 218 says the Phoenician Sailor "is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples."" The title. The quote is fine.

Draft:
TITLE: Ariel's song
BODY: The song is from <i>The Tempest</i>, Act I scene ii. Ariel sings it to Ferdinand, who believes his father, the King of Naples, drowned in the wreck. The father is alive; the song is Ariel's invention. Eliot quotes the line again at line 125, and his note on line 218 says the Phoenician Sailor "is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples." Compare Part IV, where the drowned Phlebas's bones are picked by the current.

### ring

Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: yes

Problems:
1. "The clairvoyante's last card is not a card but a vision" — a neat antithesis where a plain sentence would do: she does not deal this, she says she sees it.
2. "it comes true four lines later on London Bridge" — a count for effect, and a shaky one: line 60 is four lines on, but the crowd on London Bridge is at line 62.
3. "Dante's neutrals in *Inferno* III run forever after a whirling banner; Frazer's villagers dance in rings around the maypole; the commuters go round and round between the station and the office." — three parallel clauses on semicolons, a rhythm first and information second. I cannot tell which of the three the note is asking me to take seriously, or whose readings they are. Dante is Eliot's own source (his note on line 63). The maypole is unsourced. The commuters are the writer's gloss and add nothing the line 62 cross-reference does not.
4. "They do, at line 62" — a knowing aside, the tone of someone finishing my sentence for me.

Keep: the facts of the last two sentences (Eliot's note on the crowds; lines 62 and 369). The title.

Draft:
TITLE: Crowds in a ring
BODY: Madame Sosostris does not deal this; she says she sees it. Eliot's note on line 46 says the "crowds of people" appear later. They are the crowd on London Bridge at line 62 and the "hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" at line 369. Eliot's note on line 63 cites <i>Inferno</i> III, where the souls who were neither good nor evil run for ever behind a whirling banner.

(The Frazer ring-dance can come back as a marked reading if the scholar can name who made it.)

### summary

What I threw out most often was the last sentence. Five of the seven notes end on a line built to be quoted ("The poem does not choose.", "as brief as the shepherd", "She is afraid of the police.", "never lets the transformation happen", "water is what the waste land needs"), and in every case the fact the note needed was already in the sentence before. Next came matched pairs and triads that exist for their rhythm ("love ahead / love behind", "stands behind … stands in front", "not a card but a vision", Dante / Frazer / commuters); metaphors that describe the poem instead of explaining it ("a snatch of song heard through a wall", "turned inside out", "the mystical blank that Eliot's later poems circle"); and fragments ("Lilacs, line 2.", "Not a Tarot card.", "Act III of Tristan."). Readings offered as facts with nobody's name on them ("Others hear", the gossip columns, the maypole, the merchant "in profile", the woman of Part II as Belladonna) I have asked the scholar to source or cut. Two things outside wording for the editor: the tarot note misquotes Eliot's own note and uses Markdown bold; the hyacinth note quotes the 1934 Criterion at more than the phrase or two the rights rule allows. What works, and I kept: the plain story-telling about the opera and the play (the sailor, the shepherd, Ariel and Ferdinand), the Vagrancy Act, the Rider pack, and the Emily Hale and Verdenal facts with their sources.
