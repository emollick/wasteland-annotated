# Batch 4b, scholar, round 2: the library notes as rewritten, Tennyson to Eliot's 1956 lecture

### source tennyson
Verdict: not yet

1. "In 1936 Eliot wrote that Tennyson 'had the finest ear of any English poet since Milton.'" The quotation is exact but carries no source, and a reader will want one for a quotation from a post-1929 text. It is from Eliot's essay "In Memoriam" (1936), the introduction to a Nelson selection of Tennyson, reprinted in <i>Essays Ancient and Modern</i> (1936) and <i>Selected Essays</i> (1951). Put: "In his essay "In Memoriam" (1936) Eliot wrote that Tennyson "had the finest ear of any English poet since Milton.""

Round one: made. The attributions are now marked as others' ("has been cited"), Swinburne's "Itylus" is in (its first line, "Swallow, my sister, O sister swallow", is right: <i>Poems and Ballads</i>, 1866), "buried alive" is gone, and line 429 is right in the site's count. Still needs: the essay's name (above). Dropped without loss: Conrad's "Youth", which belongs on no Tennyson page.

### source whitman-lilacs
Verdict: not yet

1. Missing: Eliot's earlier statement, which bears directly on the 1928 one now quoted. Reviewing Emory Holloway's life of Whitman in 1926, four years after the poem, he wrote: "When Whitman speaks of the lilacs or of the mocking-bird, his theories and beliefs drop away like a needless pretext" ("Whitman and Tennyson", <i>Nation and Athenaeum</i>, 18 December 1926, p. 426; wording confirmed in the Whitman Archive's entry on Eliot and in Project MUSE's listing). A 1926 text may be quoted freely. Put, after the 1928 sentence: "Two years earlier, reviewing a life of Whitman, he had written that "when Whitman speaks of the lilacs or of the mocking-bird, his theories and beliefs drop away like a needless pretext" ("Whitman and Tennyson", <i>Nation and Athenaeum</i>, 18 December 1926)."

Round one: made. "He rarely admitted to reading Whitman. The lilacs and the thrush say otherwise" is gone; the line number is given; the "water-dripping song" is attached to Eliot's note; and the Musgrove reference is a real one (S. Musgrove, <i>T. S. Eliot and Walt Whitman</i>, Wellington: New Zealand University Press, 1952). Still needs: the 1926 review. Not needed: that Lincoln is unnamed, or <i>Sequel to Drum-Taps</i>.

### source webster-white-devil
Verdict: not yet

1. "after Vittoria has fired at him what she took to be a loaded pistol": there were two pistols and two shooters. Flamineo hands over the pistols, "Two of these you shall level at my breast"; the stage direction is "They shoot, and run to him, and tread upon him"; and he says, feigning, "Kill'd with a couple of braches!" (Gutenberg #12915, Act V scene vi). Put: "rising from a feigned death after Vittoria and her maid Zanche have fired at him pistols they took to be loaded."

Round one: made. The feigned death is now described from the text, the killing of Marcello, the note references and the dirge's changes are right, and "Whispers of Immortality" is dated to 1918 (its first printing, in the <i>Little Review</i>). Nothing else outstanding.

### source shakespeare-tempest
Verdict: satisfied

Round one: all made. "Gashouse" is restored; the fisherman is a fisherman, not the Fisher King; "everyone has suffered a sea-change" and "never allows the change" have given way to what the poem does (the Phoenician Sailor and Phlebas stay drowned); the alteration at 191–192 is stated as an alteration. The quotation from Eliot's note on Tiresias ("is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples") is exact (Gutenberg #1321, note 218), and "the song Ariel sings to him, unseen" matches the stage direction ("Enter Ariel, invisible, playing and singing").

### source frazer
Verdict: not yet

1. "and by implication of Christ": Frazer says it outright, in the very volumes Eliot names. <i>Adonis Attis Osiris</i>, third edition (1914), vol. I, by its own table of contents: "resemblance of Easter ceremonies to rites of Adonis, 254–256; the Christian festival of Easter perhaps grafted on a festival of Adonis, 256 sq."; "the festival of Christmas borrowed by the Church from the Mithraic religion, 302–305; the festival of Easter apparently adapted to the spring festival of Attis, 305–310" (archive.org scan). Put: "argued that the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris tell one story, that of a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by rites in which a king is sacrificed or an effigy is buried or drowned, and that the Church's Easter was adapted from the spring festivals of Adonis and Attis."

Round one: made. The title is no longer credited to Turner; the frontispiece, the first page, Nemi and Avernus are all right (Wikipedia, "The Golden Bough (painting)"); "the Hanged God of Frazer" is given as Eliot's note has it; Stetson's corpse and Phlebas are "connected" rather than "Frazer's". The quoted phrases ("flung with them into the sea", "made of earth and corn") are in the 1914 volumes as well as the abridgement. Still needs: the Christ sentence. Not needed: the chapter title "The Hanged God" (Book I, chapter V of vol. I, if ever wanted).

### source weston
Verdict: not yet

1. "The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel)": chapter VI, "The Symbols", is missing, and it is the chapter behind Eliot's note on line 46. It is where Weston makes the four Grail hallows (Cup, Lance, Sword, Dish) the suits of the Tarot: "They exist to-day as the four suits of the Tarot" (Gutenberg #4090, chapter VI). Put: "The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), VI (The Symbols, where the Grail hallows become the four suits of the Tarot), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel)."

Round one: made. Chapter XII's three carriers (merchants, slaves, soldiers) are given with the chapter; "It does not matter" and "a very good bad book" are gone; the headnote and the 1956 phrase are quoted exactly. Optional: name the 1956 lecture, though the site's own page for it stands three entries below.

### source petronius
Verdict: not yet

1. "asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand": Ovid's words are "ego pulveris hausti / ostendens cumulum, quot haberet corpora pulvis, / tot mihi natales contingere vana rogavi" (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV.136–138, Latin Library): dust, not sand, in a heap she had scooped up and holds out. The Part I gloss's "a heap of dust she had picked up" is the exact rendering, and the library note should agree with it: yes to the editor's question. My round-one objection that "there is no hand" was too strict; "hausti" means she had taken the dust up, so a hand is implied. The errors are "sand" for dust and the heap left out. Put: "asked Apollo for as many years as there were grains in a heap of dust she had scooped up, and forgot to ask for youth to go with them". Ovid's word is the word of line 30, "dust"; if the editor wants that resonance noted, it must be marked as a parallel, since Eliot's notes give no source for line 30.

Round one: made. "Fat" is gone, the shrinking is foretold, and the replacement of the Conrad epigraph is in. Still needs: the sentence above.

### source dante-purg26
Verdict: not yet

1. Missing: the phrase of the dedication was already Pound's own. Chapter II of Pound's <i>The Spirit of Romance</i> (Dent, 1910), on Arnaut Daniel, is titled "Il Miglior Fabbro" (archive.org, spiritofromancea00pounrich; Wikipedia, "Arnaut Daniel"). Put, after the sentence on the dedication: "The phrase was already Pound's own: "Il Miglior Fabbro" is the title of his chapter on Arnaut Daniel in <i>The Spirit of Romance</i> (1910)."
2. Missing, lesser: the Ovid Press title page reads "Ara Vus Prec"; the spine label was printed correctly, and Eliot, writing to Donald Gallup in 1936, blamed the form on the Italian edition of Dante he had used (Evi Heinz, "Tricky Firsts: T. S. Eliot's <i>Ara Vos Prec</i>", 2024; the Cambridge chapter on <i>Poems</i> (1919), <i>Ara Vos Prec</i> (1920), <i>Poems</i> (1920)). A reader who meets "Ara Vus Prec" in a catalogue will want it. Put: "Eliot took the title of his 1920 collection <i>Ara Vos Prec</i> from line 145 (the Ovid Press title page misprinted it "Ara Vus Prec", a form Eliot later blamed on his Italian edition of Dante), and line 428 of the poem from line 148, which his note quotes with the three lines before it."

Round one: made. The dedication's dating (written into Pound's copy, January 1923; first printed in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>), "li affina" against "gli", Guinizelli and Arnaut, and the four lines quoted in Eliot's note are all right. Still needs: the two additions above.

### source dickens-omf
Verdict: not yet

1. "Sloppy, the foundling she has brought up": he was brought up in the workhouse, not by her. Betty Higden: "'A love-child,' returned Betty Higden, dropping her voice; 'parents never known; found in the street. He was brought up in the—' with a shiver of repugnance, '—the House.'" ("The Poor-house?" asks the Secretary, and she nods.) He now lives with her and works for her, turning the mangle of her "Minding-School" ("And does he work for you?" "Yes ... And well too"; Book I chapter 16, Gutenberg #883). Put: "Betty Higden, a poor old woman who minds children for a living, is praising Sloppy, a foundling brought up in the workhouse who lives with her and turns her mangle, and who reads her the newspaper and does all the voices in the police-court reports."

Round one: made. The heading is typed, and dated to the drafts; "including its author" and "the poem's river in prose" are gone; the opening is stated as fact (the boat is searching the river, and by the end of the chapter has a body in tow).

### source conrad-hod
Verdict: not yet

1. "stood at the head of the typescript of Part I as the poem's epigraph": it stands on the typescript's title page, before Part I, as the epigraph of the whole poem (the 1971 facsimile, p. 3, which I could not open today; the British Library's account of the manuscript says the Conrad quotation "stands at the front of this manuscript"). Part I's own typescript opens under the "He Do the Police" heading. Put: "stood on the title page of the typescript, as the poem's epigraph."
2. The letters are quoted without date or source. Pound's is dated in his private calendar, "24 Saturnus, An 1", which the facsimile reads as 24 December 1921 and later editors, the British Library among them, as 24 January 1922; Eliot's reply and Pound's "Do as you like about Conrad" are of late January 1922; all three are printed in the facsimile and in the <i>Letters</i>, vol. 1. Put: "Pound, returning the poem in January 1922, wrote that he doubted whether Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation." Eliot answered that it was "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," and Pound withdrew the objection (the exchange is printed in the 1971 facsimile and in the <i>Letters</i>, vol. 1); Eliot nevertheless replaced it with the passage from Petronius that stands there now."

Round one: made. Pound's withdrawal and Eliot's decision are in; "the heart of light" is marked as a reading; the yawl at anchor is right. Not needed: "Mistah Kurtz—he dead" as the epigraph of "The Hollow Men" (1925), which the editor may add or not.

### source eliot-frontiers
Verdict: satisfied

Round one: all made. The false quotation of "bought" is gone, the <i>Criterion</i> and <i>Dial</i> printings without notes are in, the closing instruction is gone; date, place, audience and the 1957 printing are right, and the three short phrases from the lecture ("spiking the guns", "bogus scholarship", "a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail"), two of them already in the passage above the note, keep within the rule for a post-1929 text.

### summary
Satisfied: shakespeare-tempest, eliot-frontiers.
Not yet, the corrections that matter most:
1. frazer: "by implication of Christ" is wrong; Frazer argues in the two volumes Eliot names that Easter was adapted from the spring festivals of Adonis and Attis (vol. I, pp. 254–57, 305–10).
2. petronius: Ovid's Sibyl shows a heap of dust she had scooped up ("pulveris hausti ... cumulum", XIV.136–137), not "grains of sand in her hand"; the library note should agree with the Part I gloss, and my round-one "no hand" was over-strict.
3. dickens-omf: Sloppy was brought up in the workhouse, not by Betty Higden; he lives with her and turns her mangle.
4. webster-white-devil: two pistols, fired by Vittoria and Zanche ("They shoot ... Kill'd with a couple of braches!").
5. conrad-hod: the epigraph stood on the typescript's title page, not "at the head of the typescript of Part I"; date and source the letters (January 1922; facsimile; <i>Letters</i>, vol. 1).
6. weston: add chapter VI (The Symbols), where the Grail hallows become the suits of the Tarot.
7. whitman-lilacs: add Eliot's 1926 remark on Whitman's "lilacs" (<i>Nation and Athenaeum</i>, 18 December 1926) beside the 1928 disclaimer.
8. dante-purg26: "Il Miglior Fabbro" was Pound's own chapter title in <i>The Spirit of Romance</i> (1910); the 1920 title page reads "Ara Vus Prec".
9. tennyson: name the 1936 essay ("In Memoriam").
