# Batch 2, scholar, round 2 (lines 31–59)

### frisch-weht

Verdict: satisfied

Everything in the rewrite checks against the libretto (murashev.com, Act I): the stage direction "aus der Höhe, wie vom Mast her", the ship's course in the four lines before Eliot's, Marke as Tristan's uncle, Isolde's "Wer wagt mich zu höhnen?", and "verses 5–8". One precision the editor may take or leave, since it is not an error a reader would be misled by: the orchestral Prelude comes first and the libretto prints it under Act I ("Erster Aufzug. ### hyacinth-girl

Verdict: not yet

1. "The lines themselves take nothing from the myth but the name." A reading given as fact, and one that cuts against Eliot's own headnote, which tells readers of Frazer that they "will immediately recognise in the poem certain references to vegetation ceremonies"; the hyacinth is usually counted among them, which is why the commentaries give the myth at all. Put: "None of this is needed to read the lines. The commentaries give it because Eliot's headnote promises readers of Frazer 'certain references to vegetation ceremonies', and the hyacinth is usually counted as one."

2. "the first section of <i>Burnt Norton</i> (1935)". The poem was written in 1935 but first published in <i>Collected Poems 1909–1935</i> (Faber, 1936); the note's other dates are publication dates (<i>Crome Yellow</i> November 1921, <i>Prufrock</i> 1917, <i>Eliot's Early Years</i> 1977). Put: "the first section of <i>Burnt Norton</i> (1936)". Source: Wikipedia, "Burnt Norton" ("first published in his <i>Collected Poems 1909–1935</i> (1936)").

3. "John Peter (1952) and James E. Miller (1977) proposed Jean Verdenal". Peter's 1952 essay ("A New Interpretation of <i>The Waste Land</i>", <i>Essays in Criticism</i> II.3, July 1952, pp. 242–66) read the poem as an elegy for a dead friend and did not name him; Eliot's solicitors had the issue withdrawn. The name came only in the "Postscript" Peter added when the essay was reprinted in <i>Essays in Criticism</i> XIX (1969), after Eliot's death. Sources: Wikipedia, "Jean Jules Verdenal" ("In this second essay Peter identified the friend as Verdenal"); OUP, <i>Essays in Criticism</i> II.3 (1952) and XIX.2 (1969). Put: "John Peter, whose 1952 essay read the poem as an elegy for a dead friend and whose 1969 postscript named him, and James E. Miller (1977) proposed Jean Verdenal, the friend of Eliot's Paris year, killed at the Dardanelles in May 1915, to whom <i>Prufrock and Other Observations</i> (1917) is dedicated."

Otherwise sound. Gordon's 1977 book did already make the Hale link (her own account, <i>Modernist Review</i> interview, December 2019), so that sentence stands. "(see the note on the epigraph)" lands: the batch-1 epigraph rewrite gives the Conrad passage and Pound's letter. "Compare the lilacs of line 2" may stand; it asserts nothing.

### oed-und-leer

Verdict: not yet

1. "that one from the opening of the opera, before Tristan and Isolde have fallen in love". In Wagner's Act I the love is already there: Isolde's narrative in scene 3 tells how she spared the wounded Tristan, calling himself "Tantris", when he looked into her eyes, and her rage is at his betrayal of that; what the act ends with is the potion (scene 5, "Der Liebestrank"), which releases it. Source: the libretto, Act I scenes 3 and 5 (murashev.com). Put: "that one from the opening of the opera, before the love potion, this one from its last act, when they are apart and Tristan is dying."

Everything else checks: Kareol is named in the libretto (Kurwenal: "Kareol, Herr: kennst du die Burg der Väter nicht?"); the count to "verse 24" is exact; "Id." for <i>idem</i> is right.

### tarot

Verdict: not yet

1. Misquotation and a loose fact in the Sailor entry. "Eliot's note says the Sailor 'appears later'": the note reads "The Phoenician Sailor and the Merchant appear later" (Eliot's note on 46, in `poem-part1.md`); the singular does not occur. And "Part IV, 'Death by Water', is the drowning of Phlebas": Part IV opens with Phlebas "a fortnight dead"; the drowning is over before it starts. Put: "Eliot's note says the Sailor and the Merchant 'appear later': Part IV, 'Death by Water', is Phlebas the Phoenician, 'a fortnight dead'."

2. "Weston, in her chapter on the Grail symbols, says the Tarot was first used 'to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land'." Weston does not say it; she conditions it: "if the connection with the Egyptian and Chinese monuments, referred to above, is genuine, the original use of the 'Tarot' would seem to have been, not to foretell the Future in general, but to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land" (<i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, ch. VI, "The Symbols"; Gutenberg #4090). Put: "Weston, in her chapter on the Grail symbols, suggests that the Tarot's original use 'would seem to have been, not to foretell the Future in general, but to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land'."

3. The bracketed question: no, Brooks (1939) does not make the missing card the point of her reading, and the sentence as drafted misstates him. I read the essay in its 1947 reprint ("The Waste Land: An Analysis", in B. Rajan, ed., <i>T. S. Eliot: A Study of His Writings by Several Hands</i>, archive.org in.ernet.dli.2015.53128; the text is that of <i>Modern Poetry and the Tradition</i>, 1939, as also reprinted in the Cox and Hinchliffe <i>Casebook</i>, 1968, pp. 128–61). Brooks does address her not finding the card, but explains it by the hood: "The Hanged Man, who represents the hanged god of Frazer (including the Christ), Eliot states in a note, is associated with the hooded figure who appears in 'What the Thunder Said'. That he is hooded accounts for Madame Sosostris' inability to see him; or rather, here again the palaver of the modern fortune-teller is turned to new and important account by the poet's shifting the matter into a new and serious context." His general claim is that her fortune "becomes true as the poem develops, true in a sense in which Madame Sosostris herself does not think it true". The words "Madame Sosostris does not find" occur in no reprint of the essay (archive.org full-text search finds them only in six later critics). The critic who makes something of the card she cannot find is Hugh Kenner, <i>The Invisible Poet</i> (1959), of the protagonist in the hyacinth garden: "It is not clear whether he is comforted to learn that the clairvoyante does not find the Hanged Man." Put, in place of "Since Cleanth Brooks (1939) the missing card has been read as the point of her reading: the god who dies and returns is absent from it.": "Cleanth Brooks (1939), who read her whole fortune as coming true in the poem, ties the card she cannot find to the hooded figure of Part V: 'That he is hooded accounts for Madame Sosostris' inability to see him.'" Or cut the sentence; the note loses nothing it can support.

Not an objection: the same page of Brooks supplies the name the editor wanted for "in profile", cut in round one for want of one: "The fact that the merchant is one-eyed apparently means in Madame Sosostris's speech no more than that the merchant's face on the card is shown in profile." If the editor wants it back in the Merchant entry: "Brooks (1939) took 'one-eyed' to mean no more than that the face on the card is shown in profile."
