### source chaucer
Verdict: correct
Checked: General Prologue 1–12 against Skeat's text (Gutenberg #22120); the site prints the Riverside spelling ("Aprill", "soote"), which is fine, and the twelve lines and the translation are accurate. Eliot's Part V headnote ("the approach to the Chapel Perilous") in the 1922 text (Gutenberg #1321). The date c. 1387 is the usual one.
Substance:
1. "English poetry's first great spring": not so. The spring opening is a convention Chaucer inherited. "Sumer is icumen in" (c. 1250) and the Harley lyric "Lenten ys come with love to toune" (c. 1300) are earlier English springs, and the Prologue's own opening follows Guido delle Colonne and Boccaccio. "The most famous spring opening in English poetry" is defensible; "first" is a flourish that misstates.
2. "Eliot's poem also ends in a kind of pilgrimage, to the Chapel Perilous": the poem does not end there. The chapel is in Part V (Eliot's headnote names "the approach to the Chapel Perilous"; the empty chapel is at site lines 388–391), and the thunder, the Fisher King and the fragments follow it. Say "Part V has a journey of its own, to the Chapel Perilous."
3. The comparison with Chaucer is not in Eliot's notes. It is the commentators' (Southam; Ricks and McCue), and it should be introduced as such so a reader knows what kind of claim it is.
4. "its people would rather have stayed under the snow": a reading of lines 5–7, reasonable, but a reading. Fine if kept plain ("the 'we' of lines 5–7 were kept warm by winter").
5. "the whole country wants to travel": a paraphrase of "Thanne longen folk to goon on pilgrimages"; Chaucer's folk go to a shrine, not travelling for its own sake. Minor.
Draft:
Body: The opening of the General Prologue is the spring that Eliot's first line is usually set against: April's showers end the drought of March, the sap rises, the birds sing, and people long to go on pilgrimage. Eliot's notes do not mention Chaucer; the comparison is the commentators' (Southam; Ricks and McCue), and it is hard to avoid. In the poem April breeds lilacs out of dead land, and the "we" of lines 5–7 were kept warm by the snow. Part V has a journey of its own, to the Chapel Perilous of the Grail romances (Eliot's headnote to Part V).

The grasshopper is a burden because the old can carry nothing (12:1–7 is an allegory of the body's decay); the note might say so.
4. Date "3rd century BC": the usual scholarly dating (c. 250–225 BC). Fine.
Draft:
Body: Eliot's note sends the reader to verse 5, in the chapter's allegory of old age: the grasshopper is a burden because the old can carry nothing, and desire fails. The cricket is Eliot's word; the King James Bible, like the Vulgate, has a grasshopper. The verses that follow, with the golden bowl broken and "the wheel broken at the cistern," can be set beside Part V's "empty cisterns and exhausted wells" (line 385).

### source dante-inf4
Verdict: correct
Checked: Inferno IV.25–27 at it.wikisource (Petrocchi): the passage as displayed is exact ("non avea pianto mai che di sospiri"; Eliot's note has the Temple Classics "ma' che di sospiri"). "sanza speme vivemo in disio" (42); "li spiriti magni" (119). Eliot's note 64 in work/poem-part1.md. Site line 64 counted in the 1922 text.
Substance:
1. "the 'great-hearted' souls": Dante's phrase is "li spiriti magni" (IV.119), the great spirits. "Great-hearted" renders "magnanimo," which Dante uses of Farinata (X.73), not here. Say "the great spirits," or drop the quotation marks.
2. "the office crowd sigh like the noble pagans, not like the damned": a reading, and on substance shaky. Limbo is the first circle of hell and its souls are lost ("semo perduti," IV.41); and Eliot's sighs are "short and infrequent" where Dante's make the eternal air tremble. The likeness is the sigh; the difference is the scale. "Office crowd" is an inference from the hour and the street, a fair one.
3. "home of the virtuous who died unbaptised: Homer, Virgil, Aristotle": right (IV.88, 131; Virgil's own place, IV.39–42), with the unbaptised infants (IV.30, 35–36). "who are not tormented but live in desire without hope": right (IV.28, 41–42).
4. "One line of the poem, one canto of Dante": true as far as the notes go (note 64 cites IV), and the note to line 63 cites III; the two notes together are the fact worth giving (see dante-inf3).
Draft:
Body: Limbo, the first circle, holds the unbaptised and the virtuous pagans, Homer and Aristotle among them, and Virgil himself. They are not tormented, but, as Virgil says, "without hope we live in desire" (IV.42); their sighs, not weeping, make the eternal air tremble. Eliot's note attaches the tercet to line 64. The line before it he had sent to the vestibule of <i>Inferno</i> III, so the crowd on London Bridge is given, in successive notes, the neutrals' number and the noble pagans' sighs.

There is no dry stone in Ezekiel; the phrase is the poem's (line 24). Cut it from the list.
2. The chapter 6 link is a commentator's comparison (Southam; Ricks and McCue), not Eliot's, whose note cites only 2:1. Mark it as such.
3. "ninety-odd times": 93 in the King James text; the exact figure is as short to say.
4. "which is to say mortal": a fair gloss of "ben adam," a human being, and the reason Eliot's note is useful: it keeps the phrase from being read as Christ's title in the Gospels.
5. "In the valley the bones stand up. In the poem they do not.": accurate as a contrast (37:7–10, the bones come together, are given breath and stand up "an exceeding great army"; the poem's bones lie by the empty chapel). Fine on substance; the two fragments are a register matter for the reader.
6. "prophet of the Babylonian exile," "6th century BC," "the moment of his commission": right (1:1–3; 2:1–5).
Draft:
Body: Ezekiel prophesied among the exiles in Babylon in the sixth century BC, and God addresses him as "son of man," a mortal, ninety-three times in the King James text. Eliot's note points to the first of them, at the prophet's commission (2:1). Commentators add 6:4–6, where the idolaters' "images shall be broken," for line 22, and the valley of dry bones (37:1–14) for line 391, "Dry bones can harm no one." In Ezekiel the bones are given breath and stand up, "an exceeding great army"; in the poem they lie still.

### source isaiah
Verdict: correct
Checked: Isaiah 32:1–2 and 38:1–5, KJV, en.wikisource: the passage as displayed is word for word; 38:2–5 for the wall, the weeping and the fifteen years. Eliot's notes in work/poem-part1.md have nothing on line 25; his note to line 424 (site 425) cites Weston's chapter on the Fisher King. Site line 426 "Shall I at least set my lands in order?" counted in the 1922 text (Gutenberg #1321); it matches the batch's 426.
Substance:
1. Neither passage is Eliot's; both are commentators' comparisons (Southam; Ricks and McCue). The note should say so; as it stands it reads as if Eliot had pointed to Isaiah.
2. "The red rock of Part I offers the prophet's shelter without the prophet's promise of water": a reading built on a slip. In 32:2 the shelter is not the prophet's: in the reign of a righteous king (32:1) "a man shall be as an hiding place from the wind," and "rivers of water in a dry place" is one of four likenesses for that man. The contrast with line 24 ("the dry stone no sound of water") is fair; phrase it as the site's own observation.
3. "The Fisher King of line 426 asks the question and gets no answer": that the speaker of lines 424–426 is the Fisher King rests on Eliot's note to line 425 pointing to Weston's chapter on him; it is still a reading, and "gets no answer" is a reading of the fragments that follow. Say "the speaker, whom Eliot's note to line 425 links to Weston's Fisher King."
4. Missing, one word: Isaiah's "house" becomes Eliot's "lands." The change is the point, since the Fisher King's land lies waste with him.
5. DATE "8th century BC": right for the prophet; chapters 36–39 are a narrative parallel to 2 Kings 18–20 and usually dated later. "8th century BC and later" would be safer. Minor.
Draft:
Body: Neither passage is in Eliot's notes; both are commentators' comparisons (Southam; Ricks and McCue). For the shadow of the red rock (line 25) they cite 32:2, where in the reign of a righteous king a man is "as rivers of water in a dry place, as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land"; the poem's rock gives shadow and no water (line 24). For "Shall I at least set my lands in order?" (line 426) they cite the sentence on King Hezekiah, "Set thine house in order: for thou shalt die." Hezekiah turned his face to the wall and wept, and was given fifteen more years (38:2–5). Eliot's note to line 425 sends the reader to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King, whose land lies waste with him; the poem turns house into lands.

### summary
1. tristan: "four words" is five ("Oed' und leer das Meer"). The 1909 Boston performance is undocumented: the evidence is the poem "Opera," dated November 1909 (Brooker hedges "or heard an orchestral arrangement"; the Met's Boston Tristan was April 1908; the Boston Opera Company opened 8 November 1909). "Senior" is wrong: AB June 1909, graduate student and assistant in philosophy 1909–10. Ludwig was found dead in the lake and drowning is disputed (match batch 1). The Act I ship is under way, not setting out. Eliot's "verses 5–8" and "verse 24" both check against the printed libretto, which is worth saying.
2. huxley-crome-yellow: "the immediate source" is Eliot's own recollection (letter to Grover Smith, 10 March 1952, "almost certain," unconscious) and is disputed by Rainey's dating of the typescript of Parts I–II to May 1921, before Huxley finished the novel (August 1921). Give both. What was borrowed is the name; Scogan reads palms, and the cards are Eliot's. Huxley's event is "the Fair," not a fête.
3. death-of-st-narcissus: the saint "had been" a tree, a fish and a girl, not "wanted to be"; the red rock is already in the 1915 poem's fifth line, so "made red" misdescribes the borrowing; add the first printing (Poems Written in Early Youth, 1950; trade 1967) to agree with batch 1.
4. ecclesiastes: there is no "dry cistern" in the chapter ("the wheel broken at the cistern"); the drought is Part V's (line 385). The cricket is Eliot's word for the King James grasshopper.
5. ezekiel: "dry stone" is not in Ezekiel; "broken images" (ch. 6) is a commentator's link, not Eliot's note. "Son of man" is 93 times.
6. chaucer: not "English poetry's first great spring" (earlier English springs exist and the opening follows a convention); the poem does not "end" at the Chapel Perilous, which is in Part V; the comparison is the commentators', not Eliot's.
7. baudelaire-au-lecteur: first printed 1 June 1855 in the Revue des deux mondes; 1857 is the book. Name the essays the two quotations come from ("Baudelaire," 1930; "What Dante Means to Me," 1950).
8. dante-inf3 and dante-inf4: "are these" is a reading, and Eliot's own notes put the London crowd in two places (III, the neutrals; IV, Limbo); the notes should say so. "Great-hearted" misrenders "spiriti magni" (the great spirits). Eliot's tercets are the Temple Classics text, which is why they differ from the Petrocchi passages displayed.
9. isaiah: both links are commentators' (Southam; Ricks and McCue), not Eliot's; in 32:2 the shelter is a man's in a righteous reign, not "the prophet's"; Isaiah's "house" becomes Eliot's "lands."
10. baudelaire-sept-vieillards stands; the "method" sentence is a reading that Eliot's 1950 talk supports and could be attributed to it.
11. The line numbers this batch gives for Part V (391, 426) are right in the every-line count; 385 for "empty cisterns" is my own count from the 1922 text.
