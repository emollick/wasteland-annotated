### unreal-city  (kind: echo; line 60–61; hangs on the words “Unreal City”; library source: baudelaire-sept-vieillards)
TITLE: Baudelaire's swarming city
QUOTE (source passage shown above the note): Fourmillante cité, cité pleine de rêves, / Où le spectre en plein jour raccroche le passant!
TRANSLATION (shown under the quote): Swarming city, city full of dreams, where the ghost in broad daylight accosts the passer-by!
BODY:
The opening of "Les Sept Vieillards" ("The Seven Old Men") from the *Tableaux parisiens* section of *Les Fleurs du mal*, in which the poet, walking in a yellow fog, meets the same hideous old man seven times over. Eliot said in 1950 that Baudelaire had taught him "the poetical possibilities . . . of the more sordid aspects of the modern metropolis, of the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric." That is the recipe for the next sixteen lines: a real bridge, a real street, a real church clock, and the dead.

The fog is coal smoke. Eliot went into the City through it every working morning from 1917, to Lloyds Bank in Lombard Street. "Unreal City" returns at line 207 (a winter noon) and, stripped to one word, at line 377.

### london-bridge-dead  (kind: echo; line 63–65; hangs on the words “I had not thought death had undone so many”; library source: dante-inf3)
TITLE: The crowd at the gate of Hell
QUOTE (source passage shown above the note): . . . sì lunga tratta / di gente, ch’i’ non averei creduto / che morte tanta n’avesse disfatta.
TRANSLATION (shown under the quote): . . . so long a train of people, that I would never have believed death had undone so many.
BODY:
*Inferno* III, lines 55–57. Just inside the gate, before the river, Dante sees the neutrals, the souls who lived "without infamy and without praise," and is astonished at how many there are. Line 64, "Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled," is from the next canto, the Limbo of the virtuous unbaptised, where "there was no weeping but only sighs, which made the eternal air tremble" (IV, 25–27). So the commuters crossing from London Bridge station into the City between half past eight and nine are damned twice over, once as people who never chose anything, once as people who were merely born too early. Eliot worked among them. "Each man fixed his eyes before his feet" is what a crowd on a bridge in a fog actually looks like.

### woolnoth  (kind: place; line 67–68; hangs on the words “Saint Mary Woolnoth”; image: st-mary-woolnoth)
TITLE: The dead sound at nine
BODY:
King William Street runs from London Bridge up to the Bank, and at its top, on the corner of Lombard Street, stands Nicholas Hawksmoor's St Mary Woolnoth (1716–27), a squat, heavily rusticated church with two little turrets. Lloyds Bank, where Eliot worked in the Colonial and Foreign Department from 1917 to 1925, was across the road. The church's clock struck the start of his working day.

"A phenomenon which I have often noticed," says Eliot's note on the dead sound of the final stroke, the poem's driest joke about its own apparatus. There is a literal death under this church too: when the Bank station of the City and South London Railway was built beneath it in 1897–1900, the bodies in the crypt were cleared out and reburied at Ilford, and the crypt became the station's booking hall. The commuters come up out of it every morning.

### stetson  (kind: gloss; line 69–70; hangs on the words “Stetson”)
TITLE: Stetson, and the ships at Mylae
BODY:
Mylae is the sea battle of 260 BC in which Rome first beat Carthage at sea, early in the First Punic War, a trade war between two commercial empires. The friend met in the City of London, the financial centre of another commercial empire four years after another war, was "in the ships" there. The war dead and the office crowd are the same people, and all wars are the one war. Stetson has often been taken for Pound, who wore wide-brimmed hats; nothing in the poem needs him to be anyone in particular. The name is a hat, an American hat, on a bridge in London.

### corpse  (kind: echo; line 71–73; hangs on the words “That corpse you planted last year in your garden”; library source: frazer)
TITLE: The buried god
BODY:
Frazer's *The Golden Bough*, in the volumes Eliot's headnote singles out (*Adonis, Attis, Osiris*), describes the "gardens of Adonis": pots of quick-sprouting seed carried in the god's funeral procession and thrown into the sea, and the "Osiris beds," effigies of the god filled with earth and corn and buried so that the sprouting grain showed his resurrection. The corpse in Stetson's suburban garden is that ritual gone domestic and gone wrong. Will it bloom? Has the frost got at it? The question is asked in the voice of a man talking about bulbs, and it is the question of the whole poem: can anything come back?

### dog  (kind: echo; line 74–75; hangs on the words “keep the Dog far hence”; library source: webster-white-devil)
TITLE: Webster's dirge, with one word changed
QUOTE (source passage shown above the note): Call for the robin-redbreast and the wren, / Since o’er shady groves they hover, / And with leaves and flowers do cover / The friendless bodies of unburied men. . . . / But keep the wolf far thence, that’s foe to men, / For with his nails he’ll dig them up again.
BODY:
Cornelia's mad song over her murdered son in *The White Devil* (1612), Act V scene iv. Eliot swaps the wolf for a dog and "foe" for "friend," and capitalises the Dog. The domestic pet, man's friend, becomes the thing that will not let the buried god stay buried long enough to sprout. Readers who want more have found the Dog Star, Sirius, whose rising announced the flooding of the Nile and the return of Osiris. The change of one word is the kind of thing Eliot meant when he wrote, in "Philip Massinger" (1920), that "immature poets imitate; mature poets steal."

### hypocrite-lecteur  (kind: echo; line 76; hangs on the words “hypocrite lecteur!—mon semblable,—mon frère!”; library source: baudelaire-au-lecteur)
TITLE: The reader accused
QUOTE (source passage shown above the note): C’est l’Ennui! — l’œil chargé d’un pleur involontaire, / Il rêve d’échafauds en fumant son houka. / Tu le connais, lecteur, ce monstre délicat, / — Hypocrite lecteur, — mon semblable, — mon frère!
TRANSLATION (shown under the quote): It is Boredom! Eye charged with an involuntary tear, he dreams of scaffolds while smoking his hookah. You know him, reader, this delicate monster, hypocrite reader, my double, my brother!
BODY:
The last line of "Au Lecteur," the poem that opens *Les Fleurs du mal* (1857). Baudelaire's catalogue of vices ends with the worst, boredom, and then turns on the reader and makes him an accomplice. Eliot ends the first section by doing the same thing to you. The "you" that has been Stetson is suddenly whoever is holding the book, and the Frenchness of the line is part of the accusation: you were meant to recognise it.
