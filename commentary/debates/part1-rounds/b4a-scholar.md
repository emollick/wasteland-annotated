## Library notes, Chaucer to Isaiah (site/sources.txt)

### source chaucer
TITLE: The Canterbury Tales, General Prologue
AUTHOR: Geoffrey Chaucer
DATE: c. 1387
WHAT: Chaucer’s April of sweet showers and pilgrimages, behind Eliot’s “cruellest month.”
LINES: 1
NOTE: The Prologue opens with April rain ending the drought of March, sap rising, birds singing through the night and people longing to go on pilgrimage. Eliot’s notes say nothing of Chaucer; readers have long set “April is the cruellest month” against these lines, in which the same month and the same rain are welcome. In Eliot’s poem it is winter that “kept us warm” (lines 5–6).

### source ecclesiastes
TITLE: Ecclesiastes 12
AUTHOR: The Bible (King James Version)
DATE: 3rd century BC; translation 1611
WHAT: “the cricket no relief”: the chapter on old age and death.
LINES: 23
NOTE: Eliot’s note on line 23 cites verse 5, where “the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail”; the poem has a cricket in the grasshopper’s place. The chapter is an allegory of old age and death, and its pitcher broken at the fountain and wheel broken at the cistern have been heard again in the “empty cisterns and exhausted wells” of line 385.

### source tristan
TITLE: Tristan und Isolde
AUTHOR: Richard Wagner
DATE: 1865 (first performed Munich)
WHAT: The sailor’s song (lines 31–34) and the shepherd’s “Öd und leer das Meer,” desolate and empty the sea (line 42).
LINES: 31, 42
NOTE: Eliot’s notes cite the libretto by act and verse: I, verses 5–8, and III, verse 24. The young sailor’s song is the first thing sung in the opera; the sailor sings it unseen, from the masthead, above the deck where Isolde lies. The shepherd’s “Öd und leer das Meer!” comes in Act III, answering Tristan’s servant Kurwenal, who has asked whether any ship is in sight while Tristan lies wounded and waiting for Isolde. In the poem the hyacinth garden (lines 35–41) falls between the two quotations. Eliot heard <i>Tristan</i>, or its music in concert, in Boston in the autumn of 1909, at the start of his graduate year at Harvard, and wrote a poem about it, “Opera,” dated November 1909; Stravinsky, who met him in 1956, thought <i>Tristan</i> “must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life.” Ludwig II of Bavaria, whose money paid for the first performance, was found dead in 1886 in the Starnberger See, the lake of line 8.

### source baudelaire-sept-vieillards
TITLE: Les Sept Vieillards (from Les Fleurs du mal)
AUTHOR: Charles Baudelaire
DATE: 1859; in the 1861 edition of Les Fleurs du mal
WHAT: “Unreal City”: the swarming Paris of “The Seven Old Men.”
LINES: 60
NOTE: The poet, walking through a dirty yellow fog on a Paris morning, meets a hideous old man, then another exactly like him, and another, seven in all, and flees home half mad. Eliot’s note on line 60 quotes the first two lines. Stetson, hailed on King William Street at nine in the morning as a shipmate from the battle of Mylae (lines 69–70), has been read as Eliot’s version of Baudelaire’s daylight ghost.

### source baudelaire-au-lecteur
TITLE: Au Lecteur (the preface poem of Les Fleurs du mal)
AUTHOR: Charles Baudelaire
DATE: 1857
WHAT: “hypocrite lecteur!—mon semblable,—mon frère!”
LINES: 76
NOTE: Baudelaire opens his book by listing the sins of mankind, saving the worst for last, boredom, and then turning on whoever is reading. Eliot closes his first section the same way. He said in 1930 that Baudelaire was “the greatest exemplar in modern poetry in any language,” and in 1950 that he had learned from him “the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric.”

### source death-of-st-narcissus
TITLE: The Death of Saint Narcissus
AUTHOR: T. S. Eliot
DATE: 1915 (withdrawn from publication)
WHAT: “Come in under the shadow of this red rock.”
LINES: 25
NOTE: Set in type for <i>Poetry</i> (Chicago) in 1915 and withdrawn in proof; first published in 1950. The poem is about a saint who is sure he had been a tree, a fish and a young girl, and who dies pierced with arrows. The manuscript survived among the papers Eliot sent in 1922 to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector, with the <i>Waste Land</i> drafts, and is now in the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library. After these two lines the speaker promises to show the listener something other than the listener’s own shadow, sprawling on the sand at daybreak or leaping behind the fire. <i>The Waste Land</i> keeps the second line, with “gray” changed to “red,” and reshapes the first. The poem is still in copyright, so only its first two lines are given here.

### source huxley-crome-yellow
TITLE: Crome Yellow, chapter 27
AUTHOR: Aldous Huxley
DATE: 1921
WHAT: “Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana”: the name behind Madame Sosostris.
LINES: 43
NOTE: Huxley’s country-house satire was published in November 1921. Its Mr. Scogan, a cynical guest who dresses up as an old gypsy woman to tell fortunes at the village fête under the name “Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana,” is the accepted source of Madame Sosostris. Asked about it by the critic Grover Smith, Eliot wrote to him on 10 March 1952 that he was “almost certain” he had taken the name from <i>Crome Yellow</i> without being conscious of doing so. Huxley and Eliot had known each other since the war years through Lady Ottoline Morrell’s circle at Garsington, her house near Oxford.

### source dante-inf3
TITLE: Inferno, canto III
AUTHOR: Dante Alighieri
DATE: c. 1308
WHAT: The crowd on London Bridge: “I had not thought death had undone so many.”
LINES: 62, 63, 56
NOTE: Inside the gate (“Abandon all hope”) but before the river Acheron, Dante finds the souls who were neither good nor bad, “who lived without infamy and without praise,” rejected by Heaven and refused by Hell, running for ever after a banner and stung by wasps. Eliot’s note on line 63 quotes lines 55–57 as the source of the crowd flowing over London Bridge, and his note on line 46 says that the “crowds of people” in Madame Sosostris’s cards “appear later,” which has generally been taken to mean this crowd. Eliot said in 1950 that Dante’s poetry was still, “after forty years,” “the most persistent and deepest influence” on his own verse.

### source dante-inf4
TITLE: Inferno, canto IV
AUTHOR: Dante Alighieri
DATE: c. 1308
WHAT: Limbo’s sighs, behind “Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled.”
LINES: 64
NOTE: Limbo, the first circle of Hell, holds the virtuous who died unbaptised, Homer, Virgil and Aristotle among them; Dante calls them the “great spirits” (<i>spiriti magni</i>), and they are not tormented but live in desire without hope. Eliot’s note on line 64 cites these lines. The comparison likens the sighs of the office crowd to those of Limbo, where there is grief but no torment, not to the cries of the damned.

### source ezekiel
TITLE: Ezekiel 2 and 37
AUTHOR: The Bible (King James Version)
DATE: 6th century BC; translation 1611
WHAT: “Son of man”; “a heap of broken images”; the valley of dry bones.
LINES: 20, 22, 391
NOTE: Ezekiel, prophet of the Babylonian exile, is addressed by God some ninety times as “son of man,” that is, mortal man. Eliot’s note on line 20 cites 2:1, where God first speaks to him. The “heap of broken images” of line 22 has been traced to chapter 6, where the idols of Israel are to be destroyed (“your images shall be broken”), and the dry bones of line 391 to chapter 37, where the bones are joined, clothed with flesh and made to live.

### source isaiah
TITLE: Isaiah 32 and 38
AUTHOR: The Bible (King James Version)
DATE: 8th century BC; translation 1611
WHAT: “the shadow of a great rock in a weary land”; “Set thine house in order.”
LINES: 25, 426
NOTE: Eliot’s notes do not cite Isaiah. The “shadow of a great rock in a weary land” of chapter 32 has long been recognised behind the “shadow under this red rock” of line 25; in Isaiah the shadow is promised together with “rivers of water in a dry place,” and the red rock of the poem gives shade but no water. In chapter 38 King Hezekiah, told to set his house in order because he will die, turns his face to the wall and weeps, and is granted fifteen more years. At line 426 the speaker, fishing on the shore, asks “Shall I at least set my lands in order?” and gets no answer; Eliot’s note on the line before cites Weston’s chapter on the Fisher King.
