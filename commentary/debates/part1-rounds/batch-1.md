# Batch 1: glosses on the title, epigraph, dedication and lines 1–30

Each item below is one piece of commentary exactly as it stands in the site's source files. Review every one. Keep the ids exactly.

---
### title  (kind: gloss; line 0; hangs on the words “The Waste Land”; library source: weston)
TITLE: Where the title comes from
BODY:
Eliot's first note says it plainly: the title, "the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism" came from Jessie L. Weston's *From Ritual to Romance* (1920). Weston's book argues that the Grail romances preserve, under a Christian skin, a much older fertility cult: a land laid waste because its king is wounded or impotent, healed only when a stranger arrives and asks the right question. The waste land is the country, the king, and the reader who has not yet asked anything.

In the typescript the poem had a different working title, written over the first two parts: *He Do the Police in Different Voices*. It is Betty Higden's praise of the boy Sloppy in Dickens's *Our Mutual Friend* (1865): "You mightn't think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices." A poem that reads the news of the world aloud in as many voices as it can do. Keep that title in your head; it explains more than the Grail does.

### epigraph  (kind: echo; line 0; hangs on the words “Nam Sibyllam”; library source: petronius)
TITLE: The Sibyl in the bottle
QUOTE (source passage shown above the note): “Nam Sibyllam quidem Cumis ego ipse oculis meis vidi in ampulla pendere, et cum illi pueri dicerent: Σίβυλλα τί θέλεις; respondebat illa: ἀποθανεῖν θέλω.”
TRANSLATION (shown under the quote): “For I myself, with my own eyes, saw the Sibyl at Cumae hanging in a jar, and when the boys said to her, ‘Sibyl, what do you want?’ she answered, ‘I want to die.’”
BODY:
The speaker is Trimalchio, the vulgar millionaire host of the dinner party in Petronius's *Satyricon* (first century AD), boasting between courses. The Sibyl of Cumae had asked Apollo for as many years of life as there were grains in a handful of sand, and forgot to ask for youth. She shrank until she could be kept in a bottle. Her wish is the poem's first word on the subject of living on after the point of living has gone. Latin, then Greek, then the poem's English: three languages before the first line, and a handful of sand before the "handful of dust" at line 30.

This was not the first epigraph. The typescript carried Kurtz's dying words from Conrad's *Heart of Darkness*: "Did he live his life again in every detail of desire, temptation, and surrender during that supreme moment of complete knowledge? He cried in a whisper at some image, at some vision,—he cried out twice, a cry that was no more than a breath—'The horror! the horror!'" Pound wrote in January 1922 that he doubted "if Conrad is weighty enough to stand the citation." Eliot answered that it was "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," and then replaced it anyway.

### dedication  (kind: echo; line 0; hangs on the words “il miglior fabbro”; library source: dante-purg26)
TITLE: The better craftsman
QUOTE (source passage shown above the note): “O frate,” disse, “questi ch’io ti cerno / col dito,” e additò un spirto innanzi, / “fu miglior fabbro del parlar materno.”
TRANSLATION (shown under the quote): “O brother,” he said, “this one I point out to you with my finger,” and he pointed to a spirit ahead, “was a better craftsman of the mother tongue.”
BODY:
*Purgatorio* XXVI, line 117. The poet Guido Guinizelli, doing penance among the lustful in the refining fire, points Dante toward Arnaut Daniel, the Provençal troubadour, and calls him the better smith of his native speech. The same canto supplies the poem's last page: "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina" at line 428 is Arnaut stepping back into the flames.

The dedication is not in the 1922 printings. Eliot wrote it by hand in Pound's copy of the Boni & Liveright edition in January 1923 and first printed it in *Poems 1909–1925*. It credits the cutting, not the writing: Pound worked over the typescript in Paris in January 1922 and by post afterwards, and roughly halved it. His verdict, in a letter dated in his own invented calendar and read as either 24 December 1921 or 24 January 1922: "Complimenti, you bitch. I am wracked by the seven jealousies."

### april  (kind: echo; line 1–7; hangs on the words “April is the cruellest month”; library source: chaucer)
TITLE: Chaucer's April, turned inside out
QUOTE (source passage shown above the note): Whan that Aprill with his shoures soote / The droghte of March hath perced to the roote, / And bathed every veyne in swich licour / Of which vertu engendred is the flour . . .
TRANSLATION (shown under the quote): When April with its sweet showers has pierced the drought of March to the root, and bathed every vein in such liquid as has the power to engender the flower . . .
BODY:
The most famous opening in English poetry says that April is kind: rain ends the drought, sap rises, people want to go on pilgrimages. Eliot's first line answers it. Rain is still there ("stirring / Dull roots with spring rain"), but it is unwelcome, because it stirs "memory and desire" in something that would rather stay numb. Winter was kinder: "forgetful snow," "a little life with dried tubers." Read the first seven lines aloud and notice how five of them end on a present participle left hanging, breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding, so that the verbs never quite arrive at their objects. Growth that will not finish.

Behind Chaucer stand two other Aprils. Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" (1865) mourns Lincoln with lilacs, spring, and a hermit thrush, and that thrush turns up at line 357 of this poem. And Rupert Brooke, dead in April 1915, whose "The Old Vicarage, Grantchester" opens with the lilac in bloom. The line's "us" is a group that never gets named. It might be the leisured Europeans of the next paragraph, or the dead, or you.

### boston-cut  (kind: draft; line 1; hangs on the words “April”)
TITLE: What came before "April"
BODY:
In the typescript, "April is the cruellest month" was not the first line. It was preceded by fifty-four lines of a night on the town in Boston, in the voice of a young man out with his friends: a bar, a burlesque show, a drunken run from a policeman, a sunrise. The whole passage was struck through with a single pencil line, apparently by Eliot himself before Pound saw it. What survived is the second beginning, a poem that starts already exhausted. The manuscript is at the New York Public Library's Berg Collection and is reproduced in the 1971 facsimile edited by Valerie Eliot.

### starnbergersee  (kind: place; line 8–11; hangs on the words “Starnbergersee”)
TITLE: Munich, August 1911
BODY:
The Starnberger See is the long lake south of Munich; the Hofgarten is the court garden in the city centre, with an arcaded walk on two sides, which is the "colonnade" the speakers shelter in. Eliot spent August 1911 in Munich, at the end of his year in Paris, and finished "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" there. The lake is also where King Ludwig II of Bavaria, Wagner's patron and the builder of Neuschwanstein, was found drowned in June 1886, a death by water for the king who paid for *Tristan und Isolde*. The poem never says so. It only stops for coffee.

### marie  (kind: gloss; line 15–18; hangs on the words “Marie”)
TITLE: Countess Marie Larisch
BODY:
Valerie Eliot, in the facsimile edition, reported that her husband had met Countess Marie Larisch (1858–1940), a niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria, and that the sledding story and the tone of these lines came from her conversation, not from any book. Larisch had been the go-between in the affair of her cousin Crown Prince Rudolf and Mary Vetsera, which ended with both dead at Mayerling in 1889; her memoir *My Past* (1913) made her notorious. "The archduke's, my cousin's" fits Rudolf. What the poem takes from her is a voice: a displaced aristocrat of a vanished empire, at once frightened and free, reading much of the night. "In the mountains, there you feel free" is the poem's first quotation of someone who is not sure they mean it.

### litauen  (kind: tongue; line 12; hangs on the words “Bin gar keine Russin, stamm’ aus Litauen, echt deutsch.”)
TITLE: A café sentence
TRANSLATION (shown under the quote): I am not Russian at all; I come from Lithuania, real German.
BODY:
Overheard, unattributed, in a language the reader may not have. After 1918 the old multinational empires had become new nation states, and a woman from the Baltic gentry could be Russian by passport, Lithuanian by birthplace and German by blood, and need to say so quickly. The poem's first foreign sentence is about identity, and it is defensive.

### son-of-man  (kind: echo; line 20–24; hangs on the words “Son of man”; library source: ezekiel)
TITLE: The prophet's address
QUOTE (source passage shown above the note): And he said unto me, Son of man, stand upon thy feet, and I will speak unto thee.
BODY:
Eliot's note points to Ezekiel 2:1, where God addresses the prophet as "son of man" before sending him to a rebellious people. The next chapter has the prophet eat a scroll; chapter 37 has him prophesy over a valley of dry bones until they stand up as an army ("Dry bones can harm no one," says line 391, with the same book in mind). "A heap of broken images" is Ezekiel too: the Lord promises to break the idols of Israel (6:4–6). The voice that speaks here is not the café voice of Marie. It is the one Eliot would use again in "Ash-Wednesday" and the choruses of *The Rock*, the desert voice, and it has been given a stony landscape, a "red rock," and a question with no answer: what grows here?

Eliot's second note, on line 23, sends you to Ecclesiastes 12:5, the chapter on old age where "the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail: because man goeth to his long home, and the mourners go about the streets." Cricket, grasshopper: the same small dry noise.

### red-rock  (kind: draft; line 25–30; hangs on the words “this red rock”)
TITLE: Eliot borrowing from himself
BODY:
"Come in under the shadow of this red rock" is lifted, almost word for word, from a poem Eliot wrote in 1915 and withdrew from publication, "The Death of Saint Narcissus," where the invitation is to come in under the shadow of a gray rock and be shown something different from two shadows, one sprawling over the sand at daybreak, the other leaping behind a fire against a red rock. The saint's grey rock has turned red. The two shadows, morning and evening, are a sundial reading of a whole life, and the line that follows, "I will show you fear in a handful of dust," carries Isaiah's "shadow of a great rock in a weary land" (32:2), the Sibyl's handful of sand, and Tennyson's *Maud*: "Dead, long dead, / Long dead! / And my heart is a handful of dust."
