### source chaucer  (lines 1)
TITLE: The Canterbury Tales, General Prologue
AUTHOR: Geoffrey Chaucer   DATE: c. 1387   LANGUAGE: Middle English   KIND: poem
WHAT: The kind April that Eliot's first line contradicts.
PASSAGE: Whan that Aprill with his shoures soote / The droghte of March hath perced to the roote, / And bathed every veyne in swich licour / Of which vertu engendred is the flour; / Whan Zephirus eek with his sweete breeth / Inspired hath in every holt and heeth / The tendre croppes, and the yonge sonne / Hath in the Ram his half cours yronne, / And smale foweles maken melodye, / That slepen al the nyght with open ye / (So priketh hem Nature in hir corages), / Thanne longen folk to goon on pilgrimages.
TRANSLATION: When April with its sweet showers has pierced the drought of March to the root and bathed every vein in the liquid whose power engenders the flower; when Zephyrus with his sweet breath has quickened the tender shoots in every wood and heath, and the young sun has run half his course in the Ram, and small birds make melody that sleep all night with open eye (so Nature pricks them in their hearts), then people long to go on pilgrimages.
NOTE:
English poetry's first great spring, in which rain ends drought, sap rises and the whole country wants to travel. Eliot's poem also ends in a kind of pilgrimage, to the Chapel Perilous, but its April breeds lilacs out of dead land and its people would rather have stayed under the snow.

### source ecclesiastes  (lines 23)
TITLE: Ecclesiastes 12
AUTHOR: The Bible (King James Version)   DATE: 3rd century BC; translation 1611   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: scripture
WHAT: "the cricket no relief": the chapter on old age and death.
PASSAGE: Also when they shall be afraid of that which is high, and fears shall be in the way, and the almond tree shall flourish, and the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail: because man goeth to his long home, and the mourners go about the streets: or ever the silver cord be loosed, or the golden bowl be broken, or the pitcher be broken at the fountain, or the wheel broken at the cistern. Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it.
NOTE:
Eliot's note sends the reader to verse 5, where "the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail." The rest of the chapter, with its broken pitcher, its dry cistern and its dust, could be a plan for Part V.

### source tristan  (lines 31, 42)
TITLE: Tristan und Isolde
AUTHOR: Richard Wagner   DATE: 1865 (first performed Munich)   LANGUAGE: German   KIND: opera
WHAT: The sailor's song (lines 31–34) and the shepherd's "Öd und leer das Meer" (line 42).
PASSAGE: Westwärts / schweift der Blick; / ostwärts / streicht das Schiff. / Frisch weht der Wind / der Heimat zu: / mein irisch Kind, / wo weilest du? / Sind’s deiner Seufzer Wehen, / die mir die Segel blähen? / Wehe, wehe, du Wind! / Weh, ach wehe, mein Kind! / Irische Maid, / du wilde, minnige Maid! /  / . . . /  / HIRT: Öd und leer das Meer!
TRANSLATION: Westward the gaze wanders; eastward the ship runs. Fresh blows the wind toward home: my Irish child, where do you linger? Is it the breath of your sighs that fills my sails? Woe, woe, you wind! Woe, ah woe, my child! Irish maid, you wild, lovely maid! . . . SHEPHERD: Desolate and empty the sea!
NOTE:
Eliot's note cites the opera by act and verse as if it were a book, and it was: the libretto was sold and read. The young sailor's song is the first thing sung in Act I, from the masthead, unseen, above the deck where Isolde lies; the shepherd's four words in Act III answer Kurwenal's question whether any ship is in sight while Tristan lies dying. Between the two quotations the poem puts its hyacinth garden, which is to say that the garden happens between a ship setting out and a ship not arriving. Eliot heard *Tristan* in Boston in 1909, as a Harvard senior, and wrote a poem called "Opera" about it; Stravinsky, who met him in 1956, came away thinking that *Tristan* "must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life." Ludwig II, who paid for the first performance, drowned in the Starnberger See of line 8.

### source baudelaire-sept-vieillards  (lines 60)
TITLE: Les Sept Vieillards (from Les Fleurs du mal)
AUTHOR: Charles Baudelaire   DATE: 1859; in the 1861 edition of Les Fleurs du mal   LANGUAGE: French   KIND: poem
WHAT: "Unreal City."
PASSAGE: Fourmillante cité, cité pleine de rêves, / Où le spectre en plein jour raccroche le passant! / Les mystères partout coulent comme des sèves / Dans les canaux étroits du colosse puissant.
TRANSLATION: Swarming city, city full of dreams, where the ghost in broad daylight accosts the passer-by! Mysteries everywhere flow like sap in the narrow channels of the mighty giant.
NOTE:
The poet, walking through a yellow fog in a Paris morning, meets a hideous old man, then another exactly like him, and another, seven in all, and flees home half mad. Eliot's note quotes only the first two lines, and the whole "Unreal City" passage is built on their method: a real street, and a ghost on it at nine in the morning.

### source baudelaire-au-lecteur  (lines 76)
TITLE: Au Lecteur (the preface poem of Les Fleurs du mal)
AUTHOR: Charles Baudelaire   DATE: 1857   LANGUAGE: French   KIND: poem
WHAT: "hypocrite lecteur!—mon semblable,—mon frère!"
PASSAGE: C’est l’Ennui! — l’œil chargé d’un pleur involontaire, / Il rêve d’échafauds en fumant son houka. / Tu le connais, lecteur, ce monstre délicat, / — Hypocrite lecteur, — mon semblable, — mon frère!
TRANSLATION: It is Boredom! Its eye charged with an involuntary tear, it dreams of scaffolds while smoking its hookah. You know it, reader, this delicate monster, hypocrite reader, my double, my brother!
NOTE:
Baudelaire opens his book by listing the sins of mankind, saving the worst for last, boredom, and then turning on whoever is reading. Eliot closes his first section the same way. He said in 1930 that Baudelaire was "the greatest exemplar in modern poetry in any language," and in 1950 that he had learned from him "the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric."

### source death-of-st-narcissus  (lines 25)
TITLE: The Death of Saint Narcissus
AUTHOR: T. S. Eliot   DATE: 1915 (withdrawn from publication)   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: poem
WHAT: "Come in under the shadow of this red rock."
PASSAGE: Come under the shadow of this gray rock— / Come in under the shadow of this gray rock,
NOTE:
Set in type for *Poetry* (Chicago) in 1915 and withdrawn at proof stage, the poem about a saint who wanted to be a tree, a fish and a girl, and died pierced with arrows, survived among the manuscripts Eliot sent to John Quinn in 1922 along with the *Waste Land* drafts. Its opening goes on, in the cadence the later poem keeps, to promise the listener something other than his own shadow on the sand or against the fire: the saint's bloody cloth and limbs. The gray rock's shadow, made red, became the poem's first prophecy. The poem is still in copyright, so only its first two lines are given here.

### source huxley-crome-yellow  (lines 43)
TITLE: Crome Yellow, chapter 27
AUTHOR: Aldous Huxley   DATE: 1921   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: novel
WHAT: "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana": the name behind Madame Sosostris.
PASSAGE: Mr. Scogan had been accommodated in a little canvas hut. Dressed in a black skirt and a red bodice, with a yellow-and-red bandana handkerchief tied round his black wig, he looked—sharp-nosed, brown, and wrinkled—like the Bohemian Hag of Frith’s Derby Day. A placard pinned to the curtain of the doorway announced the presence within the tent of “Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana.”
NOTE:
Huxley's country-house satire came out in November 1921, while Eliot was at Margate and Lausanne; the cynical Mr. Scogan telling fortunes in drag at a village fête is the immediate source of the "famous clairvoyante." Huxley had been at Garsington with Eliot, and the two knew each other's work.

### source dante-inf3  (lines 62, 63, 56)
TITLE: Inferno, canto III
AUTHOR: Dante Alighieri   DATE: c. 1308   LANGUAGE: Italian   KIND: poem
WHAT: The crowd on London Bridge: "I had not thought death had undone so many."
PASSAGE: E io, che riguardai, vidi una ’nsegna / che girando correva tanto ratta, / che d’ogne posa mi parea indegna; / e dietro le venìa sì lunga tratta / di gente, ch’i’ non averei creduto / che morte tanta n’avesse disfatta.
TRANSLATION: And I, who looked, saw a banner which, whirling, ran so fast that it seemed to scorn all pause; and behind it came so long a train of people that I would never have believed death had undone so many.
NOTE:
Inside the gate ("Abandon all hope") but before the river Acheron, Dante finds the souls who were neither good nor bad, "who lived without infamy and without praise," rejected by Heaven and refused by Hell, running forever after a banner and stung by wasps. Eliot's commuters, going round in a ring, are these. He said in 1950 that Dante's poetry was still, "after forty years," "the most persistent and deepest influence" on his own verse.

### source dante-inf4  (lines 64)
TITLE: Inferno, canto IV
AUTHOR: Dante Alighieri   DATE: c. 1308   LANGUAGE: Italian   KIND: poem
WHAT: "Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled."
PASSAGE: Quivi, secondo che per ascoltare, / non avea pianto mai che di sospiri / che l’aura etterna facevan tremare.
TRANSLATION: Here, as far as I could tell by listening, there was no weeping but only sighs, which made the eternal air tremble.
NOTE:
Limbo, the first circle, home of the virtuous who died unbaptised: Homer, Virgil, Aristotle, the "great-hearted" souls who are not tormented but live in desire without hope. One line of the poem, one canto of Dante: the office crowd sigh like the noble pagans, not like the damned.

### source ezekiel  (lines 20, 22, 391)
TITLE: Ezekiel 2 and 37
AUTHOR: The Bible (King James Version)   DATE: 6th century BC; translation 1611   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: scripture
WHAT: "Son of man"; "a heap of broken images"; the valley of dry bones.
PASSAGE: And he said unto me, Son of man, stand upon thy feet, and I will speak unto thee. (2:1) /  / The hand of the LORD was upon me, and carried me out in the spirit of the LORD, and set me down in the midst of the valley which was full of bones, and caused me to pass by them round about: and, behold, there were very many in the open valley; and, lo, they were very dry. And he said unto me, Son of man, can these bones live? And I answered, O Lord GOD, thou knowest. (37:1–3)
NOTE:
Ezekiel, prophet of the Babylonian exile, is addressed by God ninety-odd times as "son of man," which is to say mortal. Eliot's note points to the moment of his commission; the poem's dry stone, broken images and dry bones come from the same book (chapters 6 and 37). In the valley the bones stand up. In the poem they do not.

### source isaiah  (lines 25, 426)
TITLE: Isaiah 32 and 38
AUTHOR: The Bible (King James Version)   DATE: 8th century BC; translation 1611   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: scripture
WHAT: "the shadow of a great rock in a weary land"; "Set thine house in order."
PASSAGE: And a man shall be as an hiding place from the wind, and a covert from the tempest; as rivers of water in a dry place, as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land. (32:2) /  / In those days was Hezekiah sick unto death. And Isaiah the prophet the son of Amoz came unto him, and said unto him, Thus saith the LORD, Set thine house in order: for thou shalt die, and not live. (38:1)
NOTE:
The red rock of Part I offers the prophet's shelter without the prophet's promise of water. Hezekiah, told to set his house in order, turned his face to the wall, wept, and was given fifteen more years. The Fisher King of line 426 asks the question and gets no answer.
