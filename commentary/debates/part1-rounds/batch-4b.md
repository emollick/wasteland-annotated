### source tennyson  (lines 30, 429)
TITLE: Maud, and The Princess
AUTHOR: Alfred Tennyson   DATE: 1855; 1847   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: poem
WHAT: "a handful of dust"; "O swallow swallow."
PASSAGE: Dead, long dead, / Long dead! / And my heart is a handful of dust, / And the wheels go over my head, / And my bones are shaken with pain, / For into a shallow grave they are thrust, / Only a yard beneath the street . . . (Maud, II.v) /  / O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South, / Fly to her, and fall upon her gilded eaves, / And tell her, tell her, what I tell to thee. (The Princess)
NOTE:
The mad speaker of *Maud*, who imagines himself buried alive under a city street with the traffic going over him, is a Victorian ancestor of the man in rats' alley; the swallow song in *The Princess* gives Eliot the English half of a Latin line. Eliot's respect for Tennyson's ear ("the finest ear of any English poet since Milton," he wrote in 1936) shows in what he took.

### source whitman-lilacs  (lines 2, 357)
TITLE: When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd
AUTHOR: Walt Whitman   DATE: 1865   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: poem
WHAT: Lilacs, spring, death, and a hermit thrush.
PASSAGE: When lilacs last in the dooryard bloom’d, / And the great star early droop’d in the western sky in the night, / I mourn’d, and yet shall mourn with ever-returning spring. . . . /  / In the swamp in secluded recesses, / A shy and hidden bird is warbling a song. / Solitary the thrush, / The hermit withdrawn to himself, avoiding the settlements, / Sings by himself a song. / Song of the bleeding throat, / Death’s outlet song of life . . .
NOTE:
Whitman's elegy for Lincoln, shot in April 1865, binds three things together: the lilac, the evening star, and the hermit thrush singing in the swamp. Eliot's poem opens on lilacs out of dead land and, three hundred and fifty lines later, longs for the hermit-thrush's "water-dripping song." He rarely admitted to reading Whitman. The lilacs and the thrush say otherwise.

### source webster-white-devil  (lines 74, 408)
TITLE: The White Devil
AUTHOR: John Webster   DATE: 1612   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: play
WHAT: Cornelia's dirge ("keep the wolf far thence") and Flamineo on the spider and the winding-sheet.
PASSAGE: Call for the robin-redbreast and the wren, / Since o’er shady groves they hover, / And with leaves and flowers do cover / The friendless bodies of unburied men. / Call unto his funeral dole / The ant, the field-mouse, and the mole, / To rear him hillocks that shall keep him warm, / And (when gay tombs are robb’d) sustain no harm; / But keep the wolf far thence, that’s foe to men, / For with his nails he’ll dig them up again. (V.iv) /  / O men, / That lie upon your death-beds, and are haunted / With howling wives! ne’er trust them; they’ll re-marry / Ere the worm pierce your winding-sheet, ere the spider / Make a thin curtain for your epitaphs. (V.vi)
NOTE:
Webster's tragedy of Vittoria Corombona, the "white devil," and her murderous lover Brachiano. Cornelia sings the dirge over her son Marcello, killed by his brother Flamineo; Flamineo gives the speech about widows as he rises from a feigned death, having just learned that his sister would have let him die. Eliot's essays on the Elizabethan dramatists and his poem "Whispers of Immortality" ("Webster was much possessed by death / And saw the skull beneath the skin") explain why these two speeches were in his head.

### source shakespeare-tempest  (lines 48, 125, 191, 257)
TITLE: The Tempest, Act I scene ii
AUTHOR: William Shakespeare   DATE: c. 1611   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: play
WHAT: Ariel's song, "Those are pearls that were his eyes"; Ferdinand on the bank; "This music crept by me upon the waters."
PASSAGE: FERDINAND: Where should this music be? i’ th’ air or th’ earth? / It sounds no more: and sure, it waits upon / Some god o’ th’ island. Sitting on a bank, / Weeping again the king my father’s wreck, / This music crept by me upon the waters, / Allaying both their fury and my passion / With its sweet air . . . /  / ARIEL (sings): Full fathom five thy father lies; / Of his bones are coral made; / Those are pearls that were his eyes: / Nothing of him that doth fade / But doth suffer a sea-change / Into something rich and strange. / Sea-nymphs hourly ring his knell: / Ding-dong. / Hark! now I hear them,—Ding-dong, bell.
NOTE:
Ferdinand, washed ashore after the shipwreck, believes his father Alonso drowned; Ariel's song tells him so, beautifully and falsely. Alonso is alive, and by the end of the play everyone has suffered a sea-change for the better. Eliot quotes the song twice and Ferdinand's speech twice, and never allows the change. The drowned father with pearls for eyes becomes the drowned Phoenician and Phlebas; Ferdinand weeping on the bank becomes the Fisher King by the gasworks.

### source frazer  (lines 46, 71, 312)
TITLE: The Golden Bough (Adonis, Attis, Osiris)
AUTHOR: Sir James George Frazer   DATE: 1906–15 (third edition); one-volume abridgement 1922   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: anthropology
WHAT: The dying and reviving god; the buried corpse that sprouts; the Hanged God; the effigy thrown into the sea.
NOTE:
Frazer's twelve-volume comparative study of magic and religion, which Eliot's headnote says "has influenced our generation profoundly," argued that behind the myths of Adonis, Attis, Osiris and, by implication, Christ lay a single agricultural drama: the god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped along by ritual (a sacrificed king, an effigy buried or drowned). The volumes Eliot names describe the "gardens of Adonis," pots of quick-sprouting seed thrown into the sea, and the effigies of Osiris "made of earth and corn," buried to sprout. Stetson's planted corpse, Phlebas in the current, the Hanged Man and the whole question of whether April can bring anything back are Frazer's. The book's title and its opening page come from Turner's painting *The Golden Bough* (1834), which Frazer took for a view of Nemi; it shows the Sibyl at Lake Avernus.

### source weston  (lines 0, 46, 386, 424)
TITLE: From Ritual to Romance
AUTHOR: Jessie L. Weston   DATE: 1920   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: anthropology
WHAT: The title, the plan, the Fisher King, the Chapel Perilous, the Syrian merchants.
NOTE:
Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance who came to believe, under the influence of Frazer, that the Grail stories preserved the remains of an ancient fertility cult: a wounded king, a land gone waste, a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics, and a ritual of initiation whose secrets travelled Europe with Syrian merchants. Almost no medievalist now accepts the argument. It does not matter. Eliot's note says the book suggested "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism," and thirty-four years later he said he regretted "having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail." Read chapters II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel) and you have the poem's skeleton; read the rest and you have a very good bad book.

### source petronius  (lines 0)
TITLE: Satyricon, chapter 48
AUTHOR: Petronius   DATE: 1st century AD   LANGUAGE: Latin, with Greek   KIND: prose
WHAT: The epigraph: the Sibyl who wanted to die.
PASSAGE: Nam Sibyllam quidem Cumis ego ipse oculis meis vidi in ampulla pendere, et cum illi pueri dicerent: Σίβυλλα τί θέλεις; respondebat illa: ἀποθανεῖν θέλω.
TRANSLATION: For I myself, with my own eyes, saw the Sibyl at Cumae hanging in a jar, and when the boys said to her, "Sibyl, what do you want?" she answered, "I want to die."
NOTE:
The speaker is Trimalchio, the freed slave turned millionaire whose dinner party is the surviving centre of the *Satyricon*, showing off between courses. The Sibyl of Cumae, in Ovid's telling, asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand and forgot to ask for youth, and shrank with age until only her voice was left. A prophetess kept as a curiosity; a poem introduced by a fat man's boast.

### source dante-purg26  (lines 0, 428)
TITLE: Purgatorio, canto XXVI
AUTHOR: Dante Alighieri   DATE: c. 1315   LANGUAGE: Italian and Provençal   KIND: poem
WHAT: "il miglior fabbro" (the dedication) and "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina" (line 428).
PASSAGE: «Tan m’abellis vostre cortes deman, / qu’ieu no me puesc ni voill a vos cobrire. / Ieu sui Arnaut, que plor e vau cantan; / consiros vei la passada folor, / e vei jausen lo joi qu’esper, denan. / Ara vos prec, per aquella valor / que vos guida al som de l’escalina, / sovenha vos a temps de ma dolor!». / Poi s’ascose nel foco che li affina.
TRANSLATION: "So much does your courteous question please me that I cannot and will not hide myself from you. I am Arnaut, who weep and go singing; in thought I see my past folly, and I see with joy, ahead, the joy I hope for. Now I pray you, by that power which guides you to the top of the stair, be mindful in time of my pain." Then he hid himself in the fire that refines them.
NOTE:
On the seventh terrace of Purgatory, where the lustful walk in flame, Dante meets Guido Guinizelli, who points out Arnaut Daniel as "miglior fabbro del parlar materno," the better craftsman of the mother tongue (line 117). Arnaut answers in his own Provençal, the only passage in the *Comedy* in a vernacular other than Italian, and steps back into the fire. Eliot took the dedication to Pound from line 117, the title of his 1920 collection *Ara Vos Prec* from line 145, and line 428 of the poem from line 148. Modern texts read "li affina"; Eliot's "gli" follows the editions he owned.

### source dickens-omf  (lines 0)
TITLE: Our Mutual Friend, Book I chapter 16
AUTHOR: Charles Dickens   DATE: 1865   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: novel
WHAT: The typescript's title: "He Do the Police in Different Voices."
PASSAGE: “I do love a newspaper. You mightn’t think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices.”
NOTE:
Betty Higden's praise of the foundling Sloppy, who reads her the police-court reports doing all the parts. Eliot wrote the sentence across the top of the typescript of Parts I and II. It is the plainest statement of what the poem does that anyone has made, including its author, and Dickens's novel, which opens with a body fished out of the Thames, is the poem's river in prose.

### source conrad-hod  (lines 0)
TITLE: Heart of Darkness
AUTHOR: Joseph Conrad   DATE: 1899   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: novella
WHAT: The epigraph the poem almost had.
PASSAGE: Did he live his life again in every detail of desire, temptation, and surrender during that supreme moment of complete knowledge? He cried in a whisper at some image, at some vision,—he cried out twice, a cry that was no more than a breath— / “The horror! The horror!”
NOTE:
Kurtz's last words, as Marlow reports them, stood at the head of the typescript. Pound doubted whether Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation"; Eliot defended it as "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," then replaced it with Petronius. The "heart of light" at line 41 still answers to it. The book's opening, a boat on the Thames at Gravesend at dusk with London behind it, is worth rereading with Part III in mind.

### source eliot-frontiers  (lines 0)
TITLE: The Frontiers of Criticism (Eliot on his own notes)
AUTHOR: T. S. Eliot   DATE: 1956   LANGUAGE: English   KIND: essay
WHAT: What Eliot later said about the notes.
PASSAGE: . . . the remarkable exposition of bogus scholarship that is still on view to-day. . . . I regret having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail.
NOTE:
A lecture given at the University of Minnesota in 1956 to an audience of nearly fourteen thousand, in which Eliot, thirty-four years on, described the notes as padding that had got out of hand: he had meant only to list his references, to spike the guns of critics who had called him a plagiarist, and then, when the poem proved too short to make a book on its own, expanded them to fill pages. He also admitted they could not now be removed: readers had "bought" the notes with the poem. Take the notes as part of the poem and as a joke at its expense; both are true.
