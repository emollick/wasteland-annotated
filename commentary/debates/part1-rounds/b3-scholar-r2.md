### stetson
Verdict: not yet

1. "A correspondent in the <i>Times Literary Supplement</i> in 1973 reported Eliot as saying ..." The correspondent was Valerie Eliot. Donald Childs, "Stetson in The Waste Land", <i>Essays in Criticism</i> 38 (1988), quotes the letter as hers: "Valerie Eliot, for instance, responding to speculation that Stetson might be Ezra Pound, writes: '"Stetson" is not Ezra Pound. Eliot said he was not referring to anyone in particular, but simply meant any superior bank clerk: a person in a bowler hat, black jacket and striped trousers'" (the same words are in the TLS of 11 May 1973, in the correspondence on "The Waste Land as a Buddhist Poem"). A report by his widow carries different weight from one by a stranger, and the note should say whose it is. Put: "Valerie Eliot, answering that identification in a letter to the <i>Times Literary Supplement</i> (11 May 1973), wrote that Eliot was not referring to anyone in particular but "simply meant any superior bank clerk", a man in a bowler hat, black jacket and striped trousers."
2. "Other suggestions are the slouch hat of the Australian troops at Gallipoli, and Kipling's bank clerk Charlie Mears, who remembers rowing in an ancient galley." Neither suggestion carries its source, and Southam's sentence names no battle: the felt slouch hats "(a version of the stetson) were the characteristic and identifying head-gear of Australian and New Zealand soldiers in the First World War" (<i>Guide</i>, 6th ed., note on l. 69). The Kipling connection is Robert Crawford's (<i>The Savage and the City in the Work of T. S. Eliot</i>, 1987) and Childs's (1988). Put: "Southam's <i>Guide</i> also records the felt slouch hat, a kind of stetson, worn by the Australian and New Zealand troops of 1914–18; Robert Crawford (1987) and Donald Childs (1988) connect the name with Kipling's bank clerk Charlie Mears, in "The Finest Story in the World", who remembers rowing in an ancient galley."

### corpse
Verdict: not yet

1. "describes two rites of a god who dies and returns." Adonis and Osiris are two gods, treated by Frazer as parallel figures, not one; as written the sentence makes the gardens and the effigies rites of the same god. Put: "describes two rites of gods who die and return."

### dog
Verdict: not yet

1. "Eliot alters the couplet in five places." Six. My round-one count treated "Oh" as an addition; it replaces Webster's "But". Webster: "But keep the wolf far thence, that's foe to men, / For with his nails he'll dig them up again" (Gutenberg #12915, V.iv; the site's quote has the same words). Eliot: "Oh keep the Dog far hence, that's friend to men, / Or with his nails he'll dig it up again!" The words that differ are But/Oh, wolf/Dog, thence/hence, foe/friend, For/Or, them/it. Put: "Eliot alters the couplet in six places. Webster's "But keep the wolf far thence" becomes "Oh keep the Dog far hence", the Dog with a capital; "foe to men" becomes "friend to men"; "For" becomes "Or", and "them" becomes "it"."

### hypocrite-lecteur
Verdict: not yet

1. "In the 1922 text the line is still Stetson's, inside the quotation marks and opening with 'You!'" The wording is mine from round one and it is wrong: the speech of lines 69–76 is the speaker's, addressed to Stetson ("stopped him, crying: 'Stetson! / You who were with me in the ships at Mylae!'"), so the line is spoken to Stetson, not by him. The original note had this right ("The 'you' that has been Stetson"). Put: "In the 1922 text the line is still spoken to Stetson, inside the quotation marks of the speech that began "Stetson!" and opening with "You!"; but Baudelaire's line is addressed to the reader, and a reader who knows it is addressed too."
