# Editor, after round one: batch 3 (glosses on lines 60–76)

Each item: the editor's decision, then the draft that goes to both sides for round two. Wording follows the reader where the two sides differed on wording; substance follows the scholar.

### unreal-city
Decision: trim, as both sides said. The 1950 quotation is cut to a phrase and given its source; "the recipe for the next sixteen lines" goes; "every working morning" becomes "from March 1917"; the Lloyds fact is left to the St Mary Woolnoth note, which carries it in full.
TITLE: Baudelaire's swarming city
BODY:
The opening of "Les Sept Vieillards" ("The Seven Old Men"), in the <i>Tableaux parisiens</i> section of <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i>. The poet, walking through a dirty yellow fog, meets the same hideous old man seven times over. In a talk on Dante in 1950 ("What Dante Means to Me") Eliot said that Baudelaire had shown him what poetry could make of the modern city, the fusion of "the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric".

The brown fog is London's coal-smoke fog. Eliot went in to the City through it from March 1917, when he joined Lloyds Bank in Lombard Street. "Unreal City" returns at line 207, under the same fog at noon, and as the single word "Unreal" at line 377.

### london-bridge-dead
Decision: rewrite. "Damned twice over" and "born too early" go, as the scholar showed they misstate Dante; the half-hour goes; the crowd is described in Eliot's own 1950 words, which the scholar supplied; the parallel with the neutrals is left for the reader to draw from the facts, as the scholar preferred, rather than stated as a reading.
TITLE: The crowd at the gate of Hell
BODY:
<i>Inferno</i> III, 55–57. Just inside the gate of Hell, before the river Acheron, Dante sees the souls of those who lived "without infamy and without praise" (III, 36), refused by Heaven and by Hell alike, and is astonished at how many there are. Line 64, "Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled," comes from the next canto, Limbo, where the unbaptised and the virtuous pagans live without hope, in desire, and there is no weeping, only sighs that make the eternal air tremble (IV, 25–42). Eliot said in 1950 that he had altered Dante's line on purpose, and that he gave the references in his notes so that a reader who recognised them would know he was meant to. In the same talk he called this crowd "my city clerks trooping over London Bridge from the railway station to their offices". He was one of them, at Lloyds Bank at the top of King William Street, from 1917 to 1925.

### woolnoth
Decision: trim. Title by the church, as the reader asked. "Struck the start of his working day" corrected by the scholar's 9.15; "driest joke" and the Tube sentence go; the reburial place corrected to Manor Park; the department dropped from the dates.
TITLE: St Mary Woolnoth
BODY:
King William Street runs up from London Bridge to the Bank, and at its top, on the corner of Lombard Street, stands Nicholas Hawksmoor's St Mary Woolnoth (1716–27), a squat church with two flat-topped turrets and its stone cut in deep bands. Lloyds Bank's head office, at 71 Lombard Street, faced the church across the street. Eliot worked there from March 1917 to November 1925; his hours, he told his mother in 1917, were "from 9.15 to 5". His note on line 68 says only: "A phenomenon which I have often noticed."

When the City and South London Railway built Bank station beneath the church in 1897–1900, the bodies in the crypt were reburied in the City of London Cemetery at Manor Park, and the crypt became the station's booking hall.

### stetson
Decision: rewrite. The trade-war and one-war claims are given to Brooks and Matthiessen, as the scholar established; the meeting moved off the bridge; the 1973 report of Eliot's denial added as a report; the other identifications given in one sentence, unendorsed; the hat sentence made plain.
TITLE: Stetson, and the ships at Mylae
BODY:
Mylae was the sea battle of 260 BC in which Rome first beat Carthage at sea, early in the First Punic War. F. O. Matthiessen identified the battle in 1935, and Cleanth Brooks in 1939 read the Punic War as a trade war and so a parallel to the war of 1914–18; on that reading, in Brooks's words, "all the wars are one war". The friend met in the City of London a few years after the Armistice was "in the ships" twenty-two centuries before. The meeting is in King William Street, where the crowd has just arrived, not on the bridge.

Stetson is an American name and the name of an American hat. He has often been taken for Pound, who wore a wide-brimmed hat; Southam's <i>Guide</i> records the identification. A correspondent in the <i>Times Literary Supplement</i> in 1973 reported Eliot as saying that he meant no one in particular, only a superior bank clerk in a bowler hat. Other suggestions are the slouch hat of the Australian troops at Gallipoli, and Kipling's bank clerk Charlie Mears, who remembers rowing in an ancient galley. Nothing in the poem needs him to be anyone in particular.

### corpse
Decision: rewrite. Frazer's own words replace "Osiris beds"; "suburban" and "the question of the whole poem" go; the headnote's promise about vegetation ceremonies added, as the scholar suggested; the bulbs sentence kept, both sides having allowed it.
TITLE: The buried god
BODY:
Frazer's <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, the two volumes Eliot's headnote names (with the title garbled), describes two rites of a god who dies and returns. The "gardens of Adonis" were pots of earth sown with quick-sprouting wheat and barley, tended for eight days, then carried out with the images of the dead god and thrown into the sea or into springs. Effigies of Osiris made of earth and corn were buried at the Egyptian festival of sowing and taken up when the grain had sprouted, and corn-stuffed figures of the god have been found in tombs; their sprouting, Frazer says, signified his resurrection. Eliot's headnote promises that readers of Frazer will "immediately recognise in the poem certain references to vegetation ceremonies". This is the plainest of them, asked in the voice of a man talking to a neighbour about his bulbs.

### dog
Decision: rewrite. The title's "one word" was wrong, as both sides noticed and the scholar counted; five changes listed; the Dog readings given to Brooks and to "some annotators"; the Massinger quotation kept in full (a 1920 text) without the sentence that tied it to a one-word change.
TITLE: Webster's dirge, altered
BODY:
Cornelia's song over her murdered son Marcello in Webster's <i>The White Devil</i> (1612), Act V scene iv, sung, the stage direction says, "in several forms of distraction". Eliot alters the couplet in five places. Webster's wolf becomes the Dog, with a capital; "foe to men" becomes "friend to men"; "thence" becomes "hence", "for" becomes "or", and "them" becomes "it". In Webster the wolf digs up the unburied dead; in Eliot the danger to the corpse in the garden is the dog, man's friend. Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the capitalised Dog as humanitarianism, the well-meant modern thing that digs up what should be left to lie; some annotators see the Dog Star, Sirius, whose rising marked the flooding of the Nile in the Egyptian calendar Frazer describes. Eliot's own account of such borrowing is in "Philip Massinger" (1920): "Immature poets imitate; mature poets steal; bad poets deface what they take, and good poets make it into something better, or at least something different."

### hypocrite-lecteur
Decision: trim. TITLE: The reader accused
TRANS: It is Boredom! Eye charged with an involuntary tear, he dreams of scaffolds while smoking his hookah. You know him, reader, this delicate monster, hypocrite reader, my likeness, my brother!
BODY:
The last line of "Au Lecteur", the prefatory poem of <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i> (1857), which Eliot's note calls its preface. Baudelaire's catalogue of vices ends with the ugliest of them, Ennui, and then turns on the reader as its accomplice. In the 1922 text the line is still Stetson's, inside the quotation marks and opening with "You!"; but Baudelaire's line is addressed to the reader, and a reader who knows it is addressed too. Eliot said in 1950 that he gave his references in the notes so that a reader who recognised an allusion would know he was meant to.
