# Batch 2, as it now stands (after round three)

### frisch-weht
TITLE: The sailor's song from the masthead
BODY:
These are the first words sung in Wagner's <i>Tristan und Isolde</i>. A young sailor sings them from high in the rigging, heard but not seen, as Tristan's ship carries Isolde from Ireland to Cornwall, where she is to marry King Marke, Tristan's uncle. The four lines before the ones Eliot quotes give the ship's course ("westward roams the gaze; eastward runs the ship"), so the wind that blows "toward home" blows toward the sailor's Cornwall and away from Isolde's Ireland. She takes the song as mockery; her first words in the opera are "Who dares to mock me?" Eliot's note gives the act and the verse numbers, 5 to 8, and nothing else. His second quotation from the opera, at line 42, comes from its last act.

### hyacinth-girl
TITLE: The hyacinth garden
BODY:
Hyacinthus was the boy Apollo loved and killed by accident with a discus. The flower sprang from his blood, and in Ovid its petals carry the letters AI AI, a cry of grief (<i>Metamorphoses</i> X, 162–219). Sparta kept a three-day festival for him at Amyclae, the Hyacinthia, which began with a day of mourning and ended in rejoicing. In <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, the volumes Eliot's headnote names, Frazer gives him a short chapter at the end of the Attis book. He writes there that Hyacinth "has been interpreted as the vegetation which blooms in spring and withers". None of this is needed to read the lines. The commentaries give it because Eliot's headnote promises readers of Frazer "certain references to vegetation ceremonies", and the hyacinth is usually counted as one. Annotators set "neither / Living nor dead" beside Dante's words at the sight of Satan, <i>Inferno</i> XXXIV 25: "Io non mori' e non rimasi vivo" (I did not die and did not stay alive). "The heart of light" has been read as an inversion of the title of Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, the book the typescript's epigraph came from (see the note on the epigraph). Eliot used the phrase again in the first section of <i>Burnt Norton</i> (1936).

Two people in Eliot's life have been proposed for the figure in the garden; the text names neither. Lyndall Gordon, from <i>Eliot's Early Years</i> (1977) to <i>The Hyacinth Girl</i> (2022), attaches it to Emily Hale of Boston, to whom Eliot declared his love before sailing for Europe in 1914; his 1,131 letters to her, opened at Princeton in January 2020, show how long the attachment lasted. John Peter's 1952 essay read the poem as an elegy for a dead friend, and his 1969 postscript named the friend as Jean Verdenal; James E. Miller (1977) made the same identification. Verdenal was the friend of Eliot's Paris year, killed at the Dardanelles in May 1915, to whom <i>Prufrock and Other Observations</i> (1917) is dedicated. In the <i>Criterion</i> for April 1934 Eliot recalled that friend crossing the Luxembourg Gardens "waving a branch of lilac", and his death in "the mud of Gallipoli". Compare the lilacs of line 2.

### oed-und-leer
TITLE: The shepherd's answer
BODY:
The words are from Act III of <i>Tristan und Isolde</i>. Tristan lies wounded at Kareol, his castle in Brittany, and his servant Kurwenal has sent to Cornwall for Isolde, the only one who can heal him. A shepherd keeps watch and is to play a merry tune the moment a sail appears; told to look, he looks out to sea and answers with these four words. Eliot's note, "Id. III, verse 24," points back to the note on line 31 ("Id." is <i>idem</i>, the same work), and the count is exact: the line is the twenty-fourth sung in the act. <i>Öd</i> is the German for desolate or waste, as in <i>Ödland</i>, waste land. This quotation and the one at line 31 stand on either side of the hyacinth garden. That one comes from the opening of the opera, before Tristan and Isolde drink the love potion; this one from its last act, when they are apart and Tristan is dying.

### sosostris
TITLE: The clairvoyante with a cold
BODY:
The name is usually traced to Aldous Huxley's <i>Crome Yellow</i>, published in November 1921, where Mr. Scogan, dressed as a gipsy woman, reads palms at the charity fair in the grounds of Crome under a placard announcing "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana". Sesostris was a legendary Egyptian king. Eliot, asked by Grover Smith in 1952, said he was "almost certain" he had taken the name from the novel without knowing it. Lawrence Rainey has since dated the typescript of Parts I and II to May 1921, before Huxley wrote the book, so the debt is in doubt. The spelling and the head cold are Eliot's own. "Clairvoyante," with the French feminine ending, is the trade term; "the wisest woman in Europe" is the trade puff. Eliot's note ties her cards to what follows: the Sailor, the Merchant and the crowds "appear later", and "Death by Water is executed in Part IV".

Her parting line, "One must be so careful these days" (line 59), is usually read as a fortune-teller's caution about the law. Section 4 of the Vagrancy Act 1824 made "every person pretending or professing to tell fortunes" a rogue and vagabond, and palmists and mediums were still being prosecuted under it in the 1920s.

### tarot
TITLE: The cards she deals
BODY:
Eliot's note says he was "not familiar with the exact constitution of the Tarot pack" and had "obviously departed" from it "to suit my own convenience". Weston, in her chapter on the Grail symbols, suggests that the Tarot's original use "would seem to have been, not to foretell the Future in general, but to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land". The cards, in the order Madame Sosostris names them, are these.

<b>The drowned Phoenician Sailor.</b> There is no such card in the pack. Eliot's note says the Sailor and the Merchant "appear later". The Sailor is Phlebas the Phoenician of Part IV, "Death by Water", already "a fortnight dead" when the section opens. His note on line 218 adds that the one-eyed merchant "melts into the Phoenician Sailor", who "is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples" in <i>The Tempest</i>. Line 48, the line in brackets, is from a song telling Ferdinand that his father has drowned; see the note there.

<b>Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks.</b> Belladonna is Italian for "beautiful lady". It is also the name of the deadly nightshade, and of the drops made from it that women used to widen their pupils. "The Lady of the Rocks" is usually taken to echo Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>.

<b>The man with three staves.</b> The Three of Wands, which Eliot calls "an authentic member of the Tarot pack". In the Rider pack a man stands at a cliff's edge with his back to us, watching ships. Eliot "quite arbitrarily" associated him with the Fisher King.

<b>The Wheel.</b> The Wheel of Fortune, card X. Compare "O you who turn the wheel" at line 320.

<b>The one-eyed merchant.</b> There is no such card in the pack. Eliot's note says the Merchant appears later; he is Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant, at line 209. Cleanth Brooks (1939) took "one-eyed", on the card, to mean no more than that the face is shown in profile, and the same word, once Eliot applies it to Mr. Eugenides, to mean a defect, like Madame Sosostris's cold.

<b>The Hanged Man.</b> Card XII. In the Rider pack a young man hangs by one foot from a T-shaped gallows of living wood, a halo round his head, his face calm. Eliot's note associates him with "the Hanged God of Frazer" and with the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. "The Hanged God" is a chapter in <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, on Attis, Marsyas and Odin, gods hanged on trees. Madame Sosostris does not find him. Brooks, who read her whole fortune as coming true in the poem, ties the card she cannot find to the hooded figure of Part V: "That he is hooded accounts for Madame Sosostris' inability to see him." Her one piece of advice, "Fear death by water", is answered by Eliot's note: "Death by Water is executed in Part IV."

The cards shown here are Pamela Colman Smith's designs for the pack published in London by William Rider &amp; Son in December 1909, the English pack of Eliot's time.

### pearls
TITLE: Ariel's song
BODY:
The song is from <i>The Tempest</i>, Act I scene ii. Ariel sings it to Ferdinand, who believes his father, the King of Naples, drowned in the wreck. The father is alive, and the song is not true. The poem quotes the line again at line 125, and Eliot's note there (numbered 126 in the 1922 notes) points back to lines 37 and 48, the hyacinth garden and this line. Two more passages come from the same scene: Ferdinand's "the king my father's wreck" behind lines 191–2, and "This music crept by me upon the waters" at line 257, both with notes by Eliot. His note on line 218 says the Phoenician Sailor "is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples." Compare Part IV, on the drowned Phlebas.

### ring
TITLE: Crowds in a ring
BODY:
Madame Sosostris does not deal this; she says she sees it. Eliot's note on line 46 says that the "crowds of people", like the Sailor and the Merchant, "appear later". They come at line 62, the crowd flowing over London Bridge, and again as the "hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" at lines 369–70. The ring has been compared with the souls in <i>Inferno</i> III who lived "without infamy and without praise" and run for ever behind a whirling banner; Eliot's note on line 63 cites the same canto.
