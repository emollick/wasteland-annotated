# Batch 6, the card notes in the tarot tool (ui.txt), as proposed after round three

### card-note phoenician-sailor
NOTE: He is not a card in any Tarot pack. Eliot's note on line 46 says he appears later; he is Phlebas the Phoenician of Part IV. The note on line 218 says that the one-eyed merchant "melts into" him and that he is "not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples" in <i>The Tempest</i>. "Those are pearls that were his eyes" is from Ariel's song to Ferdinand about his father, whom Ferdinand wrongly believes drowned. Madame Sosostris says "Fear death by water," and Eliot's note adds that "Death by Water is executed in Part IV."
DECK (last sentence only): He is drawn head down on purpose, in the posture of the Hanged Man on a tarot card, which is the card Madame Sosostris cannot find.

### card-note belladonna
NOTE: Belladonna means "beautiful lady." It is also deadly nightshade, and the drops made from it that women once used to widen their pupils. "The Lady of the Rocks" is usually traced to Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>, and the card is often taken to foretell the woman at her dressing-table at the start of Part II. Nobody has settled what "the lady of situations" means.

### card-note three-staves
NOTE: This is a real card, the Three of Wands. In the Rider pack it shows a figure on a headland, back turned, watching ships go out. Eliot's note says he associated the card, "quite arbitrarily," with the Fisher King; the figure who sits fishing on the shore at line 424 is usually taken to be him.
DECK (second sentence only): His three staves are a crutch for the Fisher King's wound, a fishing rod with its line down, and a staff of office planted in the ground, which has begun to sprout.

### card-note wheel
NOTE: The Wheel of Fortune is card X of the major arcana. In the medieval picture of Fortune's wheel, figures climb it, reign at the top and are thrown down. At line 320, "O you who turn the wheel and look to windward" is addressed to a helmsman at a ship's wheel; readers have taken the wheel there for this card too.
DECK: In this pack: Fortune's wheel drawn as a ship's wheel, because of line 320. The same man in a bowler hat appears four times round it, enthroned at the top, falling on one side, crushed beneath and climbing on the other.

### card-note one-eyed-merchant
NOTE: He is not a card. He is Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant of lines 209–214, unshaven, with a pocket full of currants and an invitation to lunch at the Cannon Street Hotel and a weekend at the Metropole. Eliot's note on line 218 says the merchant "melts into" the Phoenician Sailor. The poem does not say why he has one eye; Cleanth Brooks (1939) took it to mean no more than that the face on the card is shown in profile.
DECK: In this pack: Mr. Eugenides in profile, the only kind of one-eyed man a printed card can have, in a bowler and an astrakhan collar, holding out a handful of currants; on his back is a roped bundle whose contents are not shown.

### card-note blank
NOTE: Madame Sosostris is forbidden to see what the merchant carries on his back, and the poem never says. Weston argued in <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> that the old Mysteries of Attis and Mithra travelled the Roman empire with Syrian merchants, "founding their chapels at the same time as their counting-houses", as she quotes Cumont. Following her, Cleanth Brooks (1939) read this merchant as carrying a mystery he knows nothing of.
DECK (last sentence only): Hold the pointer over it, or touch it, and the bundle shows more clearly.

### card-note hanged-man
NOTE: He is card XII of the major arcana. In the Rider pack he is a young man hanging by one foot from a living tree, calm-faced and haloed. Eliot's note on line 46 links him to "the Hanged God of Frazer", the god whose death renews the year, and to the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. Madame Sosostris does not find him. Cleanth Brooks (1939) explained her failure by the hood: "That he is hooded accounts for Madame Sosostris' inability to see him." Readers since Brooks have read the absent card as the one thing her reading lacks, a death that is followed by a return.
DECK: unchanged, with "a green shoot on the dead bough" added at the end if the drawing has it.

### card-note crowd-in-a-ring
NOTE: This is not a card but something she sees. Eliot's note on line 46 says the "crowds of people" appear later: the crowd flowing over London Bridge at line 62 is the first, and the "hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" at lines 369–70 are usually taken to be another. The ring has been compared to the souls in <i>Inferno</i> III who lived "without infamy and without praise" and run for ever after a banner.
