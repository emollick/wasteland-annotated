# Batch 5, ways in and pathway stops, as they now stand

### way-1
TITLE: Hear it before you read it
BODY:
Virginia Woolf, who heard Eliot read the poem at dinner in June 1922, wrote in her diary that "he sang it & chanted it rhythmed it." Start with his own voice: the listening room has his recordings. Then read the seventy-six lines silently and notice where his stresses fell.

### way-2
TITLE: Count the speakers
BODY:
Switch on Voices and the section splits into ten: the poem's own voice, Marie, a voice in German, a prophet, a sailor and a shepherd from Wagner, a girl with hyacinths, someone who could not speak, a clairvoyante, and a walker in the City. The typescript of this section was headed "He Do the Police in Different Voices", a phrase from Dickens. For each voice, ask whether it is quoting someone else.

### way-3
TITLE: Turn the tarot cards
BODY:
Madame Sosostris turns up six cards, fails to find a seventh, and reports one vision. Turn them over one at a time on the table and read the note on each. Then ask what she leaves out. The seventh card, the one she cannot find, is the Hanged Man, and the only water in her reading is the kind that drowns: a drowned sailor, and "Fear death by water."

### way-4
TITLE: Follow the route in Part I
BODY:
Lines 60–68 follow a route: over London Bridge, "up the hill and down King William Street", and past St Mary Woolnoth as its clock strikes nine. It is about half a mile, the way into the City that thousands of clerks walked every morning from London Bridge station. Eliot's bank stood at the end of it, and his note on line 68 calls the dead sound on the final stroke "a phenomenon which I have often noticed". Follow it on the map, then read the lines again with the walk in mind.

### way-5
TITLE: Put Chaucer beside it
BODY:
Read the first twelve lines of the General Prologue, then Eliot's first seven. Both begin with April, with rain, and with roots. Decide what has changed between Chaucer's April and Eliot's.

### way-6
TITLE: Follow the water
BODY:
Part I names water, or the lack of it, again and again: spring rain and forgetful snow in the first seven lines, a shower over the Starnbergersee, a dry stone with no sound of water, a handful of dust, a drowned sailor, and then "Fear death by water." The Water lens tints the lines that name water and the lines that name its absence.

### way-7
TITLE: Meet the dead
BODY:
The Dead pathway goes through Part I with the sources open beside the lines: Dante's crowd at the gate of Hell and the sighs in Limbo, Webster's dirge, the corpse in Stetson's garden, and the buried god of Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i>.

### way-8
TITLE: Read what was cut
BODY:
The section did not begin with April. In the typescript it began with fifty-four lines about a night out in Boston, which were cut. The drafts view shows where the cut fell. Read the cancelled opening and then line 1, and decide what the change did.

### way-9
TITLE: Read it without the translations
BODY:
Five lines of Wagner (31–34 and 42), a sentence of German at line 12 and a line of French at line 76 are left untranslated. Switch on Tongues to see what they say, then switch it off and read the section as a reader with no German or French would have read it in 1922, hearing those lines without understanding them.

### way-10
TITLE: Three questions with no answer
BODY:
Part I asks questions it does not answer: what roots clutch and what branches grow out of this stony rubbish (19–20); where are you lingering (34, in Wagner's German); has the corpse in the garden begun to sprout (72). One way in is to read the section with those questions open.

### first@1
BODY:
Start with the sound. Read the first seven lines aloud. Five of the seven end on a participle left hanging at the line break: breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding.

### first@19
BODY:
The speaker changes here. Marie's talk stops and a prophet's voice begins, and nothing marks the change but the tone. The poem does this throughout: new voices begin without being introduced.

### first@35
BODY:
Between the two passages from <i>Tristan</i> (31–34 and 42), someone remembers coming back late from a garden with a companion and being unable to speak or see. Who the two are is not said.

### first@43
BODY:
Madame Sosostris is a comic fortune-teller with a cold, but some of the cards she deals come back later in the poem, and Eliot's note on line 46 says which. The card she cannot find is the Hanged Man.

### first@60
BODY:
The place is real: London Bridge, King William Street, St Mary Woolnoth and its clock. The crowd crossing the bridge is also Dante's crowd of the dead; Eliot's notes on lines 63 and 64 give the passages. His note on line 60 quotes Baudelaire, whose Paris is a city where a ghost stops a passer-by in daylight.

### water@4
BODY:
"Dull roots with spring rain." The first water in the poem is spring rain reaching the roots, in a sentence that calls the month that brings it the cruellest.

### water@24
BODY:
"the dry stone no sound of water." The first drought. The sound of water that is missing here is wished for again, at length, in Part V.

### water@42
BODY:
"Oed' und leer das Meer." Desolate and empty the sea: in the third act of Wagner's <i>Tristan</i>, the shepherd set to watch for Isolde's ship sees nothing on it.

### water@47
BODY:
The drowned Phoenician Sailor. The first drowned man in the poem is a card in a pack. The line in brackets, "Those are pearls that were his eyes," is from Ariel's song in <i>The Tempest</i>.

### water@55
BODY:
"Fear death by water." Madame Sosostris warns against water. In the Grail legend that Eliot's headnote points to, water is what the waste land lacks and needs, and Cleanth Brooks (1939) read her warning as a warning against the one death that might bring life. Part IV of the poem is called Death by Water.

### dead@0
BODY:
The Sibyl, who cannot die, in her jar.

### dead@2
BODY:
"Lilacs out of the dead land." The poem's second line already calls the land dead.

### dead@23
BODY:
"the dead tree gives no shelter." Eliot's notes send these lines to the Bible: "Son of man" (20) to Ezekiel 2:1, where God so addresses the prophet, and this line to Ecclesiastes 12:5, where the grasshopper is a burden and "man goeth to his long home," the grave.

### dead@39
BODY:
"I was neither / Living nor dead." The hyacinth garden.

### dead@62
BODY:
Dante's crowd on London Bridge. Line 63, "I had not thought death had undone so many," is from <i>Inferno</i> III, where Dante sees the train of the dead just inside the gate of Hell; Eliot's note gives the passage.

### dead@71
BODY:
"That corpse you planted last year in your garden." Eliot's headnote says that readers of Frazer will recognise "references to vegetation ceremonies" in the poem, and this is one of them: a god buried so that he will come up again, here a corpse in a garden.

### dead@74
BODY:
From the dirge in Webster's <i>The White Devil</i> that Cornelia sings over her murdered son, calling on the birds to cover "the friendless bodies of unburied men." Eliot turns the wolf into a dog and the foe into a friend.

### women@0
BODY:
The Sibyl, kept in a jar, asked by boys what she wants.

### women@13
BODY:
Marie, who was frightened on the sled as a child, and who says that in the mountains you feel free.

### women@35
BODY:
The hyacinth girl. She speaks the two lines in quotation marks, 35–36. The garden that follows is remembered by someone else.

### women@43
BODY:
Madame Sosostris, who has a cold, deals cards that come back later in the poem. "One must be so careful these days" is usually read as a fortune-teller's caution about the law, since telling fortunes for money could be prosecuted.

### women@49
BODY:
Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks. Belladonna is deadly nightshade, a poison and a cosmetic, and the word means beautiful lady; her title is usually traced to Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>.

### cities@8
BODY:
The Starnbergersee, the lake south-west of Munich, and the Hofgarten, a garden in the city centre. Eliot spent the summer of 1911 in Munich.

### cities@12
BODY:
Lithuania is named in the poem's one line of German that is not from Wagner: "I am not Russian at all, I come from Lithuania, real German."

### cities@60
BODY:
"Unreal City" is London. Eliot's note quotes Baudelaire on Paris, the "swarming city, city full of dreams, where the ghost in broad daylight accosts the passer-by."

### cities@66
BODY:
King William Street and St Mary Woolnoth: the commuters' way into the City from London Bridge. Eliot's bank stood by the church, and his note on line 68 says he "often noticed" the dead sound at nine.

### cities@70
BODY:
Mylae, the sea battle of 260 BC between Rome and Carthage.

### birds@23
BODY:
"the cricket no relief." Not a bird, but the first creature in the poem that sings. Eliot's cricket stands in for the grasshopper of Ecclesiastes 12:5, the verse his note cites, where "the grasshopper shall be a burden."

### nothing@40
BODY:
"I knew nothing, / Looking into the heart of light, the silence." The one who came back from the hyacinth garden.

### eyes@0
BODY:
"oculis meis vidi": with my own eyes I saw the Sibyl.

### eyes@39
BODY:
"my eyes failed."

### eyes@48
BODY:
"Those are pearls that were his eyes."

### eyes@52
BODY:
"the one-eyed merchant."

### eyes@65
BODY:
"each man fixed his eyes before his feet."

### eliot@8
BODY:
Munich, summer 1911: the Hofgarten and its colonnade, and the Starnbergersee, where Ludwig II of Bavaria was found drowned in 1886.

### eliot@35
BODY:
The hyacinth garden. Two people from Eliot's life have been proposed for it: Emily Hale, in Lyndall Gordon's reading of the letters Eliot wrote her, and Jean Verdenal, whom Eliot remembered in 1934 crossing the Luxembourg Gardens "waving a branch of lilac". The poem names neither.

### eliot@66
BODY:
Lloyds Bank's head office, at 71 Lombard Street, a few doors from St Mary Woolnoth. Eliot's note on line 68 says the dead sound on the final stroke of nine was "a phenomenon which I have often noticed."

### eliot@0
BODY:
Paris, January 1922. Eliot stopped there on his way back from Lausanne, and Pound went through the typescript with him, cutting it hard. The dedication came later. In 1923 Eliot wrote "il miglior fabbro" in Pound's copy, and from 1925 it was printed with the poem. The words are Dante's, for the poet Arnaut Daniel, and mean the better craftsman.
