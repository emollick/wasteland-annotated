# Editor's drafts of the ways, stops and drafts record after the reader's round one (provisional: the scholar's corrections still to be folded in)

### way-1
HEADING: Hear it before you read it
BODY: Virginia Woolf, who heard Eliot read the poem at dinner in June 1922, wrote in her diary that "he sang it & chanted it rhythmed it." Start with his own voice: the listening room has his recordings, and the first section is where the chant is plainest. Then read the seventy-six lines silently and notice where his stresses fell.
(kept as it stands, on the reader's verdict; [scholar: the Woolf quotation and date])

### way-2
HEADING: Count the speakers
BODY: Switch on Voices and the section splits into at least nine: the poem's own voice, Marie, a stranger in a café, a prophet, a sailor in Wagner, a girl with hyacinths, someone who could not speak, a clairvoyante, a commuter. The typescript's title was "He Do the Police in Different Voices." For each one, ask whether it is quoting someone else.

### way-3
HEADING: Lay out the cards
BODY: Madame Sosostris deals six cards, fails to find a seventh, and sees a crowd. Turn them over one at a time on the table and read the note on each. Then ask what she leaves out. She cannot find the Hanged Man, and the only thing she says about water is "Fear death by water." [scholar: the count]

### way-4
HEADING: Walk the commute
BODY: Lines 60–68 follow a route: over London Bridge, up King William Street, past St Mary Woolnoth as its clock strikes nine. It is about six hundred yards, and it is the walk Eliot made to Lloyds Bank. Follow it on the map, then read the lines again with the walk in mind. [scholar: how the route is known]

### way-5
HEADING: Put Chaucer beside it
BODY: Read the first twelve lines of the General Prologue, then Eliot's first seven. Both begin with April, with rain, and with roots. Decide what has changed between 1387 and 1922.

### way-6
HEADING: Follow the water
BODY: Part I names water, or the lack of it, over and over: spring rain and snow in the first sentence, a shower over the Starnbergersee, a dry stone with no sound of water, a handful of dust, a drowned sailor, and then "Fear death by water." The Water lens tints the lines that name water and the lines that name its absence.

### way-7
HEADING: Meet the dead
BODY: The Dead pathway goes through Part I with the sources open beside the lines: Dante's crowd at the gate of Hell and the sighs in Limbo, Webster's dirge, the corpse in Stetson's garden, and the buried god of Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i>.

### way-8
HEADING: Read what was cut
BODY: The section did not begin with April. In the typescript it began with fifty-four lines about a night out in Boston, which were cut. The drafts view shows where the cut fell. Read the cancelled opening and then line 1, and decide what the change did.

### way-9
HEADING: Read it without the translations
BODY: Five lines of Wagner (31–34 and 42), a sentence of German at line 12 and a line of French at line 76 are left untranslated. Switch on Tongues to see what they say, then switch it off and read the section as a reader with no German or French would have read it in 1922, hearing those lines without understanding them.

### way-10
HEADING: Three questions with no answer
BODY: Part I asks questions and answers none of them. What roots clutch and what branches grow out of this stony rubbish (19–20)? Where are you lingering (34, in Wagner's German)? Has the corpse in the garden begun to sprout (72)? One way in is to read the section with those questions open.

### first@1
Start with the sound. Read the first seven lines aloud. Five of the seven end on a participle left hanging at the line break: breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding.

### first@19
The speaker changes here. Marie's talk stops and a prophet's voice begins, and nothing marks the change but the tone. The poem does this throughout: new voices begin without being introduced.

### first@35
Between the two passages from <i>Tristan</i> (31–34 and 42), someone remembers coming back late from a garden with a companion and being unable to speak or see. Who the two are is not said.

### first@43
Madame Sosostris is a comic fortune-teller with a cold, but the cards she deals come back later in the poem; Eliot's note on line 46 says so. The card she cannot find is the Hanged Man.

### first@60
The place is real: London Bridge, King William Street, St Mary Woolnoth and its clock. The crowd crossing the bridge is also Dante's crowd of the dead; Eliot's notes on lines 63 and 64 give the passages. His note on line 60 quotes Baudelaire, whose Paris is a city where a ghost stops a passer-by in daylight.

### water@4
"Dull roots with spring rain." The first water in the poem is spring rain reaching the roots, in a sentence that calls the month that brings it the cruellest.

### water@24
"the dry stone no sound of water." The first drought. The sound of water that is missing here is wished for again, at length, in Part V.

### water@42
"Oed' und leer das Meer." Desolate and empty the sea: Wagner's shepherd sees no ship.

### water@47
The drowned Phoenician Sailor. The first drowned man in the poem is a card in a pack. The line in brackets, "Those are pearls that were his eyes," is from Ariel's song in <i>The Tempest</i>.

### water@55
"Fear death by water." Madame Sosostris warns against water. In the Grail legend that Eliot's headnote points to, water is what the waste land lacks and needs. Part IV of the poem is called Death by Water.

### dead@0
The Sibyl, who cannot die, in her jar.

### dead@2
"Lilacs out of the dead land." The first thing the poem calls dead is the land itself, in its second line.

### dead@23
"the dead tree gives no shelter." Eliot's notes send these lines to the Bible. "Son of man" (20) is how God addresses Ezekiel, whose vision of a valley of dry bones is in chapter 37; the cricket and the dead tree come from Ecclesiastes 12:5, where the grasshopper is a burden and "man goeth to his long home," the grave. [scholar: does the dead tree come from Ecclesiastes?]

### dead@39
"I was neither / Living nor dead." The hyacinth garden.

### dead@62
Dante's crowd on London Bridge: "I had not thought death had undone so many."

### dead@71
"That corpse you planted last year in your garden." The buried god of Frazer's vegetation rites, put in the ground so that he will come up again, here a corpse in a back garden.

### dead@74
Webster's dirge for unburied men, with the wolf turned into a dog and the foe into a friend.

### women@0
The Sibyl, kept in a jar, asked by boys what she wants.

### women@13
Marie, who was frightened on the sled as a child, and who says that in the mountains you feel free.

### women@35
The hyacinth girl. She speaks the two lines in quotation marks, 35–36. The garden that follows is remembered by someone else.

### women@43
Madame Sosostris, who has a cold, is careful about the police (fortune-telling could be prosecuted), and deals cards that come back later in the poem.

### women@49
Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks. Belladonna is deadly nightshade, a poison and a cosmetic, and the word means beautiful lady; her title recalls Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>.

### cities@8
The Starnberger See and the Hofgarten: Munich, where Eliot spent August 1911.

### cities@12
Lithuania, by way of a sentence in German: "I am not Russian at all, I come from Lithuania, real German."

### cities@60
"Unreal City": London, from Baudelaire's Paris.

### cities@66
King William Street and St Mary Woolnoth. Eliot's route to work.

### cities@70
Mylae, the sea battle of 260 BC between Rome and Carthage.

### birds@23
"the cricket no relief." Not a bird, but the first singer, from Ecclesiastes.

### nothing@40
"I knew nothing, / Looking into the heart of light, the silence." The one who came back from the hyacinth garden.

### eyes@0
"oculis meis vidi": with my own eyes I saw the Sibyl.

### eyes@39
"my eyes failed."

### eyes@48

### eyes@52

### eyes@65

### eliot@8
Munich, August 1911: the Hofgarten and its colonnade, and the Starnbergersee, where Ludwig II of Bavaria was found drowned in 1886.

### eliot@35
The hyacinth garden. Two people from Eliot's life have been proposed for it: Emily Hale, in Boston, and Jean Verdenal, whom Eliot later remembered crossing the Luxembourg Gardens with a branch of lilac. The poem names neither.

### eliot@66
Lloyds Bank, Lombard Street, across the road from St Mary Woolnoth. Eliot's own note on line 68 says the dead sound on the final stroke of nine is "a phenomenon which I have often noticed."

### eliot@0
Paris, January 1922. On his way back from Lausanne Eliot stopped at Pound's flat, and Pound went through the typescript, cutting it hard. The dedication, added later, calls him "il miglior fabbro," the better craftsman, which is what Dante calls the poet Arnaut Daniel in the <i>Purgatorio</i>. [scholar: the sequence]

### draft boston
TITLE: The cancelled opening: a night out in Boston
BODY:
The typescript of Part I opens with fifty-four lines of loose, chatty verse about a night out with the boys in Boston: drinks at "Tom's place," a burlesque show, a girl, a run from a policeman, a walk home at sunrise. The whole passage is struck through in pencil, apparently by Eliot before Pound saw it. "April is the cruellest month" was not the poem's first line until it was cut. Pound's comments on the rest of Part I are light: he queried a few words and let the section stand. [scholar: whose judgement on the cancellation; Pound's marks on Part I]
