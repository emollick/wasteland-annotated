# Editor, after round one: batch 4 (library notes, voices, drafts records)

After round one the editor drafted rewrites for the first eleven library notes (below, as drafted, from the reader's wording and the batch 4a scholar's corrections) and for the Part I drafts record. Before the rest were drafted, the shared files turned out to have been rewritten in place by the thread that owns them, "Debate: front matter and notes", between 18:44 and 18:51 UTC. The drafts below were therefore not sent; round two was run against the other thread's text, which is printed under each item in the editor's entry after round two, together with what this debate still proposed.

### source chaucer
Decision: rewrite. "English poetry's first great spring" and "ends in a kind of pilgrimage" go (both wrong, as the scholar showed); the comparison marked as the commentators'; the reader's sentence form kept.
WHAT: The April of sweet showers that Eliot's first line answers.
NOTE: The opening of the General Prologue is the spring that Eliot's first line is usually set against: April's showers end the drought of March, the sap rises, the birds sing, and people long to go on pilgrimage. Eliot's notes do not mention Chaucer; the comparison is the commentators' (Southam; Ricks and McCue). In the poem April breeds lilacs out of dead land, and the "we" of lines 5–7 were kept warm by winter's snow. Part V has a journey of its own, to the Chapel Perilous of the Grail romances, which Eliot's headnote to that part names.

### source ecclesiastes
Decision: rewrite. "Its dry cistern" is not in the chapter and "a plan for Part V" goes; the cricket given as Eliot's word; the wheel at the cistern set beside line 385, which the scholar counted.
NOTE: Eliot's note points to verse 5, in the chapter's picture of old age, where the grasshopper is a burden because the old can carry nothing, and desire fails. The cricket is Eliot's word; the King James Bible has a grasshopper. The verses that follow, with the golden bowl broken and "the wheel broken at the cistern," can be set beside Part V's "empty cisterns and exhausted wells" (line 385).

### source tristan
Decision: rewrite. "Four words" (five), "as a Harvard senior", "drowned" and "a ship setting out" corrected; the 1909 performance given as what the evidence is (the poem "Opera"); Eliot's line references stated to fit a printed libretto, as the scholar verified; Stravinsky's source given; the Larisch cross-reference to Ludwig kept in the batch 1 note rather than here.
NOTE: Eliot's note cites the opera by act and line, as from a printed libretto, and the numbers fit one: "Frisch weht der Wind" begins the fifth line of the sailor's song, and the shepherd's "Öd und leer das Meer" is the twenty-fourth line of Act III. The song is the first thing sung after the prelude, by a young sailor heard from above, as if from the mast, over the curtained cabin on the foredeck where Isolde lies; the ship is carrying her from Ireland to Cornwall to marry King Marke. In Act III Tristan lies wounded in Brittany, waiting for Isolde's ship. Kurwenal tells the shepherd to pipe a merry tune if he sights a sail; the shepherd looks out to sea and answers that it is empty. The poem puts the hyacinth garden between the two quotations. Eliot's poem "Opera," dated November 1909 in his notebook and first printed in <i>Inventions of the March Hare</i> (1996), records a <i>Tristan</i> heard in Boston that autumn. Stravinsky, who met Eliot in December 1956, wrote in 1965 that "Tristan must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life." The first performance, at the Munich Court Theatre on 10 June 1865, was paid for by Ludwig II of Bavaria, who was found dead in the Starnbergersee of line 8 in June 1886.

### source baudelaire-sept-vieillards
Decision: trim. The summary stands; the "method" sentence is attached to Eliot's 1950 talk, as the scholar allowed; first printing and dedication added.
NOTE: In the poem the speaker, walking through a dirty yellow fog on a Paris morning, meets a hideous old man, then a second exactly like him, then more, seven in all, and goes home and shuts his door, sick and half out of his mind. The poem was first printed in the <i>Revue contemporaine</i> on 15 September 1859 and is dedicated to Victor Hugo. Eliot's note quotes only its first two lines, and in 1950 he quoted the same two lines as what he had learned from Baudelaire, "the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric". The fog and the daylight ghost both reappear in the London passage, as the brown fog of line 61 and the crowd of the dead at lines 62–63.

### source baudelaire-au-lecteur
Decision: correct. The date of first printing added (1855; the book is 1857); the two essays named; the reader's sentence form.
DATE: 1855; the preface poem of the 1857 volume
NOTE: "Au Lecteur" stands at the head of <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i>; it was first printed in the <i>Revue des deux mondes</i> on 1 June 1855, before the volume of 1857. It runs through the vices, names the ugliest last, Ennui, and turns on the reader in its final line, which Eliot borrows to end Part I. He wrote in "Baudelaire" (1930) that Baudelaire was "the greatest exemplar in modern poetry in any language," and said in "What Dante Means to Me" (1950) that he had learned from him "the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric."

### source death-of-st-narcissus
Decision: rewrite. "Wanted to be" corrected to "had been"; "made red" corrected (the red rock is already in the 1915 poem); the first printing added, to agree with the note on line 25; the poem described, with no more than a phrase quoted.
DATE: c. 1915 (withdrawn from publication)
NOTE: Eliot could not remember when he wrote the poem; Valerie Eliot puts it early in 1915. It was set in type for <i>Poetry</i> (Chicago) that year and not published. The galley proof survives in the magazine's papers, and John Hayward printed the poem from it in <i>Poems Written in Early Youth</i> (twelve copies, 1950; trade edition 1967). Two manuscripts went to John Quinn with the <i>Waste Land</i> drafts in 1922 and are reproduced in the 1971 facsimile. The saint of the title has been, or believes he has been, a tree, a fish and a girl; he becomes a dancer before God and dies pierced by arrows. Its first seven lines are the source of lines 26–30 of Part I: the rock of shelter is grey there, and the speaker promises something different from two shadows, one sprawling over the sand at daybreak, one leaping behind a fire against a red rock, and then the saint's bloody cloth and limbs. The poem is in copyright, so only its first two lines are given here.

### source huxley-crome-yellow
Decision: rewrite. "The immediate source" replaced by the evidence for the debt (Eliot's 1952 letter) and against it (Rainey's dating), to agree with the note on line 43; "fête" corrected to the fair; the Garsington sentence cut as unanchored; the name given as what was borrowed.
NOTE: The name is usually traced to Huxley's <i>Crome Yellow</i> (Chatto &amp; Windus, November 1921). At the charity fair in chapter 27 Mr. Scogan, in skirt, bodice and black wig, reads palms as "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana." Eliot himself thought so: he told Grover Smith in a letter of 10 March 1952 that he was almost certain he had taken the name from the novel without being aware of it. The chronology is awkward. Lawrence Rainey (<i>Revisiting The Waste Land</i>, 2005) dates the typescript of Parts I and II to May 1921 from its paper and typewriter, and Huxley finished the novel only that August; on that dating the name would be Eliot's own. Sesostris was a legendary king of Egypt in Herodotus. The cards, the cold and the spelling are Eliot's in any case.

### source dante-inf3
Decision: rewrite. "Are these" marked as a reading; Eliot's two notes (III for line 63, IV for line 64) given together; the Temple Classics text explained; wasps and horseflies; the 1950 talk named.
NOTE: The gate's inscription ends "Abandon all hope, you who enter" (III.9). Inside it, before the river Acheron, Dante finds the souls "who lived without infamy and without praise" (III.36), cast out by heaven and refused by hell; they run naked after a whirling banner, stung by wasps and horseflies, in a train so long that Dante had not believed death had undone so many. Eliot's note attaches the tercet to line 63, the crowd on London Bridge; his note to the next line sends the reader to <i>Inferno</i> IV and the Limbo of the virtuous pagans, so the crowd is given two places at once. The "crowds of people, walking round in a ring" that Madame Sosostris sees at line 56 are often read as the same crowd. Eliot quoted from the Temple Classics text, which is why his tercet differs in small ways from the one printed above. He said in "What Dante Means to Me" (1950) that Dante's poetry was still, "after forty years," "the most persistent and deepest influence" on his own verse.

### source dante-inf4
Decision: rewrite. "Great-hearted" corrected; "sigh like the noble pagans, not like the damned" replaced by the two notes side by side.
NOTE: Limbo, the first circle, holds the unbaptised and the virtuous pagans, Homer and Aristotle among them, and Virgil himself. They are not tormented, but, as Virgil says, "without hope we live in desire" (IV.42); their sighs, not weeping, make the eternal air tremble. Eliot's note attaches the tercet to line 64. The line before it he had sent to the vestibule of <i>Inferno</i> III, so the crowd on London Bridge is given, in successive notes, the neutrals' number and the noble pagans' sighs.

### source ezekiel
Decision: rewrite. "Dry stone" cut from the list (not in Ezekiel); the chapter 6 link marked as the commentators'; the count made exact; the contrast with the poem's bones kept as a sentence.
NOTE: Ezekiel prophesied among the exiles in Babylon in the sixth century BC, and God addresses him as "son of man," a mortal, ninety-three times in the King James text. Eliot's note points to the first of them, at the prophet's commission (2:1). Commentators add 6:4–6, where the idolaters' "images shall be broken," for line 22, and the valley of dry bones (37:1–14) for line 391, "Dry bones can harm no one." In Ezekiel the bones are given breath and stand up, "an exceeding great army"; in the poem they lie still.

### source isaiah
Decision: rewrite. Both links marked as the commentators'; "the prophet's shelter" corrected; the Fisher King given through Eliot's note to line 425; house and lands.
NOTE: Neither passage is in Eliot's notes; both are commentators' comparisons (Southam; Ricks and McCue). For the shadow of the red rock (line 25) they cite 32:2, where in the reign of a righteous king a man is "as rivers of water in a dry place, as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land"; the poem's rock gives shadow and no water (line 24). For "Shall I at least set my lands in order?" (line 426) they cite the sentence on King Hezekiah, "Set thine house in order: for thou shalt die." Hezekiah turned his face to the wall and wept, and was given fifteen more years (38:2–5). Eliot's note to line 425 sends the reader to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King, whose land lies waste with him; the poem turns house into lands.

### draft boston
Decision: rewrite. "Burlesque", "a run from a policeman", "what remains begins already tired" and "the second first line" go; the facsimile named as the source; the cancellation stated as the facsimile and Wilson state it; the sequence of the night made to agree with the gloss on line 1, which both sides have settled.
TITLE: The cancelled opening: a night out in Boston
BODY:
In the typescript, Part I opens with fifty-four lines about a night out in Boston, told by one of a party of young men in loose, chatty verse: drinks at "Tom's place," a show, a call on a woman who turns him away, a policeman who picks him up and a man he knows who gets him off, and a walk home at sunrise. The lines are struck through in pencil. Valerie Eliot's facsimile (1971) attributes the stroke to Eliot, and Pound's annotations begin only after it; whether Pound read the lines is not recorded. "April is the cruellest month" stood at the top of the typescript's second page. Pound's comments on the rest of Part I are few: he queried a few words and let the section stand.
