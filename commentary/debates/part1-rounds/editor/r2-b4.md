### source chaucer
As the front-matter thread now has it:
TITLE: The Canterbury Tales, General Prologue
AUTHOR: Geoffrey Chaucer
DATE: c. 1387
WHAT: Chaucer’s April of sweet showers and pilgrimages, behind Eliot’s “cruellest month.”
LINES: 1
NOTE: The Prologue opens with April rain ending the drought of March, sap rising, birds singing through the night and people longing to go on pilgrimage. Eliot’s notes say nothing of Chaucer; readers have long set “April is the cruellest month” against these lines, in which the same month and the same rain are welcome. In Eliot’s poem it is winter that “kept us warm” (lines 5–6).

Proposed on top of it:
Present: “birds singing through the night”.
Reason: an inference. Chaucer’s birds “maken melodye” and “slepen al the nyght with open ye” (General Prologue 9–10); “birds singing” would be closer.

### source tristan
As the front-matter thread now has it:
TITLE: Tristan und Isolde
AUTHOR: Richard Wagner
DATE: 1865 (first performed Munich)
WHAT: The sailor’s song (lines 31–34) and the shepherd’s “Öd und leer das Meer,” desolate and empty the sea (line 42).
LINES: 31, 42
NOTE: Eliot’s notes cite the libretto by act and verse: I, verses 5–8, and III, verse 24. The young sailor’s song is the first thing sung in the opera; the sailor sings it unseen, from the masthead, above the deck where Isolde lies. The shepherd’s “Öd und leer das Meer!” comes in Act III, answering Tristan’s servant Kurwenal, who has asked whether any ship is in sight while Tristan lies wounded and waiting for Isolde. In the poem the hyacinth garden (lines 35–41) falls between the two quotations. Eliot heard <i>Tristan</i>, or its music in concert, in Boston in the autumn of 1909, at the start of his graduate year at Harvard, and wrote a poem about it, “Opera,” dated November 1909; Stravinsky, who met him in 1956, thought <i>Tristan</i> “must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life.” Ludwig II of Bavaria, whose money paid for the first performance, was found dead in 1886 in the Starnberger See, the lake of line 8.

Proposed on top of it:
Present: “…and wrote a poem about it, “Opera,” dated November 1909; Stravinsky, who met him in 1956, thought <i>Tristan</i> “must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life.””
Proposed: “…and wrote a poem about it, “Opera,” dated November 1909. Stravinsky, who met him in December 1956, wrote in “Memories of T. S. Eliot” (<i>Esquire</i>, August 1965) that <i>Tristan</i> “must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life.””
Reason: the quotation has no source in the note; it is from Stravinsky’s memoir in <i>Esquire</i>, 1 August 1965 (quoted by Jewel Spears Brooker, <i>Time Present</i> 101, 2020), and the meeting was in London in December 1956. The reader lost the thread at the semicolon that joins 1909 to 1956.

TITLE: Crome Yellow, chapter 27
AUTHOR: Aldous Huxley
DATE: 1921
WHAT: “Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana”: the name behind Madame Sosostris.
LINES: 43
NOTE: Huxley’s country-house satire was published in November 1921. Proposed on top of it:
Present: “who dresses up as an old gypsy woman to tell fortunes at the village fête under the name “Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana,” is the accepted source of Madame Sosostris.”
Proposed: “who dresses up as an old gypsy woman to tell fortunes at the charity fair in the park under the name “Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana,” is the source usually given for Madame Sosostris.” And, after the sentence on the 1952 letter: “Lawrence Rainey (<i>Revisiting The Waste Land</i>, 2005) has doubted it: he dates the typescript of Parts I and II to May 1921 from its paper and typewriter, before Huxley had finished the novel, and suggests the name was Eliot’s own.”
Reason: Huxley’s event is “the Fair,” the annual charity fair in the park at Crome (chapters XXV and XXVII), not a village fête. “The accepted source” hides a real difficulty: Rainey’s dating of the typescript puts it before the novel was finished (August 1921). Eliot’s letter of 1952 says he was “almost certain” of the debt; the note should carry both, as the Part I gloss on line 43 now does.

### source frazer
As the front-matter thread now has it:
TITLE: The Golden Bough (Adonis, Attis, Osiris)
AUTHOR: Sir James George Frazer
DATE: 1906–15 (third edition); one-volume abridgement 1922
WHAT: The god who dies with the harvest and returns in spring: the Hanged God, the buried effigy, the image thrown into the sea.
LINES: 46, 71, 312
NOTE: Frazer’s twelve-volume study of magic and religion, which Eliot’s headnote says “has influenced our generation profoundly,” argued that the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris, and by implication of Christ, tell one story, that of a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by rites in which a king is sacrificed or an effigy is buried or drowned. The two volumes Eliot names describe the “gardens of Adonis,” pots of quick-sprouting seed carried out with images of the dead god and “flung with them into the sea,” and the effigies of Osiris “made of earth and corn” that the priests buried so that grain would sprout from the god’s body. Eliot’s note on the Tarot says the Hanged Man “is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer”; the corpse planted in Stetson’s garden (line 71) and Phlebas in the current (line 312) have been connected with the same rites. Frazer put a reproduction of Turner’s painting <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1834) at the front of his first edition and opened his first page with it, describing it as a view of Lake Nemi; it in fact shows Lake Avernus and the Sibyl of Cumae, the prophetess of the poem’s epigraph, holding the bough that admits Aeneas to the underworld.

Proposed on top of it:
Present: “argued that the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris, and by implication of Christ, tell one story, that of a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by rites in which a king is sacrificed or an effigy is buried or drowned.” And the last sentence, from “Frazer put a reproduction” to “the underworld.”
Proposed: “argued that the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris tell one story, that of a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by rites in which a king is sacrificed or an effigy is buried or drowned, and that the Church’s Easter was adapted from the spring festivals of Adonis and Attis.” And: “Frazer put a reproduction of Turner’s painting <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1834) at the front of his first edition and opened his first page with it, describing it as a view of Lake Nemi. It in fact shows Lake Avernus, with the Sibyl of Cumae, the prophetess of the poem’s epigraph, holding the bough that admits Aeneas to the underworld.”
Reason: “by implication” is wrong; Frazer says it outright in the two volumes Eliot names. <i>Adonis Attis Osiris</i> (third edition, 1914), vol. I, by its own table of contents: “the Christian festival of Easter perhaps grafted on a festival of Adonis” (pp. 254–257) and “the festival of Easter apparently adapted to the spring festival of Attis” (pp. 305–310). The last sentence carried the frontispiece, the first page, the mistake, the real lake, the Sibyl, the epigraph and Aeneas in one breath.

### source weston
As the front-matter thread now has it:
TITLE: From Ritual to Romance
AUTHOR: Jessie L. Weston
DATE: 1920
WHAT: The book Eliot’s headnote credits with the title and the plan: the Fisher King, the waste land, the Chapel Perilous.
LINES: 0, 46, 386, 424
NOTE: Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance. Following Frazer’s <i>Golden Bough</i>, she argued that the Grail stories preserved the remains of an ancient fertility cult: a king whose wound lays his land waste, a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics, and a rite of initiation descended from the mystery cults of Attis and Mithra. Eliot’s headnote says the book suggested “not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem,” and recommends it “to any who think such elucidation of the poem worth the trouble”; in a lecture of 1956 he said he regretted “having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail.” The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel).

Present: “The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel).”
Proposed: “The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), VI (The Symbols, where the Grail hallows become the four suits of the Tarot), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel).”
Reason: chapter VI is the chapter behind Eliot’s note on line 46. It is where Weston makes the four Grail hallows the suits of the Tarot: “They exist to-day as the four suits of the Tarot.”

### source petronius
As the front-matter thread now has it:
TITLE: Satyricon, chapter 48
AUTHOR: Petronius
DATE: 1st century AD
WHAT: The epigraph: the Sibyl who wanted to die.
LINES: 0
NOTE: The speaker is Trimalchio, a freed slave grown enormously rich, boasting to his guests between courses; his dinner party is the longest continuous episode in what survives of the <i>Satyricon</i>. The Sibyl of Cumae, in Ovid’s telling (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV), asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand and forgot to ask for youth to go with them; she foresees that she will shrink until only her voice is left. The typescript of the poem carried a different epigraph, from Conrad’s <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, ending “The horror! the horror!”; Pound doubted that Conrad was weighty enough for the place, and Eliot put Petronius in its stead before publication.

Proposed on top of it:
Present: “asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand and forgot to ask for youth to go with them” and “Eliot put Petronius in its stead before publication.”
Proposed: “asked Apollo for as many years as there were grains in a heap of dust she had scooped up, and forgot to ask for youth to go with them” and “Eliot put Petronius in its place before publication.”
Reason: Ovid’s words are “ego pulveris hausti / ostendens cumulum, quot haberet corpora pulvis” (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV, 136–137): dust, not sand, in a heap she had taken up and holds out. The Part I gloss on the epigraph says the same, and the two should agree; Ovid’s word is also the word of line 30. “In its stead” is not something a person says across a table.

LINES: 0
NOTE: A lecture given at the University of Minnesota on 30 April 1956, to an audience of about fourteen thousand, and printed in <i>On Poetry and Poets</i> (1957). Thirty-four years on, Eliot said he had meant only to list his references, “spiking the guns” of critics who had accused his earlier poems of plagiarism. The poem’s first printings, in two magazines, the <i>Criterion</i> and the <i>Dial</i>, had no notes; when Boni & Liveright were to make it a book, it was found too short, and he expanded the notes to fill the pages, with the result he called “bogus scholarship.” He regretted sending readers on “a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail,” and said the notes could not now be removed, since anyone who bought his poems and found them missing would ask for his money back.
