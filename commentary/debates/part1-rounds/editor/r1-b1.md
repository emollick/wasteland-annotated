# Editor, after round one: batch 1 (title, epigraph, dedication, lines 1–30)

Each item: the editor's decision, then the draft that goes to both sides for round two. Wording follows the reader where the two sides differed on wording; substance follows the scholar.

### title
Decision: trim. The triad and the closing instruction go; "written over" becomes "typed at the head of"; Dickens dated and placed; the working title's fate stated.
TITLE: Where the title comes from
BODY:
Eliot's first note credits the title, "the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism" of the poem to Jessie L. Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920). Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve, beneath their Christian story, the rites of a much older fertility cult: a king wounded or impotent, his land laid waste with him, and a healing that depends on a stranger who arrives and asks the right question. Eliot's note recommends the book to "any who think such elucidation of the poem worth the trouble."

The typescript had another title. Parts I and II are headed <i>He Do the Police in Different Voices</i>, a phrase from Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (1864–65), Book I, chapter 16, where Betty Higden praises her foundling Sloppy: "You mightn't think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices." The heading was dropped before publication and is known from the drafts, published in facsimile in 1971.

### epigraph
Decision: rewrite. Trimalchio a freed slave; the Sibyl's story given to Ovid, with dust for sand (the scholar's correction, which makes the link to line 30); the jar Petronius's; the count of languages gone; the Conrad passage kept in full (an 1899 text) and marked as Eliot's typing; the Pound letter dated once, 24 January 1922, to agree with the dedication note; when the epigraph first appeared added.
TITLE: The Sibyl in her jar
BODY:
The speaker is Trimalchio, a freed slave grown enormously rich, whose dinner party fills the longest surviving stretch of Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i> (first century AD). In chapter 48 he is boasting to his guests of what he has seen. The Sibyl's story is in Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV, 130–153: Apollo offered her whatever she wished; she picked up a heap of dust and asked for as many years as it had grains, and forgot to ask for youth. Ovid has her foretell that she will shrink until only her voice is left; the jar is Petronius's detail, and the boys' question and her answer are in Greek in his Latin. Ovid's word for what she held is dust, "pulvis," and line 30 of the poem ends in "a handful of dust."

This epigraph was not the first. The typescript carried Kurtz's death from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, as Eliot typed it: "Did he live his life again in every detail of desire, temptation, and surrender during that supreme moment of complete knowledge? He cried in a whisper at some image, at some vision,—he cried out twice, a cry that was no more than a breath—'The horror! the horror!'" Pound wrote on 24 January 1922 that he doubted "if Conrad is weighty enough to stand the citation." Eliot answered that it was "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," and gave it up. The Petronius epigraph first appeared in the Boni & Liveright edition of December 1922; the <i>Criterion</i> printing of October 1922 had none. [scholar: did the Dial printing of November 1922 have it?]

### dedication
Decision: trim and correct. Opener made a sentence; "credits the cutting, not the writing" gone; November 1921 added; "halved" given to Eliot's 1946 account; the letter dated once, with Paige's date in brackets; Pound's own earlier use of the phrase added; the Hogarth edition added.
TITLE: The better craftsman
BODY:
The line is <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, 117. The poet Guido Guinizelli, doing penance among the lustful in the refining fire, points Dante toward Arnaut Daniel, the Provençal troubadour, and calls him the better smith of his native speech. The same canto is quoted near the end of the poem: "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina" at line 428 is Arnaut stepping back into the flames. "Il miglior fabbro" was already Pound's phrase: it is the title of his chapter on Arnaut Daniel in <i>The Spirit of Romance</i> (1910).

The dedication is not in the printings of 1922, nor in the Hogarth Press edition of 1923. Eliot wrote it by hand in Pound's copy of the Boni & Liveright edition, in January 1923 by Ricks and McCue's dating, and first printed it in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>. Pound had seen the typescript in Paris in November 1921, on Eliot's way to Lausanne, and went through the whole poem with him there in January 1922 on his way back; further comments came by post. Eliot said in 1946 that the poem left Pound's hands "reduced to about half its size." Pound's letter of 24 January 1922, dated "24 Saturnus, An 1" in a calendar of his own devising, gives his verdict: "Complimenti, you bitch. I am wracked by the seven jealousies." (D. D. Paige's 1950 edition of Pound's letters printed it as 24 December 1921.)

### april
Decision: rewrite. "Most famous opening" and the triad go; the participle claim corrected (the objects arrive, a line late); "next paragraph" corrected (same verse paragraph); the two lilac poems no longer called Aprils, Whitman marked as accepted and Brooke as a suggestion; "or you" gone; "us" placed at line 5.
TITLE: Chaucer's April, reversed
BODY:
Chaucer's opening welcomes April. Its showers end the drought of March, the sap rises, and people set out on pilgrimages. Eliot's first line answers it; every commentary notes the echo. The rain is still there ("stirring / Dull roots with spring rain"), but the month is called cruel, because what the rain stirs is "memory and desire", and the snow of winter was "forgetful". Five of the first seven lines end on a participle (breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding), and each object is held over to the next line, so each verb waits a line for what it governs.

Two other lilac poems are usually mentioned. Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" (1865) mourns Lincoln, shot in April, with lilacs, "ever-returning spring" and a hermit thrush; Eliot's own note to line 357 names the hermit-thrush. Rupert Brooke's "The Old Vicarage, Grantchester", written in Berlin in May 1912, opens "Just now the lilac is in bloom"; Brooke died in April 1915. The Whitman connection is generally accepted; the Brooke one is a suggestion. "Us" in line 5 is never identified, and the voice that goes on to Munich at line 8 belongs to the same verse paragraph, which runs to line 18.

### boston-cut
Decision: trim and correct. The list becomes a sentence with the contents the scholar could verify (Wilson's summary), dropping "burlesque" and "run from"; the cancellation credited to Eliot on the facsimile's authority and the absence of Pound's marks; "starts already exhausted" gone.
TITLE: What came before "April"
BODY:
In the typescript, "April is the cruellest month" is not the first line. Fifty-four lines come before it, a night out in Boston told by one of a party of young men: drinks at "Tom's place," a show, a call at "Myrtle's place" after two in the morning where he is not welcome, a policeman, and a cab home at sunrise. The passage is struck through in pencil. Valerie Eliot's facsimile attributes the stroke to Eliot, and Pound's annotations begin only after it, which is why the cut is credited to Eliot rather than Pound. The typescript is in the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library, among John Quinn's papers, and is reproduced in the facsimile edition of 1971.

### starnbergersee
Decision: rewrite. Title changed, since "August 1911" was wrong and read the biography into the lines; July and August; "found dead", with the disputed verdict; "paid for the first performance"; the Königssee of the typescript added; the Ludwig connection marked as the commentators'; "It only stops for coffee" gone; the Larisch link to the lake added.
TITLE: The lake and the Hofgarten
BODY:
The Starnberger See (one word in the poem) is the long lake south-west of Munich; the Hofgarten is the court garden beside the Residenz in the city centre, with arcades along two sides, which are probably the "colonnade". In the typescript the lake was the Königssee, in the far south-east of Bavaria; Eliot changed it. He was in Munich in July and August 1911, at the end of his year in Paris, and finished "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" there. King Ludwig II of Bavaria, Wagner's patron and the builder of Neuschwanstein, was found dead in the lake on 13 June 1886; the verdict was suicide by drowning, though the autopsy found no water in his lungs. Commentators, Southam among them, have set that death beside the poem's "death by water"; the poem itself says nothing of it. The lake is also in Countess Marie Larisch's memoir (see the note at line 15): her aunt the Empress Elisabeth grew up at Possenhofen on its shore.

### marie
Decision: rewrite. "Not from any book" reversed the evidence, as the scholar showed: Morris found most of lines 8–18 in <i>My Past</i>; Valerie Eliot's note covers the sledding and the meeting, with her caveat. The two closing readings go.
TITLE: Countess Marie Larisch
BODY:
Lines 8–18 draw on Countess Marie Larisch (1858–1940), a niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria. G. L. K. Morris pointed out in 1954 ("Marie, Marie, Hold On Tight," <i>Partisan Review</i>) that her memoir <i>My Past</i> (1913) has the archduke who was her cousin, the Starnbergersee, the mountains where one feels free, the reading late at night and the winters in the south. Valerie Eliot's note in the 1971 facsimile adds that Eliot had met her, though when and where is not known, and that the sledding, which is not in the book, came word for word from her conversation. Larisch had carried messages between her cousin Crown Prince Rudolf and Mary Vetsera before the two were found dead at Mayerling in January 1889; she was banished from court, and the memoir revived the scandal. "The archduke's, my cousin's" fits Rudolf.

### litauen
Decision: rewrite. The one hard fact ("Russin" is feminine) added; the café, the Baltic gentry, the passport triad and "defensive" gone; the attribution left open, with the site's own choice stated.
TITLE: German, not Russian
BODY:
The line is in German. "Russin" is feminine, so the speaker is a woman. Whether she is the Marie of the lines that follow, a companion, or someone overheard, the poem does not say; the site's Voices lens gives the line to a separate voice. Lithuania was part of the Russian Empire until 1918 and a republic after it; either way a German speaker from there might expect to be taken for Russian and want to correct it. It is the first line of the poem proper not in English.

### son-of-man
Decision: rewrite. Title changed; the scroll, "the desert voice", the rhetorical question and "the same small dry noise" go; the grasshopper corrected (a weight, not a sound); "second note" corrected; the Ezekiel 6 and 37 links marked as the usual ones; the comparison with the later poems given to readers.
TITLE: "Son of man" in Ezekiel
BODY:
Eliot's note sends the reader to Ezekiel 2:1, where God addresses the prophet as "son of man" and sends him to "a rebellious nation" (2:3). The address runs through the book; in chapter 37 the prophet is set down in a valley of dry bones and told to prophesy until they stand up, and "Dry bones can harm no one" (line 391) is usually referred to that chapter. "A heap of broken images" is usually traced to Ezekiel 6:4–6, where the idols and images of Israel are to be broken. The voice here is unlike anything in the first eighteen lines; readers have compared it to "Ash-Wednesday" (1930) and the choruses of <i>The Rock</i> (1934).

Eliot's note on line 23 points to Ecclesiastes 12:5, in the chapter on old age: "the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail: because man goeth to his long home, and the mourners go about the streets." The grasshopper there is a weight, not a sound: in age even so light a thing is a burden. Eliot's cricket gives "no relief."

### red-rock
Decision: rewrite. The eighty-word sentence broken up; Isaiah moved to lines 25–26 where it belongs; the sundial marked as a reading; sand corrected to dust; the earlier poem's history added so a reader knows why it is little known.
TITLE: Eliot borrowing from himself
BODY:
"Come in under the shadow of this red rock" comes almost unchanged from "The Death of Saint Narcissus," a poem Eliot wrote about 1915, had set in type for <i>Poetry</i> (Chicago) and then withdrew; it was first printed in 1950 and is reproduced in the 1971 facsimile. There the rock of shelter is grey, and the speaker promises to show something different from two shadows, one sprawling over the sand at daybreak, the other leaping behind a fire against a red rock. Line 27 is carried over word for word. For the shelter of lines 25–26 commentators cite Isaiah 32:2, "the shadow of a great rock in a weary land." The two shadows of lines 28–29, behind you in the morning and rising to meet you at evening, have been read as a life from birth to death. For "a handful of dust" editors cite Tennyson's <i>Maud</i> (Part II, section V): "Dead, long dead, / Long dead! / And my heart is a handful of dust." The Sibyl of the epigraph, in Ovid, asked for as many years as there were grains in a heap of dust.
