# After round four: the nine pathway stops

The scholar verified the facts in all nine and corrected one (dead@74); the reader was satisfied with three and gave a plainer sentence for six, none of them changing a fact the scholar had checked. The reader has the last word on wording and the scholar on substance, so the six are taken as the reader wrote them and the Webster stop merges the two. What was applied:

### water@42
Decision: both satisfied; the round-three draft stands.
BODY:
"Oed' und leer das Meer." Desolate and empty the sea: in the third act of Wagner's <i>Tristan</i>, the shepherd set to watch for Isolde's ship sees nothing on it.

### dead@62
Decision: the reader's wording taken. It keeps the poem's line as the poem's (the note gives the Italian) and makes "the train of the dead" what Dante sees rather than a name for the canto; no fact the scholar checked is changed.
BODY:
Dante's crowd on London Bridge. Line 63, "I had not thought death had undone so many," is from <i>Inferno</i> III, where Dante sees the train of the dead just inside the gate of Hell; Eliot's note gives the passage.

### dead@71
Decision: the reader's wording taken. "Promises" made the headnote wry and "the plainest" was a ranking given as fact; the headnote is now reported in its own terms and the reading stated once.
BODY:
"That corpse you planted last year in your garden." Eliot's headnote says that readers of Frazer will recognise "references to vegetation ceremonies" in the poem, and this is one of them: a god buried so that he will come up again, here a corpse in a garden.

### dead@74
Decision: the scholar's correction taken (she sings over her murdered son, Marcello; the quoted words are what the song asks the birds to cover), in the reader's shape, which says who Cornelia is. The reader's second wording kept "for the friendless bodies", which the scholar had objected to, so the editor merged the two.
BODY:
From the dirge in Webster's <i>The White Devil</i> that Cornelia sings over her murdered son, calling on the birds to cover "the friendless bodies of unburied men." Eliot turns the wolf into a dog and the foe into a friend.

### women@49
Decision: both satisfied; the round-three draft stands.
BODY:
Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks. Belladonna is deadly nightshade, a poison and a cosmetic, and the word means beautiful lady; her title is usually traced to Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>.

### cities@8
Decision: the reader's wording taken. "A garden in the city centre" is what the name means and keeps a reader from taking the Hofgarten for a café.
BODY:
The Starnbergersee, the lake south-west of Munich, and the Hofgarten, a garden in the city centre. Eliot spent the summer of 1911 in Munich.

### cities@12
Decision: the reader's wording taken. "The poem's only German", in a sentence about who is Russian and who is German, read as a person; the scholar had confirmed that line 12 is the poem's only German not from Wagner, and the reader's sentence keeps that.
BODY:
Lithuania is named in the poem's one line of German that is not from Wagner: "I am not Russian at all, I come from Lithuania, real German."

### cities@60
Decision: both satisfied; the round-three draft stands.
BODY:
"Unreal City" is London. Eliot's note quotes Baudelaire on Paris, the "swarming city, city full of dreams, where the ghost in broad daylight accosts the passer-by."

### birds@23
Decision: the reader's wording taken. It restores the reason the cricket is on a walk through the poem's birds, in the terms the old stop and the front-matter thread's reader both used; the cricket is the first creature the poem names.
BODY:
"the cricket no relief." Not a bird, but the first creature in the poem that sings. Eliot's cricket stands in for the grasshopper of Ecclesiastes 12:5, the verse his note cites, where "the grasshopper shall be a burden."
