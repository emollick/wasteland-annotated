# Editor's provisional proposals for the shared items (library notes, voice labels, shared drafts records, tarot card notes), after the reader's round one; the scholar's corrections still to come

### source chaucer
WHAT: The April of sweet showers that Eliot's first line answers.
NOTE: Chaucer's Prologue opens with April rain ending the drought of March, sap rising, birds singing and people setting out on pilgrimage. Eliot's first line is usually read against it. In his opening it is winter that kept people warm (lines 5–7) and April that is cruel. The poem too has a journey to a chapel, in Part V.

### source ecclesiastes
NOTE: Eliot's note sends the reader to verse 5, where "the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail." The chapter is a picture of old age and death. Eliot's cricket that gives no relief is the Bible's grasshopper that has become a burden. The chapter's later verses, with the pitcher broken at the fountain and the dust returning to the earth, are close to the imagery of Part V.

### source tristan
NOTE: Eliot's note cites the opera by act and verse, as a book; the libretto was sold and read as one. The young sailor's song opens Act I. He sings it from the masthead, unseen, above the deck where Isolde lies. The shepherd's four words in Act III are his answer to Kurwenal, who has asked whether any ship is in sight while Tristan lies dying. The two quotations stand on either side of the hyacinth garden passage, lines 35–41. Eliot heard <i>Tristan</i> in Boston in 1909, as a Harvard senior, and wrote a poem called "Opera" about it. Stravinsky, who met him in 1956, came away thinking that <i>Tristan</i> "must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life." Ludwig II of Bavaria, who paid for the first performance, drowned in the Starnbergersee, the lake of line 8.

### source baudelaire-sept-vieillards
NOTE: In the poem the speaker, walking through a yellow fog on a Paris morning, meets a hideous old man, then a second exactly like him, then more, seven in all, and flees home half mad. Eliot's note quotes only the first two lines. The fog and the daylight ghost both reappear in the London passage, as the brown fog of line 61 and the crowd of the dead at lines 62–63.

### source baudelaire-au-lecteur
NOTE: Baudelaire opens his book with a catalogue of the sins of mankind, keeps the worst, Ennui, for last, and then turns on whoever is reading. Eliot closes his first section the same way. He said in 1930 that Baudelaire was "the greatest exemplar in modern poetry in any language," and in 1950 that he had learned from him "the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric."

### source death-of-st-narcissus
NOTE: Eliot's poem about a saint who wished to be a tree, a fish and a girl, and died pierced with arrows, was set in type for <i>Poetry</i> (Chicago) in 1915 and withdrawn at proof stage. The manuscript survived among the papers Eliot sent to John Quinn in 1922 with the <i>Waste Land</i> drafts. Its opening gave the poem lines 25–26, and what follows in the earlier poem runs parallel to lines 27–30: the speaker promises to show the listener something other than his own shadow on the sand or against the fire, and shows him the saint's bloody cloth and limbs. The poem is still in copyright, so only its first two lines are given here.

### source huxley-crome-yellow
NOTE: Huxley's country-house satire came out in November 1921, while Eliot was at Margate and Lausanne. Mr. Scogan, the novel's cynic, telling fortunes dressed as a woman at a village fête, is usually taken as the source of the "famous clairvoyante." Huxley had been at Garsington with Eliot, and the two knew each other's work.

### source dante-inf3
NOTE: Inside the gate of Hell ("Abandon all hope") but before the river Acheron, Dante meets the souls who were neither good nor bad, "who lived without infamy and without praise." Heaven rejects them and Hell will not have them; they run forever after a banner, stung by wasps. Eliot's note ties the crowd on London Bridge (line 63) to them, and the "crowds of people, walking round in a ring" that Madame Sosostris sees at line 56 are usually read the same way. Eliot said in 1950 that Dante's poetry was still, "after forty years," "the most persistent and deepest influence" on his own verse.

### source dante-inf4
NOTE: Limbo is the first circle of Hell, where the virtuous who died unbaptised live: Homer, Virgil and Aristotle among them, the "great-hearted" souls who are not tormented but live in desire without hope. Eliot's note for line 64 sends the reader here, one canto on from the crowd of line 63. The sighs of the London crowd are the sighs of these souls, not of the damned.

### source ezekiel
NOTE: Ezekiel was a prophet of the Babylonian exile. God addresses him ninety-odd times as "son of man," which means simply a human being, a mortal. Eliot's note points to the moment God first speaks to him. The poem's dry stone, broken images and dry bones all come from the same book: chapter 6 has the broken images and chapter 37 the valley of bones. In Ezekiel the bones are made to live again; the poem's "Dry bones" at line 391 are not.

### source isaiah
NOTE: Isaiah's rock stands beside "rivers of water in a dry place"; Eliot's red rock gives shade and no water (lines 24–25). Eliot's notes do not cite Isaiah; the parallel is the commentators'. Hezekiah, told to set his house in order because he will die, turned his face to the wall and wept, and was given fifteen more years. At line 426 the speaker turns the command into a question: "Shall I at least set my lands in order?"

### source tennyson
NOTE: "A handful of dust" (line 30) is in <i>Maud</i>, where the mad speaker imagines himself buried alive under a city street with the traffic going over him. Line 429 is half Latin and half English: "Quando fiam uti chelidon," when shall I be like the swallow, and then "O swallow swallow," from the song in <i>The Princess</i>. The buried-alive passage has also been set beside "rats' alley" in Part II (line 115). Eliot wrote in 1936 that Tennyson had "the finest ear of any English poet since Milton."

### source whitman-lilacs
WHAT: The lilacs of line 2 and the hermit-thrush of line 357.
NOTE: Whitman's elegy for Lincoln, who was shot in April 1865, turns on three things: the lilac, the evening star and the hermit thrush singing in the swamp. Eliot's poem opens with lilacs bred out of dead land, and at line 357 it longs for the hermit-thrush's "water-dripping song." Eliot rarely admitted to reading Whitman. The lilacs and the thrush are the main evidence that he had, and had remembered this poem.

### source webster-white-devil
NOTE: Webster's tragedy is about Vittoria Corombona, the "white devil" of the title, and her murderous lover Brachiano. Cornelia sings the dirge over her son Marcello, killed by his brother Flamineo. Flamineo gives the speech about widows as he rises from a feigned death, having just learned that his sister would have let him die. Eliot's line 74 turns Webster's wolf, "foe to men," into a Dog, "friend to men." Eliot wrote about Webster in his essays on the Elizabethan dramatists and in "Whispers of Immortality": "Webster was much possessed by death / And saw the skull beneath the skin."

### source shakespeare-tempest
NOTE: Ferdinand, washed ashore after the shipwreck, believes his father Alonso has drowned. Ariel's song tells him so, and it is not true: Alonso is alive, and by the end of the play the shipwreck has done everyone good. Eliot quotes the song at lines 48 and 125 and Ferdinand's speech at lines 191 and 257. In Eliot's poem the drowning is not undone. The father with pearls for eyes becomes the drowned Phoenician Sailor of line 47 and Phlebas of Part IV; Ferdinand weeping on the bank becomes the man fishing behind the gashouse at lines 189–191.

### source frazer
NOTE: Frazer's comparative study of magic and religion ran to twelve volumes in its third edition; Eliot's headnote says it "has influenced our generation profoundly." Frazer argued that behind the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris, and by implication of Christ, lay one agricultural story: a god who dies with the harvest and returns in spring, helped by ritual, whether a king sacrificed or an effigy buried or drowned. The volumes Eliot names describe the "gardens of Adonis," pots of quick-sprouting seed thrown into the sea, and effigies of Osiris "made of earth and corn," buried to sprout. Eliot's note on line 46 links the Hanged Man with Frazer's Hanged God. The corpse planted in Stetson's garden (line 71) and Phlebas in the current (Part IV) are read against the Adonis gardens and the Osiris effigies. The title and opening page of <i>The Golden Bough</i> come from Turner's painting of that name (1834). Frazer describes it as a view of the lake of Nemi; it in fact shows Lake Avernus, with the Sibyl.

### source weston
NOTE: Jessie Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance. Under Frazer's influence she came to believe that the Grail stories preserved the remains of an ancient fertility cult: a wounded king, a land gone waste, a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics, and a rite of initiation whose secrets travelled across Europe with Syrian merchants. Almost no medievalist now accepts the argument, but the poem's debt does not depend on its being right. Eliot's note says the book suggested "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism"; thirty-four years later he said he regretted "having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail." The chapters most directly behind the poem are II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel).

### source petronius
NOTE: The speaker is Trimalchio, a freed slave turned millionaire, showing off to his guests between courses; his dinner party is the centre of what survives of the <i>Satyricon</i>. The Sibyl of Cumae, in Ovid's telling, asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand and forgot to ask for youth, and shrank with age until only her voice was left. Trimalchio's claim to have seen her with his own eyes is one of his boasts.

### source dickens-omf
NOTE: Betty Higden is praising the foundling Sloppy, who reads her the police-court reports from the newspaper and does all the parts. Eliot wrote the sentence across the top of the typescript of Parts I and II. It names the poem's method: many voices, done one after another by a single reader. Dickens's novel opens with a body being pulled out of the Thames.

### source conrad-hod
WHAT: The typescript's first epigraph.
NOTE: Kurtz's last words, as Marlow reports them, stood at the head of the typescript. Pound doubted whether Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation"; Eliot defended it as "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," then replaced it with Petronius. "The heart of light" at line 41 is usually read as an echo of Conrad's title. The book opens on a boat on the Thames at Gravesend at dusk, with London behind it; Part III of the poem is on the same river.

### source eliot-frontiers
NOTE: Eliot gave the lecture at the University of Minnesota in 1956, to an audience of nearly fourteen thousand. Looking back thirty-four years, he said the notes had begun as a list of his references, meant to answer critics who had called him a plagiarist, and had then been expanded to fill pages when the poem proved too short to make a book on its own. He also admitted they could not now be removed: readers had "bought" the notes with the poem.

### voice poem
NOTE: The voice that opens the poem, describes the room at the start of Part II, speaks the elegy for Phlebas in Part IV, and interprets the thunder in Part V. It belongs to no named character.

### draft title-draft
TITLE: The title that was dropped
BODY: Across the top of the typescript of the first two parts Eliot typed "He Do the Police in Different Voices," a line from Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i>. "The Waste Land" replaced it. The phrase comes from the Grail legend: the blighted country of Weston's book, and of Malory, where the land is laid waste by the Dolorous Stroke. The Dickens title was not generally known until the drafts were published in 1971. [scholar: confirm]

### draft epigraph-draft
BODY: Kurtz's "The horror! the horror!" from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i> stood at the head of the typescript. Pound doubted Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation"; Eliot called it "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," and then substituted the Sibyl from Petronius.

### card belladonna (artist's note)
ANSWERS: 49–50. "The Lady of the Rocks" recalls Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>, and Walter Pater's Mona Lisa, who "is older than the rocks among which she sits." Belladonna is the plant, the poison, the cosmetic and "beautiful lady."

### card three-staves (artist's note)
ANSWERS: 51. Eliot's note ties the Man with Three Staves to the Fisher King, so the three staves here are a crutch for his wound, a rod to fish with, and a rod of office planted in the waste ground. The composition follows Pamela Colman Smith's Three of Wands in the Rider pack of 1909.

### card wheel (artist's note)
ANSWERS, last sentence: The four stations are the medieval wheel of fortune's <i>regno, regnavi, sum sine regno, regnabo</i>: I reign, I have reigned, I have no kingdom, I shall reign.
