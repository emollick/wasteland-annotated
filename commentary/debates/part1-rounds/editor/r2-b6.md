# Editor, after round two: batch 6 (the card notes in the tarot tool)

Only the items changed after round two are given here; the rest stands as in r1-b6.md. Each item: the objections, the decision, the draft for round three, the last.

### card-note phoenician-sailor
Reader: the two-"says" sentence split; "wrongly believes drowned". Both taken.
NOTE: He is not a card in any Tarot pack. Eliot's note on line 46 says he appears later; he is Phlebas the Phoenician of Part IV. The note on line 218 says that the one-eyed merchant "melts into" him and that he is "not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples" in <i>The Tempest</i>. "Those are pearls that were his eyes" is from Ariel's song to Ferdinand about his father, whom Ferdinand wrongly believes drowned. Madame Sosostris says "Fear death by water," and Eliot's note adds that "Death by Water is executed in Part IV."
DECK (last sentence only): He is drawn head down on purpose, in the posture of the Hanged Man on a tarot card, which is the card Madame Sosostris cannot find.

### card-note belladonna
Reader: "usually" twice. Taken.
NOTE: Belladonna means "beautiful lady." It is also deadly nightshade, and the drops made from it that women once used to widen their pupils. "The Lady of the Rocks" is usually traced to Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>, and the card is often taken to foretell the woman at her dressing-table at the start of Part II. Nobody has settled what "the lady of situations" means.

### card-note blank
Reader: the Weston sentence split, "on her account" replaced. Scholar: the quoted words are Cumont's, quoted by Weston. Both taken.
NOTE: Madame Sosostris is forbidden to see what the merchant carries on his back, and the poem never says. Weston argued in <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> that the old Mysteries of Attis and Mithra travelled the Roman empire with Syrian merchants, "founding their chapels at the same time as their counting-houses", as she quotes Cumont. Following her, Cleanth Brooks (1939) read this merchant as carrying a mystery he knows nothing of.
DECK (last sentence only): Hold the pointer over it, or touch it, and the bundle shows more clearly.

### card-note hanged-man
Scholar: Brooks does not read the card's absence; give him what he said and the absence-reading to readers since. The batch 2 scholar, who reached the text, found Brooks's own explanation of her failure, the hood; it is used here. Reader: "missing ... missing ... reading" made plain. All taken.
NOTE: He is card XII of the major arcana. In the Rider pack he is a young man hanging by one foot from a living tree, calm-faced and haloed. Eliot's note on line 46 links him to "the Hanged God of Frazer", the god whose death renews the year, and to the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. Madame Sosostris does not find him. Cleanth Brooks (1939) explained that by the hood ("That he is hooded accounts for Madame Sosostris' inability to see him"); readers since Brooks have taken the absent card for the one thing her reading lacks, a death that is followed by a return.
DECK: unchanged, with "a green shoot on the dead bough" added at the end if the drawing has it.

### card-note crowd-in-a-ring
Scholar: the maypole struck, nothing being behind it. Reader's "more loosely" is then moot; the Dante gloss is given in Dante's words, as in the gloss on line 56.
NOTE: This is not a card but something she sees. Eliot's note on line 46 says the "crowds of people" appear later: the crowd flowing over London Bridge at line 62 is the first, and the "hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" at lines 369–70 are usually taken to be another. The ring has been compared to the souls in <i>Inferno</i> III who lived "without infamy and without praise" and run for ever after a banner.
