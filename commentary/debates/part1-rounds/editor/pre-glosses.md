# Editor's first drafts of the glosses, written before the round-one reports came in (to be revised against them)

### title
TITLE: The title
BODY:
Eliot's first note says that the title, "the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism" came from Jessie L. Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920). Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve an older fertility rite: a land laid waste because its king is wounded or impotent, and healed when a stranger comes and asks the right question.

The typescript of the first two parts carried a different title, "He Do the Police in Different Voices", from Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (1865), where Betty Higden says of the boy Sloppy: "You mightn't think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices."

### epigraph
TITLE: The Sibyl at Cumae
BODY:
Petronius, <i>Satyricon</i> 48. The speaker is Trimalchio, the rich freedman whose dinner party fills the surviving middle of the book, and the sentence is one of his boasts to his guests. In Ovid (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV) the Sibyl of Cumae asked Apollo for as many years of life as there were grains in a handful of sand and did not ask for youth; she withered until, in Trimalchio's story, she could be kept in a jar. The handful of sand has been set beside the "handful of dust" of line 30.

The typescript had a different epigraph: Kurtz's last words from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, ending "The horror! the horror!" Pound wrote in January 1922 that he doubted "if Conrad is weighty enough to stand the citation". Eliot answered that it was "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative", and then replaced it with Petronius.

### dedication
TITLE: The better craftsman
BODY:
<i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, 117. On the terrace of the lustful, Guido Guinizelli points out to Dante the Provençal poet Arnaut Daniel as "miglior fabbro del parlar materno", a better craftsman of the mother tongue. The same canto gives the poem line 428, "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina", Arnaut going back into the fire.

The dedication is not in the 1922 printings. Eliot wrote it in Pound's copy of the Boni & Liveright edition in January 1923 and first printed it in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>. Pound had worked over the typescript in Paris in January 1922 and by post afterwards, and cut it by about half. His letter on the result, dated by his own invented calendar and read as either 24 December 1921 or 24 January 1922, begins "Complimenti, you bitch. I am wracked by the seven jealousies."

### april
TITLE: Chaucer's April
BODY:
The General Prologue to <i>The Canterbury Tales</i> opens with April's showers ending the drought of March, sap rising, and people setting out on pilgrimage. Eliot's line keeps the rain and the roots and calls the month cruel; lines 5 to 7 say that winter was kinder. Five of the first seven lines end on a participle (breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding).

Other Aprils have been heard behind the line. Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" (1865) mourns Lincoln with lilacs and a hermit thrush, and the thrush is at line 357 of this poem. Rupert Brooke's "The Old Vicarage, Grantchester" (1912) opens with the lilac in bloom; Brooke died in April 1915. The "us" of line 5 is not identified.

### boston-cut
TITLE: What came before "April"
BODY:
In the typescript, "April is the cruellest month" is not the first line. Fifty-four lines come before it: a night out in Boston in a young man's voice, with a bar, a burlesque show, a run from a policeman and a walk home at sunrise. The passage is struck through in pencil, and the cancellation is generally taken to be Eliot's own, made before Pound saw the typescript. The manuscript is in the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library and is reproduced in the facsimile edition of 1971, edited by Valerie Eliot.

### starnbergersee
TITLE: Munich, summer 1911
BODY:
The Starnberger See is the lake south of Munich. The Hofgarten is the court garden in the middle of the city, with an arcade along two sides, the "colonnade" of line 9. Eliot was in Munich in July and August 1911, at the end of his year in Paris, and finished "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" there. King Ludwig II of Bavaria, Wagner's patron, was found drowned in the Starnberger See in June 1886; the poem does not mention him, and the connection is one that commentators have drawn.

### marie
TITLE: Countess Marie Larisch
BODY:
Valerie Eliot's note in the 1971 facsimile records that Eliot had met Countess Marie Larisch (1858–1940), a niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria, and that the sledding story and the tone of these lines came from her conversation rather than from any book. Larisch had carried messages between her cousin Crown Prince Rudolf and Mary Vetsera, who died together at Mayerling in 1889; her memoir <i>My Past</i> (1913) made her notorious. "The archduke's, my cousin's" fits Rudolf.

### litauen
TITLE: A sentence in German
BODY:
Nobody in the poem is named as saying this, and nobody answers it. After 1918 the borders of the Baltic had moved, and a member of the German-speaking gentry of Lithuania could be Russian by former passport, Lithuanian by birthplace and German by descent, and might want to say so.

### son-of-man
TITLE: Ezekiel and Ecclesiastes
BODY:
Eliot's note refers to Ezekiel 2:1, where God addresses the prophet as "son of man" before sending him to "a rebellious nation". "A heap of broken images" recalls Ezekiel 6:4–6, where the idols of Israel are to be broken; the dry bones of Ezekiel 37 return at line 391. The speaker changes at line 19 without announcement.

Eliot's note on line 23 refers to Ecclesiastes 12:5, in the chapter on old age: "the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail: because man goeth to his long home, and the mourners go about the streets." Eliot's cricket stands where the Bible has the grasshopper.

### red-rock
TITLE: The red rock, from an earlier poem
BODY:
"Come in under the shadow of this red rock" comes from "The Death of Saint Narcissus", a poem Eliot set in type for <i>Poetry</i> in 1915 and withdrew. There the rock is grey, and the speaker offers to show something other than the listener's own two shadows, one on the sand at daybreak and one behind a fire against a red rock. Commentators also hear Isaiah 32:2, "the shadow of a great rock in a weary land", and, in "a handful of dust", Tennyson's <i>Maud</i> ("And my heart is a handful of dust") and the Sibyl's handful of sand in the epigraph.

### frisch-weht
TITLE: The sailor's song
BODY:
<i>Tristan und Isolde</i>, Act I, scene 1. A young sailor, unseen aloft, sings as Tristan's ship carries Isolde from Ireland to Cornwall, where she is to marry King Marke; Isolde takes the song as mockery of her. Eliot's note gives the reference and nothing more. The shepherd's line from Act III is at line 42.

### hyacinth-girl
TITLE: The hyacinth garden
BODY:
Hyacinthus, in Ovid (<i>Metamorphoses</i> X), is the boy Apollo loved and killed by accident with a discus; the flower grew from his blood. Frazer counts him among the dying gods of the spring. None of this is needed to read the passage, which is one of the few in the poem where a speaker reports feeling something. "The heart of light" has been read against Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, whose closing words were the poem's first epigraph.

Two people have been proposed for the garden. Lyndall Gordon connects it with Emily Hale, whom Eliot knew in Boston before he left for Europe in 1914; his letters to her, opened at Princeton in 2020, show how long the attachment lasted. Others have proposed Jean Verdenal, Eliot's friend in Paris in 1910 and 1911, killed at the Dardanelles in 1915, to whom <i>Prufrock and Other Observations</i> is dedicated; in 1934 Eliot recalled him crossing the Luxembourg Gardens "waving a branch of lilac". The poem gives no name.

### oed-und-leer
TITLE: The shepherd's line
BODY:
<i>Tristan und Isolde</i>, Act III, scene 1. Tristan lies wounded in Brittany waiting for Isolde's ship, and a shepherd is to pipe a merry tune when a sail appears. Asked by Kurwenal whether he sees anything, he answers with these words. Eliot's note, "Id. III, verse 24", gives the place. Line 31 has a ship setting out; here the sea is empty, and the hyacinth garden lies between the two.

### sosostris
TITLE: Madame Sosostris
BODY:
The name is usually traced to Aldous Huxley's <i>Crome Yellow</i>, published in November 1921, where Mr Scogan tells fortunes at a village fair as "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana". Sesostris was a legendary Egyptian king. "Clairvoyante", with the French feminine ending, was the trade's own word. What she deals comes true later in the poem, as Eliot's note on line 46 sets out.

"One must be so careful these days" (line 59): fortune-telling for money was an offence under the Vagrancy Act of 1824, and palmists and mediums were prosecuted in London in the 1920s. The line has been read as her caution about the police.

### tarot
TITLE: The cards
BODY:
Eliot's note says he did not know the Tarot well and "departed from it to suit my own convenience". The cards she deals:

<b>The drowned Phoenician Sailor.</b> Not in the Tarot. Eliot's note on line 218 links him to the one-eyed merchant and to Ferdinand in <i>The Tempest</i>, whose father is the drowned man of Ariel's song (line 48). Phlebas in Part IV is a Phoenician.

<b>Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks.</b> Not in the Tarot. "Belladonna" is Italian for beautiful lady, the name of the poisonous nightshade, and the name of the eye-drops made from it that widen the pupils. "The Lady of the Rocks" has been read as Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>.

<b>The man with three staves.</b> The Three of Wands, a card of the pack; in the 1909 Rider pack a figure on a headland with his back to the viewer, watching ships. Eliot's note associates him, "quite arbitrarily", with the Fisher King.

<b>The Wheel.</b> The Wheel of Fortune, trump X. Compare line 320, "O you who turn the wheel and look to windward".

<b>The one-eyed merchant.</b> Not in the Tarot. Eliot's note on line 218 makes him Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant of line 209.

<b>The Hanged Man.</b> Trump XII, a young man hanging by one foot from a living tree. Eliot's note connects him with Frazer's Hanged God and with the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. Sosostris does not find him. "Fear death by water": Part IV is called Death by Water, and in Weston's account water is what the waste land lacks.

The card shown is Pamela Colman Smith's design for the pack published by William Rider & Son in London in 1909.

### pearls
TITLE: Ariel's song
BODY:
<i>The Tempest</i>, Act I scene 2. Ariel sings to Ferdinand, who believes his father drowned in the wreck; the father is alive. The line is quoted again at 125, and Eliot's note on line 218 says the Phoenician Sailor "is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples". In the song a "sea-change" is a change for the better; commentators have noticed that the poem's drowned men get none.

### ring
TITLE: Crowds of people
BODY:
Not a card. Eliot's note on line 46 says the "crowds of people" appear later; they do at line 62, on London Bridge, and the "hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" at line 369 have been read with them. The ring has been set beside Dante's neutrals running after a banner in <i>Inferno</i> III and beside the ring dances in Frazer.

### unreal-city
TITLE: Baudelaire's city
BODY:
The opening of "Les Sept Vieillards" ("The Seven Old Men") in the <i>Tableaux parisiens</i> of <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i> (1861), in which the poet, walking in a fog, meets the same old man seven times. Eliot said in 1950 that Baudelaire had shown him "the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric". The brown fog is London's coal smoke, which Eliot walked through to Lloyds Bank in Lombard Street from 1917. "Unreal City" recurs at line 207, and "Unreal" alone at line 377.

### london-bridge-dead
TITLE: Dante's crowd
BODY:
<i>Inferno</i> III, 55–57. Inside the gate, before the river Acheron, Dante sees the souls who lived "without infamy and without praise" and is astonished at their number. Line 64 is from the next canto (IV, 25–27), the Limbo of the virtuous unbaptised, where there is no weeping, only sighs. Eliot's notes give both places. The crowd crossing London Bridge into the City is the morning crowd of clerks; Eliot was one of them from 1917.

### woolnoth
TITLE: St Mary Woolnoth
BODY:
King William Street runs from London Bridge up to the Bank. At its top, on the corner of Lombard Street, stands Nicholas Hawksmoor's St Mary Woolnoth (1716–27). Lloyds Bank, where Eliot worked in the Colonial and Foreign Department from 1917 to 1925, was across the road at 71 Lombard Street. Eliot's note on the "dead sound" reads, in full, "A phenomenon which I have often noticed." The church's crypt was cleared in 1897–1900 for the Bank station of the City and South London Railway, and the bodies were reburied at Ilford.

### stetson
TITLE: Stetson, and Mylae
BODY:
Mylae, off the north coast of Sicily, was the first sea battle of the First Punic War, in 260 BC, and Rome's first victory over Carthage at sea. The friend met in the City of London was "in the ships" there; the poem does not explain the gap of twenty-two centuries. Stetson has been identified with Pound, who wore a wide-brimmed hat, but nothing in the poem requires it. The name is that of an American hat.

### corpse
TITLE: The buried god
BODY:
Frazer, in <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, the volumes Eliot's headnote names, describes the "gardens of Adonis", pots of quick-sprouting seed carried in the god's funeral procession and thrown into the sea, and the "Osiris beds", effigies of the god filled with earth and corn and buried so that the sprouting grain showed his return. Stetson's corpse, and the question whether it will bloom, are usually read against these.

### dog
TITLE: Webster's dirge
BODY:
<i>The White Devil</i> (1612), Act V scene 4: Cornelia's song over her murdered son Marcello. Webster has "keep the wolf far thence, that's foe to men"; Eliot has the Dog, friend to men, with a capital letter. Some readers have taken the Dog for Sirius, the Dog Star, whose rising marked the flooding of the Nile. Eliot's note gives the reference to Webster and nothing more.

### hypocrite-lecteur
TITLE: Baudelaire's reader
BODY:
The last line of "Au Lecteur", the poem that opens <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i> (1857). Baudelaire's list of the reader's vices ends with ennui, and then turns on the reader as an accomplice: "mon semblable, mon frère". Eliot's note gives the reference. Part I ends by addressing the reader in Baudelaire's words, in French.
