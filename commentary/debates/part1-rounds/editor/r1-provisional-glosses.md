# Editor's drafts of the glosses after the reader's round one (provisional: the scholar's round-one corrections still to be folded in)

Wording follows the reader where the reader gave a draft; facts and hedges marked [scholar] await the scholar.

### title
TITLE: Where the title comes from
BODY:
Eliot's first note credits the title, "the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism" to Jessie L. Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920). Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve, beneath their Christian story, a much older fertility rite: a land lies waste because its king is wounded or impotent, and is healed only when a stranger arrives and asks the right question.

The typescript carried a different working title at the head of the first two parts: <i>He Do the Police in Different Voices</i>. The words are Betty Higden's praise of the boy Sloppy in Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (1865): "You mightn't think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices." The working title was dropped before publication.

### epigraph
TITLE: The Sibyl at Cumae
BODY:
The speaker is Trimalchio, the rich freedman who hosts the dinner in Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i> (first century AD), and the story is one of his boasts. In Ovid's telling (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV) the Sibyl of Cumae had asked Apollo for as many years of life as there were grains in a handful of sand, and forgot to ask for youth; in Trimalchio's she has shrunk until she can be kept in a jar. The boys' question and her answer are in Greek in Petronius's Latin. Her handful of sand has been set beside the "handful of dust" at line 30. [scholar: confirm Ovid XIV and the Greek]

This was not the first epigraph. The typescript carried Kurtz's dying words from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, ending "The horror! the horror!" Pound wrote in January 1922 that he doubted "if Conrad is weighty enough to stand the citation." Eliot answered that it was "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," and then replaced it anyway.

### dedication
TITLE: The better craftsman
BODY:
The line is <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, 117. The poet Guido Guinizelli, doing penance among the lustful in the refining fire, points Dante toward Arnaut Daniel, the Provençal troubadour, and calls him the better smith of his native speech. The same canto is quoted near the end of the poem: "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina" at line 428 is Arnaut stepping back into the flames.

The dedication is not in the 1922 printings. Eliot wrote it by hand in Pound's copy of the Boni & Liveright edition in January 1923 and first printed it in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>. Pound worked over the typescript in Paris in January 1922 and by post afterwards, and cut it by about half. His letter on the result is dated in a calendar of his own devising, and editors have read the date as 24 December 1921 or as 24 January 1922: "Complimenti, you bitch. I am wracked by the seven jealousies."

### april
TITLE: Chaucer's April, reversed
BODY:
Chaucer's opening welcomes April. Its showers end the drought of March, the sap rises, and people set out on pilgrimages. Eliot's first line is usually read as an answer to it. The rain is still there ("stirring / Dull roots with spring rain"), but it is unwelcome, since it stirs "memory and desire" where the winter snow was "forgetful". Five of the first seven lines end on a participle (breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding) whose object is held over to the next line.

Commentators also cite two other poems with lilacs. Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" (1865) mourns Lincoln with lilacs, spring and a hermit thrush; the thrush returns at line 357 of this poem. Rupert Brooke's "The Old Vicarage, Grantchester" (1912) opens "Just now the lilac is in bloom"; Brooke died in April 1915. The "us" of line 5 is never identified.

### boston-cut
TITLE: What came before "April"
BODY:
In the typescript, "April is the cruellest month" was not the first line. Fifty-four lines came before it, describing a night on the town in Boston in the voice of a young man out with his friends, who drink in a bar, see a burlesque show, run drunk from a policeman and watch the sun rise. The whole passage is struck through in pencil, by Eliot himself in the judgement of the facsimile's editor, before Pound saw it. [scholar: confirm whose judgement] The manuscript is in the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library and is reproduced in the 1971 facsimile edited by Valerie Eliot.

### starnbergersee
TITLE: Munich, August 1911
BODY:
The Starnberger See (one word in the poem) is the long lake south of Munich; the Hofgarten is the court garden in the city centre, with an arcaded walk on two sides, which is probably the "colonnade" the speakers shelter in. Eliot spent August 1911 in Munich, at the end of his year in Paris, and finished "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" there. [scholar: July as well?] King Ludwig II of Bavaria, Wagner's patron and the builder of Neuschwanstein, was found drowned in the lake in June 1886. The poem does not mention him. It quotes Wagner's <i>Tristan und Isolde</i> at lines 31 to 34 and 42, and Madame Sosostris says "Fear death by water" at line 55.

### marie
TITLE: Countess Marie Larisch
BODY:
Valerie Eliot, in the facsimile edition, reported that her husband had met Countess Marie Larisch (1858–1940), a niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria, and that the sledding story and the tone of these lines came from her conversation, not from any book. Larisch had been the go-between in the affair of her cousin Crown Prince Rudolf and Mary Vetsera, which ended with both dead at Mayerling in 1889; her memoir <i>My Past</i> (1913) made her notorious. "The archduke's, my cousin's" fits Rudolf.

### litauen
TITLE: German, not Russian
BODY:
The line is in German and no speaker is named for it; the site's Voices lens treats it as overheard, but it can also be read as Marie's. "Russin" is the feminine form, so the speaker is a woman. After 1918 the old multinational empires had become new nation states, and a woman from the Baltic gentry could be Russian by passport, Lithuanian by birthplace and German by blood. This one insists on the last.

### son-of-man
TITLE: "Son of man" in Ezekiel
BODY:
Eliot's note points to Ezekiel 2:1, where God addresses the prophet as "son of man" before sending him to a rebellious people. Chapter 37 of the same book has the prophet prophesy over a valley of dry bones until they stand up as an army; line 391, "Dry bones can harm no one," is usually referred to it. "A heap of broken images" also recalls Ezekiel, where the Lord promises to break the idols of Israel (6:4–6). The speaker changes here, from Marie to a prophetic voice that Eliot used again in "Ash-Wednesday" and the choruses of <i>The Rock</i>.

Eliot's second note, on line 23, points to Ecclesiastes 12:5, the chapter on old age, where "the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail: because man goeth to his long home, and the mourners go about the streets." Eliot has a cricket where Ecclesiastes has a grasshopper.

### red-rock
TITLE: Eliot borrowing from himself
BODY:
"Come in under the shadow of this red rock" is lifted, almost word for word, from "The Death of Saint Narcissus", a poem Eliot wrote in 1915 and withdrew from publication. There the invitation is to come in under the shadow of a grey rock and be shown something different from two shadows: one sprawling over the sand at daybreak, the other leaping behind a fire against a red rock. In <i>The Waste Land</i> the rock of the invitation is the red one. "Shadow under this red rock" also recalls Isaiah 32:2, "the shadow of a great rock in a weary land". One reading takes the morning and evening shadows as the two ends of a life. "A handful of dust" at line 30 recalls the Sibyl's handful of sand in the epigraph, and Tennyson's <i>Maud</i>: "Dead, long dead, / Long dead! / And my heart is a handful of dust."

### frisch-weht
TITLE: The sailor's song from the masthead
BODY:
Act I of Wagner's <i>Tristan und Isolde</i> opens with this song. A young sailor sings it from high in the rigging, unseen, as Tristan's ship carries Isolde from Ireland to Cornwall to marry King Mark, whom she does not love. Isolde hears it as mockery. The wind blows toward the sailor's home and away from hers. Eliot's note gives the reference, verses 5 to 8 of Act I, and nothing else. At this point in the opera Tristan and Isolde are not yet lovers; by its end both are dead. Eliot quotes the opera once more at line 42, from its last act, after the hyacinth garden.

### hyacinth-girl
TITLE: The hyacinth garden
BODY:
Hyacinthus was the boy Apollo loved and killed by accident with a discus. The flower grew from his blood, and in Ovid its petals carry the letters AI AI, a cry of grief (<i>Metamorphoses</i> X). Sparta held a festival in his name, the Hyacinthia, and in Frazer's reading he is another of the dying and reviving gods. The passage itself takes nothing from the myth but the name. Its last phrase, "the heart of light", inverts the title of Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, whose closing words were the poem's first epigraph; Eliot used the phrase again in "Burnt Norton". [scholar: confirm Burnt Norton]

Two people in Eliot's life have been proposed for the figure in the garden, and the text names neither. Lyndall Gordon's biographies attach it to Emily Hale, the Bostonian Eliot loved before he sailed for Europe in 1914; his letters to her, unsealed at Princeton in 2020, show how long that attachment lasted. John Peter, in 1952, proposed Jean Verdenal, the friend of Eliot's Paris year, killed at the Dardanelles in 1915, to whom <i>Prufrock and Other Observations</i> is dedicated. [scholar: confirm Peter, or name whoever it should be] In a note in the <i>Criterion</i> in 1934 Eliot recalled a friend crossing the Luxembourg Gardens in the late afternoon "waving a branch of lilac", a friend who was afterwards killed at Gallipoli. Compare the lilacs of line 2.

### oed-und-leer
TITLE: The shepherd's answer
BODY:
The words are from Act III of <i>Tristan und Isolde</i>. Tristan lies dying in Brittany, waiting for Isolde's ship. A shepherd keeps watch and is to play a merry tune the moment a sail appears. Asked whether he sees anything, he answers with these four words. This quotation and the one at line 31 stand on either side of the hyacinth garden. That one comes from the opening of the opera, before Tristan and Isolde have fallen in love; this one from its last act, when they are apart and Tristan is dying. Eliot's note, "Id. III, verse 24," points back to the note on line 31: "Id." is short for <i>idem</i>, the same work.

### sosostris
TITLE: The clairvoyante with a cold
BODY:
The name is usually traced to Aldous Huxley's novel <i>Crome Yellow</i>, published in November 1921 while Eliot was drafting the poem. In it Mr. Scogan dresses up at a village fair as "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana" to tell fortunes. Sesostris was a legendary Egyptian pharaoh; Eliot's spelling and the head cold are his own. "Clairvoyante," with the French feminine ending, is the trade term; "the wisest woman in Europe" is the trade puff. The cards she deals reappear later in the poem; Eliot's note on line 46 says which.

Her parting line, "One must be so careful these days" (line 59), can be read as a practical worry. Fortune-telling for money was an offence under the Vagrancy Act of 1824, and prosecutions of palmists and mediums were routine in London in the 1920s. [scholar: confirm the Act and the prosecutions]

### tarot
TITLE: The cards she deals
BODY:
Eliot's note says he was "not familiar with the exact constitution of the Tarot pack" and had departed from it "to suit my own convenience." The cards, in the order she names them, are these.

<b>The drowned Phoenician Sailor.</b> There is no such card in the Tarot. Eliot's note on line 46 says the Sailor "appears later": he is Phlebas the Phoenician, who drowns in Part IV. The note on line 218 adds that the one-eyed merchant "melts into" the Sailor, who "is not wholly distinct from" Ferdinand in <i>The Tempest</i>. The words in brackets at line 48 are from a song telling Ferdinand that his father has drowned; see the note there.

<b>Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks.</b> Belladonna is Italian for "beautiful lady". It is also the name of the poisonous nightshade, and of the eye-drops made from it, which women used to dilate their pupils. "The Lady of the Rocks" recalls Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>. The card has been identified with the woman of "A Game of Chess" in Part II. [scholar: name, or cut]

<b>The man with three staves.</b> This is the Three of Wands, which is a card in the pack. In the 1909 design a figure stands on a headland with his back to us, watching ships. Eliot's note says he associated it, "quite arbitrarily," with the Fisher King.

<b>The Wheel.</b> This is the Wheel of Fortune, card X. Compare "O you who turn the wheel" at line 320.

<b>The one-eyed merchant.</b> There is no such card in the pack. Eliot's note on line 46 says the Merchant appears later; he is Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant, at line 209. Neither the poem nor Eliot's note says what the blank card, or the thing on his back, is.

<b>The Hanged Man.</b> Card XII. In the design shown here a young man hangs by one foot from a living tree, his face calm and a halo round his head. Eliot's note links him to the Hanged God of Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i>, a god killed so that the land may revive, and to the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. Madame Sosostris cannot find him, so the card that on Eliot's account stands for the god's death and return is the one missing from her reading. "Fear death by water" is the only advice she gives. Eliot's note says that "Death by Water is executed in Part IV", where the Phoenician sailor drowns.

The card shown here is Pamela Colman Smith's design for the pack published in London by William Rider & Son in 1909, the Tarot that an English buyer of the 1910s would have found in a shop.

### pearls
TITLE: Ariel's song
BODY:
The song is from <i>The Tempest</i>, Act I scene ii. Ariel sings it to Ferdinand, who believes his father, the King of Naples, drowned in the wreck. The father is alive; the song is Ariel's invention. Eliot quotes the line again at line 125, and his note on line 218 says the Phoenician Sailor "is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples." Compare Part IV, where the drowned Phlebas's bones are picked by the current.

### ring
TITLE: Crowds in a ring
BODY:
Madame Sosostris does not deal this; she says she sees it. Eliot's note on line 46 says the "crowds of people" appear later. They are the crowd on London Bridge at line 62 and the "hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" at line 369. Eliot's note on line 63 cites <i>Inferno</i> III, where the souls who were neither good nor evil run for ever behind a whirling banner.

### unreal-city
TITLE: Baudelaire's swarming city
BODY:
The opening of "Les Sept Vieillards" ("The Seven Old Men"), in the <i>Tableaux parisiens</i> section of <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i>. The poet, walking in a yellow fog, meets the same hideous old man seven times over. In "What Dante Means to Me" (1950) Eliot said that Baudelaire had taught him "the poetical possibilities" of "the more sordid aspects of the modern metropolis". [scholar: confirm the talk and date] The lines that follow do this. London Bridge, King William Street and St Mary Woolnoth are real places, and the crowd on them is taken from Dante's dead.

The fog is coal smoke. Eliot walked through it into the City every working morning from 1917, to Lloyds Bank in Lombard Street. "Unreal City" returns at line 207, at a winter noon, and at line 377 as the single word "Unreal."

### london-bridge-dead
TITLE: The crowd at the gate of Hell
BODY:
<i>Inferno</i> III, lines 55–57. Just inside the gate, before the river, Dante sees the neutrals, the souls who lived "without infamy and without praise," and is astonished at how many there are. Line 64, "Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled," comes from the next canto, the Limbo of the virtuous unbaptised, where "there was no weeping but only sighs, which made the eternal air tremble" (IV, 25–27). Eliot's crowd is the morning crowd from London Bridge station into the City, which he walked in himself on his way to Lloyds Bank. Its size comes from the first passage and its sighs from the second. One reading is that the commuters are like Dante's neutrals, people who never chose anything.

### woolnoth
TITLE: St Mary Woolnoth
BODY:
King William Street runs from London Bridge up to the Bank, and at its top, on the corner of Lombard Street, stands Nicholas Hawksmoor's St Mary Woolnoth (1716–27), a squat church with two little turrets and its stone cut in deep bands. Lloyds Bank, where Eliot worked in the Colonial and Foreign Department from 1917 to 1925, was across the road, and the church's clock struck the start of his working day. Eliot's note on line 68 says only: "A phenomenon which I have often noticed."

When the Bank station of the City and South London Railway was built beneath the church in 1897–1900, the crypt was cleared, the bodies were reburied at Ilford, and the crypt became the station's booking hall.

### stetson
TITLE: Stetson, and the ships at Mylae
BODY:
Mylae was a sea battle of 260 BC, early in the First Punic War, in which Rome beat Carthage at sea for the first time. Rome and Carthage were trading empires, and the war was a war over trade. Stetson is met in the City of London, the financial centre of the British Empire, four years after the end of the war of 1914–18. The usual reading is that the ancient war stands for the recent one, and that the man who was "in the ships" at Mylae had been in that war too. Stetson is an American name and the name of an American hat. He has sometimes been taken for Pound, who wore wide-brimmed hats, but nothing in the poem needs him to be anyone in particular. [scholar: who made the Pound identification]

### corpse
TITLE: The buried god
BODY:
In <i>The Golden Bough</i>, in the volumes Eliot's headnote names (<i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>), Frazer describes two rites of a god who dies and returns. The "gardens of Adonis" were pots of quick-sprouting seed, carried in the god's funeral procession and then thrown into the sea. The "Osiris beds" were effigies of the god filled with earth and corn and buried, so that the sprouting grain showed his resurrection. The corpse planted in Stetson's garden is usually read as a version of these rites, a body buried so that something will grow from it. The questions about blooming and frost are the ones a man asks about his bulbs.

### dog
TITLE: Webster's dirge, altered
BODY:
Cornelia's mad song over her murdered son in <i>The White Devil</i> (1612), Act V scene iv. Eliot swaps the wolf for a dog and "foe" for "friend," and capitalises the Dog. In Webster the wolf digs up the unburied dead. In Eliot the danger to the corpse in the garden is the dog, man's friend. Some commentators take the capital D to point to the Dog Star, Sirius, whose rising announced the flooding of the Nile and the return of Osiris. [scholar: a name] In "Philip Massinger" (1920) Eliot wrote that "immature poets imitate; mature poets steal."

### hypocrite-lecteur
TITLE: The reader accused
BODY:
The last line of "Au Lecteur," the poem that opens <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i> (1857). Baudelaire's catalogue of vices ends with the worst of them, boredom, and then turns on the reader and makes him an accomplice. Eliot ends Part I with the same turn. The "you" who has been Stetson becomes the reader of the poem. One reading is that the line is left in French on purpose, since only a reader who knows Baudelaire will recognise the charge.
