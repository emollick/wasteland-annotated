### source chaucer
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: The Canterbury Tales, General Prologue
AUTHOR: Geoffrey Chaucer
DATE: c. 1387
WHAT: Chaucer’s April of sweet showers and pilgrimages, behind Eliot’s “cruellest month.”
LINES: 1
NOTE: The Prologue opens with April rain ending the drought of March, sap rising, birds singing through the night and people longing to go on pilgrimage. Eliot’s notes say nothing of Chaucer; readers have long set “April is the cruellest month” against these lines, in which the same month and the same rain are welcome. In Eliot’s poem it is winter that “kept us warm” (lines 5–6).

### source ecclesiastes
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. Part I had nothing to add.
TITLE: Ecclesiastes 12
AUTHOR: The Bible (King James Version)
DATE: 3rd century BC; translation 1611
WHAT: “the cricket no relief”: the chapter on old age and death.
LINES: 23
NOTE: Eliot’s note on line 23 cites verse 5, where “the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail”; the poem has a cricket in the grasshopper’s place. The chapter is an allegory of old age and death, and its pitcher broken at the fountain and wheel broken at the cistern have been heard again in the “empty cisterns and exhausted wells” of line 385.

### source tristan
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: Tristan und Isolde
AUTHOR: Richard Wagner
DATE: 1865 (first performed Munich)
WHAT: The sailor’s song (lines 31–34) and the shepherd’s “Öd und leer das Meer,” desolate and empty the sea (line 42).
LINES: 31, 42
NOTE: Eliot’s notes cite the libretto by act and verse: I, verses 5–8, and III, verse 24. The young sailor’s song is the first thing sung in the opera; the sailor sings it unseen, from the masthead, above the deck where Isolde lies. The shepherd’s “Öd und leer das Meer!” comes in Act III, answering Tristan’s servant Kurwenal, who has asked whether any ship is in sight while Tristan lies wounded and waiting for Isolde. In the poem the hyacinth garden (lines 35–41) falls between the two quotations. Eliot heard <i>Tristan</i>, or its music in concert, in Boston in the autumn of 1909, at the start of his graduate year at Harvard, and wrote a poem about it, “Opera,” dated November 1909; Stravinsky, who met him in 1956, thought <i>Tristan</i> “must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life.” Ludwig II of Bavaria, whose money paid for the first performance, was found dead in 1886 in the Starnberger See, the lake of line 8.

### source baudelaire-sept-vieillards
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. Part I had nothing to add.
TITLE: Les Sept Vieillards (from Les Fleurs du mal)
AUTHOR: Charles Baudelaire
DATE: 1859; in the 1861 edition of Les Fleurs du mal
WHAT: “Unreal City”: the swarming Paris of “The Seven Old Men.”
LINES: 60
NOTE: The poet, walking through a dirty yellow fog on a Paris morning, meets a hideous old man, then another exactly like him, and another, seven in all, and flees home half mad. Eliot’s note on line 60 quotes the first two lines. Stetson, hailed on King William Street at nine in the morning as a shipmate from the battle of Mylae (lines 69–70), has been read as Eliot’s version of Baudelaire’s daylight ghost.

### source baudelaire-au-lecteur
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: Au Lecteur (the preface poem of Les Fleurs du mal)
AUTHOR: Charles Baudelaire
DATE: 1857
WHAT: “hypocrite lecteur!—mon semblable,—mon frère!”
LINES: 76
NOTE: Baudelaire opens his book by listing the sins of mankind, saving the worst for last, boredom, and then turning on whoever is reading. Eliot closes his first section the same way. He said in 1930 that Baudelaire was “the greatest exemplar in modern poetry in any language,” and in 1950 that he had learned from him “the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric.”

### source death-of-st-narcissus
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: The Death of Saint Narcissus
AUTHOR: T. S. Eliot
DATE: 1915 (withdrawn from publication)
WHAT: “Come in under the shadow of this red rock.”
LINES: 25
NOTE: Set in type for <i>Poetry</i> (Chicago) in 1915 and withdrawn in proof; first published in 1950. The poem is about a saint who is sure he had been a tree, a fish and a young girl, and who dies pierced with arrows. The manuscript survived among the papers Eliot sent in 1922 to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector, with the <i>Waste Land</i> drafts, and is now in the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library. After these two lines the speaker promises to show the listener something other than the listener’s own shadow, sprawling on the sand at daybreak or leaping behind the fire. <i>The Waste Land</i> keeps the second line, with “gray” changed to “red,” and reshapes the first. The poem is still in copyright, so only its first two lines are given here.

### source huxley-crome-yellow
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: Crome Yellow, chapter 27
AUTHOR: Aldous Huxley
DATE: 1921
WHAT: “Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana”: the name behind Madame Sosostris.
LINES: 43
NOTE: Huxley’s country-house satire was published in November 1921. Its Mr. Scogan, a cynical guest who dresses up as an old gypsy woman to tell fortunes at the village fête under the name “Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana,” is the accepted source of Madame Sosostris. Asked about it by the critic Grover Smith, Eliot wrote to him on 10 March 1952 that he was “almost certain” he had taken the name from <i>Crome Yellow</i> without being conscious of doing so. Huxley and Eliot had known each other since the war years through Lady Ottoline Morrell’s circle at Garsington, her house near Oxford.

### source dante-inf3
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: Inferno, canto III
AUTHOR: Dante Alighieri
DATE: c. 1308
WHAT: The crowd on London Bridge: “I had not thought death had undone so many.”
LINES: 62, 63, 56
NOTE: Inside the gate (“Abandon all hope”) but before the river Acheron, Dante finds the souls who were neither good nor bad, “who lived without infamy and without praise,” rejected by Heaven and refused by Hell, running for ever after a banner and stung by wasps. Eliot’s note on line 63 quotes lines 55–57 as the source of the crowd flowing over London Bridge, and his note on line 46 says that the “crowds of people” in Madame Sosostris’s cards “appear later,” which has generally been taken to mean this crowd. Eliot said in 1950 that Dante’s poetry was still, “after forty years,” “the most persistent and deepest influence” on his own verse.

### source dante-inf4
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: Inferno, canto IV
AUTHOR: Dante Alighieri
DATE: c. 1308
WHAT: Limbo’s sighs, behind “Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled.”
LINES: 64
NOTE: Limbo, the first circle of Hell, holds the virtuous who died unbaptised, Homer, Virgil and Aristotle among them; Dante calls them the “great spirits” (<i>spiriti magni</i>), and they are not tormented but live in desire without hope. Eliot’s note on line 64 cites these lines. The comparison likens the sighs of the office crowd to those of Limbo, where there is grief but no torment, not to the cries of the damned.

### source ezekiel
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. Part I had nothing to add.
TITLE: Ezekiel 2 and 37
AUTHOR: The Bible (King James Version)
DATE: 6th century BC; translation 1611
WHAT: “Son of man”; “a heap of broken images”; the valley of dry bones.
LINES: 20, 22, 391
NOTE: Ezekiel, prophet of the Babylonian exile, is addressed by God some ninety times as “son of man,” that is, mortal man. Eliot’s note on line 20 cites 2:1, where God first speaks to him. The “heap of broken images” of line 22 has been traced to chapter 6, where the idols of Israel are to be destroyed (“your images shall be broken”), and the dry bones of line 391 to chapter 37, where the bones are joined, clothed with flesh and made to live.

### source isaiah
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. Part I had nothing to add.
TITLE: Isaiah 32 and 38
AUTHOR: The Bible (King James Version)
DATE: 8th century BC; translation 1611
WHAT: “the shadow of a great rock in a weary land”; “Set thine house in order.”
LINES: 25, 426
NOTE: Eliot’s notes do not cite Isaiah. The “shadow of a great rock in a weary land” of chapter 32 has long been recognised behind the “shadow under this red rock” of line 25; in Isaiah the shadow is promised together with “rivers of water in a dry place,” and the red rock of the poem gives shade but no water. In chapter 38 King Hezekiah, told to set his house in order because he will die, turns his face to the wall and weeps, and is granted fifteen more years. At line 426 the speaker, fishing on the shore, asks “Shall I at least set my lands in order?” and gets no answer; Eliot’s note on the line before cites Weston’s chapter on the Fisher King.

### source tennyson
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: Maud, and The Princess
AUTHOR: Alfred Tennyson
DATE: 1855; 1847
WHAT: “a handful of dust”; “O swallow swallow.”
LINES: 30, 429
NOTE: Eliot cites neither poem. The mad speaker of <i>Maud</i>, who imagines himself buried under a city street with the traffic going over him, has been cited for “a handful of dust” (line 30), and the passage has been compared with the “rats’ alley” of Part II. The Prince’s song in <i>The Princess</i>, with Swinburne’s “Itylus” (“Swallow, my sister, O sister swallow”), has been cited for “O swallow swallow” at line 429, the English half of a line whose Latin half is from the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i>. In 1936 Eliot wrote that Tennyson “had the finest ear of any English poet since Milton.”

### source whitman-lilacs
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom’d
AUTHOR: Walt Whitman
DATE: 1865
WHAT: Whitman’s elegy for Lincoln: lilacs, spring, and the hermit thrush.
LINES: 2, 357
NOTE: Whitman’s elegy for Lincoln, who was shot in April 1865, brings together the lilac, the evening star and the hermit thrush singing in a swamp. Eliot’s poem opens with lilacs bred out of the dead land (line 2) and at line 357 wants the hermit-thrush’s “water-dripping song”; his note on that line describes the bird and quotes Chapman’s <i>Handbook of Birds of Eastern North America</i>, and says nothing of Whitman. In 1928 Eliot wrote that he “did not read Whitman until much later in life, and had to conquer an aversion to his form, as well as to much of his matter, in order to do so.” The case that the poem drew on Whitman all the same is made at length in S. Musgrove’s <i>T. S. Eliot and Walt Whitman</i> (1952).

### source webster-white-devil
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: The White Devil
AUTHOR: John Webster
DATE: 1612
WHAT: Cornelia’s dirge (“keep the wolf far thence”) and Flamineo on the spider and the winding-sheet.
LINES: 74, 408
NOTE: Webster’s tragedy is about Vittoria Corombona, the “white devil” of the title, and her lover Brachiano, who has her husband and his own wife murdered. Vittoria’s mother Cornelia sings the dirge over her son Marcello, killed by his brother Flamineo; Flamineo makes the speech about widows in the last scene, rising from a feigned death after Vittoria has fired at him what she took to be a loaded pistol. Eliot’s notes cite the dirge at line 74 and Flamineo’s speech at line 408. At line 74 the poem turns Webster’s wolf, “foe to men,” into a Dog, “friend to men.” Eliot’s “Whispers of Immortality” (1918) begins “Webster was much possessed by death / And saw the skull beneath the skin.”

### source shakespeare-tempest
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: The Tempest, Act I scene ii
AUTHOR: William Shakespeare
DATE: c. 1611
WHAT: Ariel’s song (“Those are pearls that were his eyes”) and Ferdinand, weeping on the bank for his father’s wreck, hearing “This music crept by me upon the waters.”
LINES: 48, 125, 191, 257
NOTE: Ferdinand, washed ashore after the shipwreck, believes his father Alonso drowned; the song Ariel sings to him, unseen, describes a drowned man turned to coral and pearl. The song is not true. Alonso is alive, and the play ends with father and son reunited; in the poem the Phoenician Sailor of line 47 and Phlebas in Part IV stay drowned. Eliot’s notes cite the scene at lines 192 and 257, and his note on the pearls of line 125 refers them back to lines 37 and 48; his note on Tiresias says the Phoenician Sailor “is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples.” Ferdinand’s “the king my father’s wreck” becomes “the king my brother’s wreck” and “the king my father’s death” for the fisherman on the canal bank behind the gashouse (lines 191–192).

### source frazer
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: The Golden Bough (Adonis, Attis, Osiris)
AUTHOR: Sir James George Frazer
DATE: 1906–15 (third edition); one-volume abridgement 1922
WHAT: The god who dies with the harvest and returns in spring: the Hanged God, the buried effigy, the image thrown into the sea.
LINES: 46, 71, 312
NOTE: Frazer’s twelve-volume study of magic and religion, which Eliot’s headnote says “has influenced our generation profoundly,” argued that the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris, and by implication of Christ, tell one story, that of a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by rites in which a king is sacrificed or an effigy is buried or drowned. The two volumes Eliot names describe the “gardens of Adonis,” pots of quick-sprouting seed carried out with images of the dead god and “flung with them into the sea,” and the effigies of Osiris “made of earth and corn” that the priests buried so that grain would sprout from the god’s body. Eliot’s note on the Tarot says the Hanged Man “is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer”; the corpse planted in Stetson’s garden (line 71) and Phlebas in the current (line 312) have been connected with the same rites. Frazer put a reproduction of Turner’s painting <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1834) at the front of his first edition and opened his first page with it, describing it as a view of Lake Nemi; it in fact shows Lake Avernus and the Sibyl of Cumae, the prophetess of the poem’s epigraph, holding the bough that admits Aeneas to the underworld.

### source weston
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: From Ritual to Romance
AUTHOR: Jessie L. Weston
DATE: 1920
WHAT: The book Eliot’s headnote credits with the title and the plan: the Fisher King, the waste land, the Chapel Perilous.
LINES: 0, 46, 386, 424
NOTE: Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance. Following Frazer’s <i>Golden Bough</i>, she argued that the Grail stories preserved the remains of an ancient fertility cult: a king whose wound lays his land waste, a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics, and a rite of initiation descended from the mystery cults of Attis and Mithra. Those cults, she says, were carried across the Roman Empire by Syrian merchants, slaves and soldiers (chapter XII). Medievalists have not accepted the argument. Eliot’s headnote says the book suggested “not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem,” and recommends it “to any who think such elucidation of the poem worth the trouble”; in a lecture of 1956 he said he regretted “having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail.” The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel).

### source petronius
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: Satyricon, chapter 48
AUTHOR: Petronius
DATE: 1st century AD
WHAT: The epigraph: the Sibyl who wanted to die.
LINES: 0
NOTE: The speaker is Trimalchio, a freed slave grown enormously rich, boasting to his guests between courses; his dinner party is the longest continuous episode in what survives of the <i>Satyricon</i>. The Sibyl of Cumae, in Ovid’s telling (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV), asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand and forgot to ask for youth to go with them; she foresees that she will shrink until only her voice is left. The typescript of the poem carried a different epigraph, from Conrad’s <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, ending “The horror! the horror!”; Pound doubted that Conrad was weighty enough for the place, and Eliot put Petronius in its stead before publication.

### source dante-purg26
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: Purgatorio, canto XXVI
AUTHOR: Dante Alighieri
DATE: c. 1315
WHAT: The dedication to Pound, “il miglior fabbro,” and line 428, Arnaut stepping back into the refining fire.
LINES: 0, 428
NOTE: On the seventh terrace of Purgatory, where the lustful walk in flame, Dante meets the poet Guido Guinizelli, who points out the troubadour Arnaut Daniel as “miglior fabbro del parlar materno,” the better craftsman of the mother tongue (line 117). Arnaut answers in his own Provençal, the only passage in the <i>Comedy</i> in a vernacular other than Italian, and steps back into the fire. Eliot took the title of his 1920 collection <i>Ara Vos Prec</i> from line 145, and line 428 of the poem from line 148, which his note quotes with the three lines before it. The dedication “For Ezra Pound / il miglior fabbro” was not printed in 1922: Eliot wrote it into Pound’s copy of the Boni & Liveright edition in January 1923, and it first appeared in print in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>. Modern texts read “li affina”; Eliot’s “gli” follows the older text he used.

### source conrad-hod
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: Heart of Darkness
AUTHOR: Joseph Conrad
DATE: 1899
WHAT: The epigraph on the typescript, replaced before publication by the passage from Petronius.
LINES: 0
NOTE: The last words of Kurtz, the trader whom the narrator, Marlow, has gone up the river to find, stood at the head of the typescript of Part I as the poem’s epigraph. The story is told on a yawl at anchor in the Thames estuary at dusk, with London upriver.

### source eliot-frontiers
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: The Frontiers of Criticism (Eliot on his own notes)
AUTHOR: T. S. Eliot
DATE: 1956
WHAT: What Eliot later said about the notes.
LINES: 0
NOTE: A lecture given at the University of Minnesota on 30 April 1956, to an audience of about fourteen thousand, and printed in <i>On Poetry and Poets</i> (1957). Thirty-four years on, Eliot said he had meant only to list his references, “spiking the guns” of critics who had accused his earlier poems of plagiarism. The poem’s first printings, in two magazines, the <i>Criterion</i> and the <i>Dial</i>, had no notes; when Boni & Liveright were to make it a book, it was found too short, and he expanded the notes to fill the pages, with the result he called “bogus scholarship.” He regretted sending readers on “a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail,” and said the notes could not now be removed, since anyone who bought his poems and found them missing would ask for his money back.

## C. Drafts records

The two records about the whole poem, as the other thread now has them:

### draft boston
Decision after round three: the scholar's order taken (Wilson's: Tom's place, the show, Myrtle's, the policeman on the way out, the man he knows, the walk home at sunrise), which also agrees with the gloss on line 1; the reader's semicolons kept, so that the bed, the bath and the breakfast do not run into the walk home.
TITLE: The cancelled opening: a night out in Boston
BODY:
In the typescript, Part I opens with fifty-four lines about a night out in Boston, told by one of a party of young men in loose, chatty verse: drinks at "Tom's place"; a show; a call at Myrtle's, where he is refused but given a bed, a bath and breakfast; a brush with a policeman on the way out, which a man he knows smooths over; and a walk home at sunrise. The lines are struck through in pencil. Valerie Eliot's facsimile (1971) attributes the cancellation to Eliot, and Pound's annotations begin only after the cancelled lines; whether Pound read them is not recorded. "April is the cruellest month" stood at the top of the typescript's second page. Pound wrote little on the rest of Part I: he queried a few words and let the section stand.

### draft title-draft
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: The earlier title
BODY: The typescripts of Parts I and II carry the heading “He Do the Police in Different Voices,” with “Part I” and “Part II” beneath it. The words are Betty Higden’s in Dickens’s <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (Book I, chapter 16), praising Sloppy, the foundling who lives with her and turns her mangle, for the way he reads the police news in the paper aloud, doing the voices: “You mightn’t think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices.” The title page Eliot typed for the finished poem reads “The Waste Land,” and his headnote to the notes says that the title, the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism were suggested by Jessie L. Weston’s book on the Grail legend, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>. Dickens is not mentioned in the notes.

### draft epigraph-draft
The front-matter thread's text, as it now stands. With a proposal from Part I in shared-proposals-part1.md.
TITLE: The epigraph that was replaced
BODY: The title page of the typescript carries, under “The Waste Land,” a passage from Conrad’s <i>Heart of Darkness</i> ending with the dying Kurtz’s words, “The horror! the horror!” Pound wrote, on 24 January 1922, that he doubted Conrad was “weighty enough to stand the citation”; Eliot answered that it was “much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative”; Pound then told him to do as he liked about Conrad. Eliot nevertheless replaced it with the sentence from Petronius’s <i>Satyricon</i> in which Trimalchio, the host of the dinner, says that he saw the Sibyl of Cumae hanging in a jar, and that when the boys asked her what she wanted she answered, “I want to die.” The Sibyl had been granted long life, but not youth.
