# Editor, after round one: batch 6 (the notes read when a card is turned in the tarot tool; proposals for the shared file ui.txt)

Each item: the editor's decision, then the NOTE (and, where it changes, the DECK text) that goes to both sides for round two. Wording follows the reader, substance the scholar. Eliot's notes are named as the source wherever they are the source, which the reader asked for and the scholar's checks allow.

### card-note phoenician-sailor
Decision: rewrite. The fragment opener and "Part IV obliges" go; the three identifications replaced by what Eliot's notes say, with his verbs; "Ferdinand's drowned father" corrected (the note ties the Sailor to Ferdinand, and the father is not drowned).
NOTE: He is not a card in any Tarot pack. Eliot's note on line 46 says he appears later: he is Phlebas the Phoenician of Part IV, and the note on line 218 says that the one-eyed merchant "melts into" him and that he is "not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples" in <i>The Tempest</i>. "Those are pearls that were his eyes" is from Ariel's song to Ferdinand about his father, whom Ferdinand believes drowned; the father is alive. Madame Sosostris says "Fear death by water," and Eliot's note adds that "Death by Water is executed in Part IV."
DECK (last sentence only): He is drawn head down on purpose, in the posture of the Hanged Man on a tarot card, which is the card Madame Sosostris cannot find.

### card-note belladonna
Decision: rewrite. The verbless triad made a sentence; the antithesis replaced by two readings marked as readings; "gossip-column English" cut, having no owner.
NOTE: Belladonna means "beautiful lady." It is also deadly nightshade, and the drops made from it that women once used to widen their pupils. "The Lady of the Rocks" is usually traced to Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>, and the card is usually taken to foretell the woman at her dressing-table at the start of Part II. Nobody has settled what "the lady of situations" means.

### card-note three-staves
Decision: trim. The opener made a sentence; the pack named; the fisher at line 424 marked as the usual identification; the DECK's colon-triad folded into the sentence.
NOTE: This is a real card, the Three of Wands. In the Rider pack it shows a figure on a headland, back turned, watching ships go out. Eliot's note says he associated the card, "quite arbitrarily," with the Fisher King; the figure who sits fishing on the shore at line 424 is usually taken to be him.
DECK (second sentence only): His three staves are a crutch for the Fisher King's wound, a fishing rod with its line down, and a staff of office planted in the ground, which has begun to sprout.

### card-note wheel
Decision: rewrite. "Rise, reign, fall" (three of the four stations) replaced; the helmsman and the card given as a reading; the DECK no longer repeats the NOTE.
NOTE: The Wheel of Fortune is card X of the major arcana. In the medieval picture of Fortune's wheel, figures climb it, reign at the top and are thrown down. At line 320, "O you who turn the wheel and look to windward" is addressed to a helmsman at a ship's wheel; readers have taken the wheel there for this card too.
DECK: In this pack: Fortune's wheel drawn as a ship's wheel, because of line 320. The same man in a bowler hat appears four times round it, enthroned at the top, falling on one side, crushed beneath and climbing on the other.

### card-note one-eyed-merchant
Decision: rewrite. The fragment opener goes; "one-eyed because seen in profile" given as the usual guess and left to the DECK, where it is the artist's reason; "his invitation" spelled out; the blank card's business left to the blank card's note.
NOTE: He is not a card. He is Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant of lines 209–214, unshaven, with a pocket full of currants and an invitation to lunch at the Cannon Street Hotel and a weekend at the Metropole. Eliot's note on line 218 says the merchant "melts into" the Phoenician Sailor. The poem does not say why he has one eye; the usual guess is that a card shows its figure in profile.
DECK: In this pack: Mr. Eugenides in profile, the only kind of one-eyed man a printed card can have, in a bowler and an astrakhan collar, holding out a handful of currants; on his back is a roped bundle whose contents are not shown.

### card-note blank
Decision: rewrite. The repeated quotation and "the poem refuses" go; Weston's claim corrected (Syrian merchants, the Mysteries of Attis and Mithra, her thesis) and the application to this merchant given to Brooks; the hover sentence made true for a phone.
NOTE: Madame Sosostris is forbidden to see what the merchant carries on his back, and the poem never says. Weston argued in <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> that the old Mysteries of Attis and Mithra travelled the Roman empire with Syrian merchants, who founded "their chapels at the same time as their counting-houses"; Cleanth Brooks (1939) read this merchant, on her account, as carrying a mystery he knows nothing of.
DECK (last sentence only): Hold the pointer over it, or touch it, and the bundle shows more clearly.

### card-note hanged-man
Decision: trim. The opener made a sentence and the pack named; "whose death brings the rain" corrected to Frazer (the Hanged God renews the year's growth; the rain is the poem's); the closing verdict cut, with the reading given to its owner.
NOTE: He is card XII of the major arcana. In the Rider pack he is a young man hanging by one foot from a living tree, calm-faced and haloed. Eliot's note on line 46 links him to "the Hanged God of Frazer", the god whose death renews the year, and to the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. Madame Sosostris does not find him; since Cleanth Brooks (1939) the missing card has been read as the missing thing in her reading, the death that would be followed by a return.
DECK: unchanged, with "a green shoot on the dead bough" added at the end if the drawing has it (the artist's note says it does).

### card-note crowd-in-a-ring
Decision: rewrite. The colon and the list of four replaced by what Eliot's note says and two comparisons marked as comparisons; "four lines later" corrected to line 62; the maypole kept only as an association with no owner, and the scholar may strike it.
NOTE: This is not a card but something she sees. Eliot's note on line 46 says the "crowds of people" appear later: the crowd flowing over London Bridge at line 62 is the first, and the "hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" at lines 369–70 are usually taken to be another. The ring has been compared to the neutrals in <i>Inferno</i> III, who run for ever after a banner, and, with less behind it, to Frazer's villagers dancing round the maypole.
