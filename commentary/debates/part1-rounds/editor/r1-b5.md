# Editor, after round one: batch 5 (the ten ways in, the pathway stops in Part I, the artist's card notes)

Each item: the editor's decision, then the draft that goes to both sides for round two. Wording follows the reader where the two sides differed on wording; substance follows the scholar. The ways and the stops are edits to Part I's own files; the card notes are proposals for the shared file and are given here as the wording the editor will propose. Items where both sides said "stands" or "keep" and neither changed a word are listed as unchanged.

## A. The ways in

### way-1
Decision: trim. "The first section is where the chant is plainest" was the one claim in it, and it is an impression; the rest is what the tool offers. Woolf's ampersand stays, since it is her diary's.
TITLE: Hear it before you read it
BODY: Virginia Woolf, who heard Eliot read the poem at dinner in June 1922, wrote in her diary that "he sang it & chanted it rhythmed it." Start with his own voice: the listening room has his recordings. Then read the seventy-six lines silently and notice where his stresses fell.

### way-2
Decision: correct. The list named nine voices and hedged with "at least"; the Voices lens gives Part I ten, and the shepherd was missing. The typescript heading placed over this section, as the scholar said, with its source.
TITLE: Count the speakers
BODY: Switch on Voices and the section splits into ten: the poem's own voice, Marie, a stranger in a café, a prophet, Wagner's sailor and his shepherd, a girl with hyacinths, someone who could not speak, a clairvoyante, a commuter. The typescript of this section was headed "He Do the Police in Different Voices", a phrase from Dickens. For each voice, ask whether it is quoting someone else.

### way-3
Decision: correct. Six cards, not seven (the scholar's count, and the site's own drawing agrees); the riddle at the end replaced by what she actually says about water, as the reader asked.
TITLE: Lay out the cards
BODY: Madame Sosostris turns up six cards, fails to find a seventh, and reports one vision. Turn them over one at a time on the table and read the note on each. Then ask what she leaves out. She cannot find the Hanged Man, and the only thing she says about water is "Fear death by water."

### way-4
Decision: rewrite. The claim that this was Eliot's own walk is undocumented (he lived in Marylebone); the route is the clerks' way in from London Bridge station, and what Eliot's note vouches for is the church clock. The distance corrected to about half a mile for the route as listed. Sentence form throughout; "with the walk in mind" for the part the reader was cast in.
TITLE: Walk the commute
BODY: Lines 60–68 follow a route: over London Bridge, up King William Street, past St Mary Woolnoth as its clock strikes nine. It is about half a mile, the way into the City that thousands of clerks walked every morning from London Bridge station, and Eliot's bank stood at the end of it; his note on line 68 calls the dead sound on the final stroke "a phenomenon which I have often noticed". Follow it on the map, then read the lines again with the walk in mind.

### way-5
Decision: trim. The date dropped, since 1387 is conventional for the Prologue and the comparison does not need it.
TITLE: Put Chaucer beside it
BODY: Read the first twelve lines of the General Prologue, then Eliot's first seven. Both begin with April, with rain, and with roots. Decide what has changed between Chaucer's April and Eliot's.

### way-6
Decision: rewrite. Ethan's own example, in five words, goes; the lens described as it works (it tints the lines that name water or its absence, not every line); the run of fragments made a sentence, its contents kept.
TITLE: Follow the water
BODY: Part I names water, or the lack of it, again and again: spring rain and snow in the first sentence, a shower over the Starnbergersee, a dry stone with no sound of water, a handful of dust, a drowned sailor, and then "Fear death by water." The Water lens tints the lines that name water and the lines that name its absence, so you can see where the section is wet and where it is stone.

### way-7
Decision: rewrite, as the reader drafted in round one: the run of fragments and "the section's underworld" go, and the sources are named in a sentence. The scholar found nothing wrong on substance ("at the gate of Hell" is the usual loose phrase for the vestibule).
TITLE: Meet the dead
BODY: The Dead pathway goes through Part I with the sources open beside the lines: Dante's crowd at the gate of Hell and the sighs in Limbo, Webster's dirge, the corpse in Stetson's garden, and the buried god of Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i>.

### way-8
Decision: trim. "Starting exhausted" goes, on both verdicts; the facts stay.
TITLE: Read what was cut
BODY: The section did not begin with April. In the typescript it began with fifty-four lines about a night out in Boston, which were cut. The drafts view shows where the cut fell. Read the cancelled opening and then line 1, and decide what the change did.

### way-9
Decision: rewrite, as the reader drafted in round one: the title "Leave the German alone" goes (the lines are not all German), the lines are numbered, and "as sound, and as a wall" goes; "no German or French" already covers the scholar's point about line 76.
TITLE: Read it without the translations
BODY: Five lines of Wagner (31–34 and 42), a sentence of German at line 12 and a line of French at line 76 are left untranslated. Switch on Tongues to see what they say, then switch it off and read the section as a reader with no German or French would have read it in 1922, hearing those lines without understanding them.

### way-10
Decision: rewrite. The questionnaire goes; the three questions are now the poem's own (19–20, 34, 72), which answers the scholar's objection that the section asked only one of the original three; the bow goes.
TITLE: Three questions with no answer
BODY: Part I asks questions it does not answer: what roots clutch and what branches grow out of this stony rubbish (19–20); where are you lingering (34, in Wagner's German); has the corpse in the garden begun to sprout (72). One way in is to read the section with those questions open.

## B. The pathway stops

### first@1
Decision: trim. The line about the whole poem goes; the count of five stays, being the point.
Start with the sound. Read the first seven lines aloud. Five of the seven end on a participle left hanging at the line break: breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding.

### first@19
Decision: trim, as the reader drafted.
The speaker changes here. Marie's talk stops and a prophet's voice begins, and nothing marks the change but the tone. The poem does this throughout: new voices begin without being introduced.

### first@35
Decision: rewrite. "Two lines of Wagner" corrected to the two passages; "the one moment of feeling" and "Hold on to" go.
Between the two passages from <i>Tristan</i> (31–34 and 42), someone remembers coming back late from a garden with a companion and being unable to speak or see. Who the two are is not said.

### first@43
Decision: trim. "Everything she deals comes true" cut to what Eliot's note vouches for; the card she cannot find named.
Madame Sosostris is a comic fortune-teller with a cold, but some of the cards she deals come back later in the poem, and Eliot's note on line 46 says which. The card she cannot find is the Hanged Man.

### first@60
Decision: rewrite, as the reader drafted; the Baudelaire point rests on Eliot's note on line 60, which the scholar confirmed is on the same screen.
The place is real: London Bridge, King William Street, St Mary Woolnoth and its clock. The crowd crossing the bridge is also Dante's crowd of the dead; Eliot's notes on lines 63 and 64 give the passages. His note on line 60 quotes Baudelaire, whose Paris is a city where a ghost stops a passer-by in daylight.

### water@4
Decision: trim, as the reader drafted.
"Dull roots with spring rain." The first water in the poem is spring rain reaching the roots, in a sentence that calls the month that brings it the cruellest.

### water@24
Decision: trim, as the reader drafted.
"the dry stone no sound of water." The first drought. The sound of water that is missing here is wished for again, at length, in Part V.

### water@42
Unchanged.
"Oed' und leer das Meer." Desolate and empty the sea: Wagner's shepherd sees no ship.

### water@47
Decision: trim, as the reader drafted.
The drowned Phoenician Sailor. The first drowned man in the poem is a card in a pack. The line in brackets, "Those are pearls that were his eyes," is from Ariel's song in <i>The Tempest</i>.

### water@55
Decision: rewrite. "The reading's one instruction" goes (there is also "Look!"); the Grail point given as Weston's waste land and the inversion given to Brooks, as the scholar asked.
"Fear death by water." Madame Sosostris warns against water. In the Grail legend that Eliot's headnote points to, water is what the waste land lacks and needs, and Cleanth Brooks (1939) read her warning as getting the rite backwards. Part IV of the poem is called Death by Water.

### dead@0
Unchanged.
The Sibyl, who cannot die, in her jar.

### dead@2
Decision: correct. The wrong count ("the first thing named after April") replaced by what is true.
"Lilacs out of the dead land." The first thing the poem calls dead is the land itself, in its second line.

### dead@23
Decision: rewrite. Eliot's notes cite Ezekiel 2:1 and Ecclesiastes 12:5; the valley of dry bones is not his citation and goes.
"the dead tree gives no shelter." Eliot's notes send these lines to the Bible: "Son of man" (20) to Ezekiel 2:1, where God so addresses the prophet, and this line to Ecclesiastes 12:5, where the grasshopper is a burden and "man goeth to his long home," the grave.

### dead@39
Unchanged.

### dead@62
Unchanged.
Dante's crowd on London Bridge: "I had not thought death had undone so many."

### dead@71
Decision: trim. "In a suburb" and "back garden" both go, since the poem gives the garden no address.
"That corpse you planted last year in your garden." The buried god of Frazer's vegetation rites, put in the ground so that he will come up again, here a corpse in a garden.

### dead@74
Decision: correct. The second change, foe to friend, added.
Webster's dirge for unburied men, with the wolf turned into a dog and the foe into a friend.

### women@0

### women@13

Marie, who was frightened on the sled as a child, and who says that in the mountains you feel free.

### women@35

### women@43
Decision: rewrite. The triad goes; "afraid of the police" and "right about everything" replaced by the usual reading, marked as one, and by what the note says.
Madame Sosostris, who has a cold, deals cards that come back later in the poem. "One must be so careful these days" is usually read as a fortune-teller's caution about the law, since telling fortunes for money could be prosecuted.

### women@49
Decision: trim, as the reader drafted.
Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks. Belladonna is deadly nightshade, a poison and a cosmetic, and the word means beautiful lady; her title recalls Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>.

### eliot@8
Decision: correct. The summer for August; the king named.
Munich, summer 1911: the Hofgarten and its colonnade, and the Starnbergersee, where Ludwig II of Bavaria was found drowned in 1886.

### eliot@35
Decision: rewrite. The two identifications given their owners: Gordon for Hale, the 1934 <i>Criterion</i> for Verdenal.
The hyacinth garden. Two people from Eliot's life have been proposed for it: Emily Hale, in Lyndall Gordon's reading of the letters Eliot wrote her, and Jean Verdenal, whom Eliot remembered in 1934 crossing the Luxembourg Gardens "waving a branch of lilac". The poem names neither.

### eliot@66
Decision: rewrite. The scene at nine replaced by Eliot's note; the bank placed as the sources allow (the head office at 71 Lombard Street, a few doors from the church; the exact office he sat in is not certain).
Lloyds Bank's head office, at 71 Lombard Street, a few doors from St Mary Woolnoth. Eliot's note on line 68 says the dead sound on the final stroke of nine was "a phenomenon which I have often noticed."

### eliot@0
Decision: rewrite. The three fragments made sentences; "Pound's flat" dropped as undocumented; the dedication dated to 1923 and 1925, not folded into January 1922.
Paris, January 1922. On his way back from Lausanne Eliot stopped in Paris, and Pound went through the typescript with him, cutting it hard. The dedication came later: Eliot wrote "il miglior fabbro", the better craftsman, Dante's phrase for the poet Arnaut Daniel, in Pound's copy in 1923, and printed it with the poem from 1925.

## C. The artist's card notes (proposals for the shared file)

### card sibyl
Decision: correct. The bargain is Ovid's, not Petronius's; and Ovid's word is dust, not sand (the batch 1 scholar's finding, which the epigraph note now carries).
ANSWERS: The epigraph, from Petronius: the Sibyl of Cumae hangs in her jar while the boys ask "Sibyl, what do you want?" and she answers "I want to die." The bargain behind her state is Ovid's: as many years as the grains of dust in her hand, and no youth to live them in.
