## Library notes, Tennyson to Eliot's 1956 lecture (site/sources.txt)

### source tennyson
TITLE: Maud, and The Princess
AUTHOR: Alfred Tennyson
DATE: 1855; 1847
WHAT: “a handful of dust”; “O swallow swallow.”
LINES: 30, 429
NOTE: Eliot cites neither poem. The mad speaker of <i>Maud</i>, who imagines himself buried under a city street with the traffic going over him, has been cited for “a handful of dust” (line 30), and the passage has been compared with the “rats’ alley” of Part II. The Prince’s song in <i>The Princess</i>, with Swinburne’s “Itylus” (“Swallow, my sister, O sister swallow”), has been cited for “O swallow swallow” at line 429, the English half of a line whose Latin half is from the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i>. In 1936 Eliot wrote that Tennyson “had the finest ear of any English poet since Milton.”

### source whitman-lilacs
TITLE: When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom’d
AUTHOR: Walt Whitman
DATE: 1865
WHAT: Whitman’s elegy for Lincoln: lilacs, spring, and the hermit thrush.
LINES: 2, 357
NOTE: Whitman’s elegy for Lincoln, who was shot in April 1865, brings together the lilac, the evening star and the hermit thrush singing in a swamp. Eliot’s poem opens with lilacs bred out of the dead land (line 2) and at line 357 wants the hermit-thrush’s “water-dripping song”; his note on that line describes the bird and quotes Chapman’s <i>Handbook of Birds of Eastern North America</i>, and says nothing of Whitman. In 1928 Eliot wrote that he “did not read Whitman until much later in life, and had to conquer an aversion to his form, as well as to much of his matter, in order to do so.” The case that the poem drew on Whitman all the same is made at length in S. Musgrove’s <i>T. S. Eliot and Walt Whitman</i> (1952).

### source webster-white-devil
TITLE: The White Devil
AUTHOR: John Webster
DATE: 1612
WHAT: Cornelia’s dirge (“keep the wolf far thence”) and Flamineo on the spider and the winding-sheet.
LINES: 74, 408
NOTE: Webster’s tragedy is about Vittoria Corombona, the “white devil” of the title, and her lover Brachiano, who has her husband and his own wife murdered. Vittoria’s mother Cornelia sings the dirge over her son Marcello, killed by his brother Flamineo; Flamineo makes the speech about widows in the last scene, rising from a feigned death after Vittoria has fired at him what she took to be a loaded pistol. Eliot’s notes cite the dirge at line 74 and Flamineo’s speech at line 408. At line 74 the poem turns Webster’s wolf, “foe to men,” into a Dog, “friend to men.” Eliot’s “Whispers of Immortality” (1918) begins “Webster was much possessed by death / And saw the skull beneath the skin.”

### source shakespeare-tempest
TITLE: The Tempest, Act I scene ii
AUTHOR: William Shakespeare
DATE: c. 1611
WHAT: Ariel’s song (“Those are pearls that were his eyes”) and Ferdinand, weeping on the bank for his father’s wreck, hearing “This music crept by me upon the waters.”
LINES: 48, 125, 191, 257
NOTE: Ferdinand, washed ashore after the shipwreck, believes his father Alonso drowned; the song Ariel sings to him, unseen, describes a drowned man turned to coral and pearl. The song is not true. Alonso is alive, and the play ends with father and son reunited; in the poem the Phoenician Sailor of line 47 and Phlebas in Part IV stay drowned. Eliot’s notes cite the scene at lines 192 and 257, and his note on the pearls of line 125 refers them back to lines 37 and 48; his note on Tiresias says the Phoenician Sailor “is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples.” Ferdinand’s “the king my father’s wreck” becomes “the king my brother’s wreck” and “the king my father’s death” for the fisherman on the canal bank behind the gashouse (lines 191–192).

### source frazer
TITLE: The Golden Bough (Adonis, Attis, Osiris)
AUTHOR: Sir James George Frazer
DATE: 1906–15 (third edition); one-volume abridgement 1922
WHAT: The god who dies with the harvest and returns in spring: the Hanged God, the buried effigy, the image thrown into the sea.
LINES: 46, 71, 312
NOTE: Frazer’s twelve-volume study of magic and religion, which Eliot’s headnote says “has influenced our generation profoundly,” argued that the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris, and by implication of Christ, tell one story, that of a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by rites in which a king is sacrificed or an effigy is buried or drowned. The two volumes Eliot names describe the “gardens of Adonis,” pots of quick-sprouting seed carried out with images of the dead god and “flung with them into the sea,” and the effigies of Osiris “made of earth and corn” that the priests buried so that grain would sprout from the god’s body. Eliot’s note on the Tarot says the Hanged Man “is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer”; the corpse planted in Stetson’s garden (line 71) and Phlebas in the current (line 312) have been connected with the same rites. Frazer put a reproduction of Turner’s painting <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1834) at the front of his first edition and opened his first page with it, describing it as a view of Lake Nemi; it in fact shows Lake Avernus and the Sibyl of Cumae, the prophetess of the poem’s epigraph, holding the bough that admits Aeneas to the underworld.

### source weston
TITLE: From Ritual to Romance
AUTHOR: Jessie L. Weston
DATE: 1920
WHAT: The book Eliot’s headnote credits with the title and the plan: the Fisher King, the waste land, the Chapel Perilous.
LINES: 0, 46, 386, 424
NOTE: Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance. Following Frazer’s <i>Golden Bough</i>, she argued that the Grail stories preserved the remains of an ancient fertility cult: a king whose wound lays his land waste, a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics, and a rite of initiation descended from the mystery cults of Attis and Mithra. Those cults, she says, were carried across the Roman Empire by Syrian merchants, slaves and soldiers (chapter XII). Medievalists have not accepted the argument. Eliot’s headnote says the book suggested “not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem,” and recommends it “to any who think such elucidation of the poem worth the trouble”; in a lecture of 1956 he said he regretted “having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail.” The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel).

### source petronius
TITLE: Satyricon, chapter 48
AUTHOR: Petronius
DATE: 1st century AD
WHAT: The epigraph: the Sibyl who wanted to die.
LINES: 0
NOTE: The speaker is Trimalchio, a freed slave grown enormously rich, boasting to his guests between courses; his dinner party is the longest continuous episode in what survives of the <i>Satyricon</i>. The Sibyl of Cumae, in Ovid’s telling (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV), asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand and forgot to ask for youth to go with them; she foresees that she will shrink until only her voice is left. The typescript of the poem carried a different epigraph, from Conrad’s <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, ending “The horror! the horror!”; Pound doubted that Conrad was weighty enough for the place, and Eliot put Petronius in its stead before publication.

### source dante-purg26
TITLE: Purgatorio, canto XXVI
AUTHOR: Dante Alighieri
DATE: c. 1315
WHAT: The dedication to Pound, “il miglior fabbro,” and line 428, Arnaut stepping back into the refining fire.
LINES: 0, 428
NOTE: On the seventh terrace of Purgatory, where the lustful walk in flame, Dante meets the poet Guido Guinizelli, who points out the troubadour Arnaut Daniel as “miglior fabbro del parlar materno,” the better craftsman of the mother tongue (line 117). Arnaut answers in his own Provençal, the only passage in the <i>Comedy</i> in a vernacular other than Italian, and steps back into the fire. Eliot took the title of his 1920 collection <i>Ara Vos Prec</i> from line 145, and line 428 of the poem from line 148, which his note quotes with the three lines before it. The dedication “For Ezra Pound / il miglior fabbro” was not printed in 1922: Eliot wrote it into Pound’s copy of the Boni & Liveright edition in January 1923, and it first appeared in print in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>. Modern texts read “li affina”; Eliot’s “gli” follows the older text he used.

### source dickens-omf
TITLE: Our Mutual Friend, Book I chapter 16
AUTHOR: Charles Dickens
DATE: 1865
WHAT: The typescript’s title: “He Do the Police in Different Voices.”
LINES: 0
NOTE: Betty Higden, a poor old woman in the novel, is praising Sloppy, the foundling she has brought up, who reads her the newspaper and does all the voices in the police-court reports. “He Do the Police in Different Voices” is typed as a title at the head of the typescripts of Parts I and II in the drafts; it did not survive to publication. The novel opens on the Thames, with Gaffer Hexam and his daughter in a boat searching the water for drowned bodies.

### source conrad-hod
TITLE: Heart of Darkness
AUTHOR: Joseph Conrad
DATE: 1899
WHAT: The epigraph on the typescript, replaced before publication by the passage from Petronius.
LINES: 0
NOTE: The last words of Kurtz, the trader whom the narrator, Marlow, has gone up the river to find, stood at the head of the typescript of Part I as the poem’s epigraph. Pound wrote that he doubted whether Conrad was “weighty enough to stand the citation.” Eliot answered that it was “much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative,” and Pound withdrew the objection; Eliot nevertheless replaced it with the passage from Petronius that stands there now. “The heart of light” at line 41 has been read as an inversion of Conrad’s title. The story is told on a yawl at anchor in the Thames estuary at dusk, with London upriver.

### source eliot-frontiers
TITLE: The Frontiers of Criticism (Eliot on his own notes)
AUTHOR: T. S. Eliot
DATE: 1956
WHAT: What Eliot later said about the notes.
LINES: 0
NOTE: A lecture given at the University of Minnesota on 30 April 1956, to an audience of about fourteen thousand, and printed in <i>On Poetry and Poets</i> (1957). Thirty-four years on, Eliot said he had meant only to list his references, “spiking the guns” of critics who had accused his earlier poems of plagiarism. The poem’s first printings, in two magazines, the <i>Criterion</i> and the <i>Dial</i>, had no notes; when Boni & Liveright were to make it a book, it was found too short, and he expanded the notes to fill the pages, with the result he called “bogus scholarship.” He regretted sending readers on “a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail,” and said the notes could not now be removed, since anyone who bought his poems and found them missing would ask for his money back.
