# Batch 3, scholar, round 3 (last): the editor's round-two rewrites of the glosses on lines 60–76

### unreal-city
Verdict: satisfied

### london-bridge-dead
Verdict: satisfied

Checked, as asked: Eliot's 1950 sentence about the notes is fairly given. His words ("What Dante Means to Me", 1950) are that he "gave the references in my notes, in order to make the reader who recognized the allusion, know that I meant him to recognize it", and the sentence goes on that such a reader would have missed the point had he not recognised it. The rewrite's "he gave the references in his notes so that a reader who recognised a borrowing would know he was meant to recognise it" keeps the subject (the references, not the notes as a whole) and the purpose; the dropped second clause is not needed here. "Altered the line about the sighs on purpose" is a fair rendering of "deliberately modified a line of Dante by altering it", with that line quoted.

### stetson
Verdict: not yet

1. "The name has also been linked, by Southam, to the felt slouch hat ..." Southam records the suggestion; he does not make it. His note on l. 69 introduces someone else and continues "He points out that felt slouch hats (a version of the stetson) were the characteristic and identifying head-gear of Australian and New Zealand soldiers in the First World War" (<i>Guide</i>, 6th ed.). I could not recover the name from the archive.org search window, so the note should credit the <i>Guide</i> as the place where the suggestion is recorded, not as its author. Put: "Southam's <i>Guide</i> also records a suggestion that links the name to the felt slouch hat, a kind of stetson, worn by the Australian and New Zealand troops of 1914–18; Stetson himself has been linked by Robert Crawford (1987) and Donald Childs (1988) to Kipling's bank clerk Charlie Mears, in "The Finest Story in the World", who remembers rowing in an ancient galley."

### dog
Verdict: satisfied

Checked, as asked: "the modern concern for man that digs up what was buried and so prevents its return" fairly states Brooks. His words (<i>Modern Poetry and the Tradition</i>, 1939; confirmed in the archive.org text of a reprint) are that he takes the Dog, "the capital letter is Eliot's", as "Humanitarianism and the related philosophies which, in their concern for man, extirpate the supernatural—dig up the corpse of the buried god and thus prevent the rebirth of life." The rewrite keeps the concern for man, the digging up, and the rebirth prevented, and the direction is now right (the round-one "left to lie" had reversed it). What it drops is the means, "extirpate the supernatural". If the editor wants that too: "the modern concern for man that, rooting out the supernatural, digs up what was buried and so prevents its return". Not an objection.

### hypocrite-lecteur
Verdict: not yet

1. "Eliot said in 1950 that his notes were there so that a reader who recognised an allusion would know it was meant." That widens the 1950 sentence into an account of what the notes as a whole were for. Eliot's sentence is about the references he gave in them ("I gave the references in my notes, in order to ..."), and in 1956, in "The Frontiers of Criticism", he gave a different account of the notes as a whole (added to fill out the volume; "bogus scholarship"). The line-63 note has the subject right; this one should too, in its own words. Put: "Eliot said in 1950 that he gave the references in his notes so that a reader who recognised an allusion would know it was meant."
