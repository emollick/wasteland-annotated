# Batch 6, reader, round 1: the notes read when a card is turned (Madame Sosostris's reading, lines 43–59)

A note on the whole set before the items. Every NOTE is two or three sentences, and the tells sit in the same two places each time: a verbless opener ("Not in any Tarot pack." "Not a card." "Card XII:" "A real card, the Three of Wands:" "Not a card but what she sees:") and a closing phrase made to be quoted ("Part IV obliges." "a helmsman and this card at once." "whatever this is, it is not shown." "No sacrifice, no resurrection, in this deal."). Turning the cards one after another, I hear the pattern by the third card. The DECK texts, which describe the drawings, are mostly fine; where they go wrong it is by repeating the NOTE or by stating the artist's idea as the poem's. Where NOTE and DECK are read together, a point needs saying once.

### card-note phoenician-sailor

Verdict: rewrite

Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "Not in any Tarot pack." — the fragment opener. One word makes it a sentence.
2. "He is Phlebas of Part IV, the merchant of Part III, and Ferdinand's drowned father in The Tempest, whose eyes are pearls." — three identifications given flat, with no source, when the source is Eliot's own notes (on 46: the Sailor "appears later"; on 218: the merchant "melts into the Phoenician Sailor, and the latter is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples"). Two things for the scholar: Eliot's note makes him Ferdinand, not Ferdinand's father; and in the play the father is not drowned, Ferdinand only believes he is, which is what Ariel's song tells him. A reader who knows the play stops here.
3. ""Fear death by water," says the clairvoyante; Part IV obliges." — "Part IV obliges" is the bow: the poem as an obliging servant, a joke at the end of the note.
4. DECK: "he is the Hanged Man the reading cannot find, gone into the water instead." — the artist's idea stated as if it were the poem's. Same ruling as in batch 5.

Keep: the DECK's first sentence.

Draft:
NOTE: He is not a card in any Tarot pack. Eliot's note on line 46 says he appears later: he is Phlebas the Phoenician of Part IV, and Eliot's note on line 218 says that the one-eyed merchant "melts into" him and that he is "not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples" in <i>The Tempest</i>. "Those are pearls that were his eyes" is from Ariel's song to Ferdinand about his father, whom Ferdinand believes drowned. Madame Sosostris says "Fear death by water," and Part IV is called Death by Water.
DECK (last sentence only): He is drawn head down on purpose, in the posture of the Hanged Man on a tarot card, which is the card Madame Sosostris cannot find.

### card-note belladonna

Verdict: rewrite

Reads as a model wrote it: yes

Problems:
1. ""Beautiful lady," the deadly nightshade, and the drops women used to widen their pupils." — three meanings with no verb. The facts are the right ones and want a sentence.
2. "Leonardo's Virgin of the Rocks is behind the title; the woman in the chair of Part II is in front of it." — an antithesis built for the semicolon. I had to work out that "behind" means source and "in front of" means what the card foretells. Both are readings and should be said as readings.
3. ""The lady of situations" is gossip-column English." — a quip given as a fact. If it is a known reading, say whose; if it is the site's, mark it as one.

Keep: DECK as it stands. It describes the drawing and stops.

Draft:
NOTE: Belladonna means "beautiful lady." It is also deadly nightshade, and the drops made from it that women once used to widen their pupils. "The Lady of the Rocks" recalls Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>. The card is usually taken to foretell the woman at her dressing-table at the start of Part II. [Scholar: "gossip-column English" for "The lady of situations" needs an owner or a "one reading is."]

### card-note three-staves

Verdict: trim

Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "A real card, the Three of Wands: a figure on a headland, back turned, watching ships go out." — the opener as a fragment with a colon. Also, the figure on the headland is the Rider-Waite design; older packs show three batons and no figure. Say which pack, as the batch-5 card text does. Scholar to confirm.
2. "the Fisher King, who sits on the shore at line 424" — that the fisher at 424 is the Fisher King is a reading, if the usual one. "Is usually taken to be" costs four words.
3. DECK: "a wound, a line, a rod of office." — the colon and the three, as a tag. Same ruling as in batch 5: put it into the sentence.

Keep: "Eliot associated him, "quite arbitrarily," with the Fisher King" — his own words, sourced. The DECK's first sentence.

Draft:
NOTE: This is a real card, the Three of Wands. In the Rider-Waite pack it shows a figure on a headland, back turned, watching ships go out. Eliot's note says he associated the card, "quite arbitrarily," with the Fisher King; the figure who sits fishing on the shore at line 424 is usually taken to be him.
DECK (second sentence only): His three staves are a crutch for the Fisher King's wound, a fishing rod with its line down, and a staff of office planted in the ground, which has begun to sprout.

### card-note wheel

Verdict: rewrite

Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "card X of the major arcana: rise, reign, fall." — the colon and the three, and the three is short by one: the medieval wheel has four stations, which the DECK text and the batch-5 card text both give.
2. ""O you who turn the wheel and look to windward," line 320, is a helmsman and this card at once." — "at once" is the pairing from the brief's list. The wheel at 320 is a ship's wheel; that it is also this card is a reading, and a good one, but it should be said as one.
3. DECK: "It looks ahead to "you who turn the wheel and look to windward"." — repeats the NOTE directly above it, and gives the drawing agency. Say instead why it is drawn as a ship's wheel.

Keep: "the same man in a bowler at its four stations, enthroned, falling, crushed, climbing" — a list of what is drawn, which is what a caption is for.

Draft:
NOTE: The Wheel of Fortune is card X of the major arcana. In the medieval picture of Fortune's wheel, figures climb it, reign at the top and are thrown down. At line 320, "O you who turn the wheel and look to windward" is addressed to a helmsman at a ship's wheel; readers have taken the wheel there for this card too.
DECK: In this pack: Fortune's wheel drawn as a ship's wheel, because of line 320. The same man in a bowler hat appears four times round it, enthroned at the top, falling on one side, crushed beneath and climbing on the other.

### card-note one-eyed-merchant

Verdict: rewrite

Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "Not a card." — the fragment opener.
2. "One-eyed because seen in profile" — a reading given as a fact, and it is the artist's reason for the drawing, which the DECK gives again in the next breath. Once is enough, and the DECK is where it belongs.
3. "with his currants and his invitation" — a pairing for rhythm; "his invitation" is coy. The poem gives lunch at the Cannon Street Hotel and a weekend at the Metropole.
4. "Something on his back that the clairvoyante is forbidden to see." — a fragment that paraphrases the lines on the card, and it is the blank card's note, not this one's.
5. DECK: two colons in one sentence.

Keep: the DECK's content, including the profile joke, which is the artist explaining a choice.

Draft:
NOTE: He is not a card. He is Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant of lines 209–214, unshaven, with a pocket full of currants and an invitation to lunch at the Cannon Street Hotel and a weekend at the Metropole. Eliot's note on line 218 says the merchant "melts into" the Phoenician Sailor. The poem does not say why he has one eye.
DECK: In this pack: Mr. Eugenides in profile, the only kind of one-eyed man a printed card can have, in a bowler and an astrakhan collar, holding out a handful of currants; on his back is a roped bundle whose contents are not shown.

### card-note blank

Verdict: rewrite

Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. The opening quotation repeats the lines on the card and in the poem beside it. It can go.
2. "The one card the poem refuses to read." — a fragment, and the poem as a person who refuses, which the brief names. It is also not what happens: Madame Sosostris is forbidden to see it, and the poem says nothing.
3. "Weston's merchants carried the Grail mysteries along the trade routes; whatever this is, it is not shown." — the Weston fact is the useful thing here, but the connection to the bundle is left for me to make, and "whatever this is, it is not shown" is a shrug for an ending that repeats "forbidden to see."
4. DECK: "Hold the pointer over it." — right to tell me, but say what happens, and remember a phone has no pointer.

Keep: DECK, first three sentences. "Something is there and cannot be read" is the sentence for this card.

Draft:
NOTE: Madame Sosostris is forbidden to see what the merchant carries on his back, and the poem never says. In Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> the Grail mysteries travelled with merchants along the trade routes, and one reading is that the bundle holds them.
DECK (last sentence only): Hold the pointer over it, or touch it, and the bundle shows more clearly. [Editor: if that is what the hover does.]

### card-note hanged-man

Verdict: trim

Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "Card XII: a young man hanging by one foot from a living tree, serene, haloed." — the colon fragment with a trailing pair. As with the Three of Wands, the living tree and the halo are the Rider-Waite design, and the note should say so. Scholar to confirm.
2. "the sacrificed king whose death brings the rain" — for the scholar: is the rain Frazer's or the poem's? Frazer's hanged god dies so that the land and crops are renewed; the rain is what the waste land wants. I do not change it, but a reader should not be handed the poem's need as Frazer's fact.
3. "No sacrifice, no resurrection, in this deal." — the bow: a fragment, a pair, and a reading of the missing card given as a fact. Cut it. If the point is wanted, mark it as a reading.
4. DECK: the batch-5 card text has a green shoot on the dead bough, and this one does not. If the drawing has the shoot it is the point of the dead tree, and worth the four words.

Keep: "Eliot linked him to Frazer's Hanged God ... and to the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus." "Madame Sosostris does not find him." The DECK.

Draft:
NOTE: He is card XII of the major arcana. In the Rider-Waite pack he is a young man hanging by one foot from a living tree, calm-faced and haloed. Eliot's note on line 46 links him to the Hanged God of Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i>, the king put to death so that the land will be renewed [scholar to settle "brings the rain"], and to the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. Madame Sosostris does not find him. [Optional, as a reading: One reading is that the sacrifice that would renew the land is missing from her cards.]

### card-note crowd-in-a-ring

Verdict: rewrite

Reads as a model wrote it: in places

Problems:
1. "Not a card but what she sees: Dante's neutrals running after their banner, Frazer's villagers round the maypole, the commuters on London Bridge four lines later, the hooded hordes of Part V." — the whole note is a colon and a list of four, with no verb and no sources, and it mixes what Eliot's note says (the "crowds of people" appear later) with two comparisons that are somebody's readings. Say which is which.
2. "four lines later" — the crowd on London Bridge is at line 62; "I see crowds of people" is line 56. Six lines. A count that exists to be neat and is wrong against the text.

Keep: DECK as it stands. "The centre of the ring is empty" is a description and it is the drawing's point.

Draft:
NOTE: This is not a card but something she sees. Eliot's note on line 46 says the "crowds of people" appear later: the crowd flowing over London Bridge at line 62 is the first, and the "hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" of Part V are usually taken to be another [scholar to add the site's line numbers]. The ring has been compared to the neutrals in <i>Inferno</i> III, who run after a banner, and to Frazer's villagers dancing round the maypole.

### summary

What I threw out most often was the shape shared by all eight notes: a verbless opener ("Not a card." "Card XII:" "A real card, the Three of Wands:") and a closing phrase made to be quoted ("Part IV obliges." "a helmsman and this card at once." "whatever this is, it is not shown." "No sacrifice, no resurrection, in this deal."). Between them, the colon-and-three ("rise, reign, fall"; "a wound, a line, a rod of office"), one antithesis ("behind the title ... in front of it"), and readings given as facts with no owner ("One-eyed because seen in profile," "gossip-column English," "the woman in the chair of Part II," the Dante and Frazer comparisons for the ring). One count is wrong against the text ("four lines later": it is six). Where Eliot's own notes are the source, which is most of the time here, the notes do not say so, and saying so is the cheapest fix there is.

The DECK texts are mostly right and should be left alone: they describe a drawing and stop. Two faults: repeating the NOTE just above (the wheel and line 320; the profile joke), and stating the artist's idea as the poem's ("he is the Hanged Man the reading cannot find").

For the scholar, not changed by me: Ferdinand's father is not drowned in the play, and Eliot's note on 218 links the Sailor to Ferdinand, not to the father; the Three of Wands and Hanged Man descriptions are the Rider-Waite designs and should say so; "whose death brings the rain" for Frazer's Hanged God; "Weston's merchants" and the Grail mysteries; "gossip-column English"; the fisher at line 424 as the Fisher King; the site's line numbers for the hooded hordes.
