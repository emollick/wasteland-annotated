# Batch 3, scholar, round 1: glosses on lines 60–76

### unreal-city
Verdict: correct

Checked: Baudelaire, "Les Sept Vieillards", 1861 text (fr.wikisource, Les_Fleurs_du_mal/1861/Les_Sept_Vieillards): section <i>Tableaux parisiens</i>; opening couplet as quoted, ending "!"; "Un brouillard sale et jaune inondait tout l'espace"; "je comptai sept fois ... ce sinistre vieillard qui se multipliait". Eliot's 1950 talk "What Dante Means to Me" (Italian Institute, London, 4 July 1950; collected in <i>To Criticize the Critic</i>, 1965), wording confirmed in secondary quotation (tseliot.com; wordandsilence.com). Site HTML fetched directly: "Unreal City" is anchored L207, "Unreal" L377. Lloyds dates from the Eliot Estate's site (tseliot.com/foundation/t-s-eliot-at-lloyds-bank-2): joined 19 March 1917, Colonial & Foreign Department, left November 1925; head office 71 Lombard Street (Wikipedia, Lombard Street, London).

Substance:
1. "Eliot said in 1950 that Baudelaire had taught him 'the poetical possibilities . . . of the more sordid aspects of the modern metropolis, of the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric.'" The words are Eliot's, but the note does not say where they come from, and at some 25 words the quotation is longer than the rights rule allows for a post-1929 text. Name the source (the talk "What Dante Means to Me", 1950) and cut the quotation to a phrase: "the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric".
2. "That is the recipe for the next sixteen lines: a real bridge, a real street, a real church clock, and the dead." The count is right (61–76), but the sentence turns a remark about Baudelaire's method into a claim about how Eliot built the passage, which the talk does not say. It is a reading; either mark it as one or cut it. The next note (london-bridge-dead) already makes the point about the named London route.
3. "The fog is coal smoke." True in substance (the London winter fog of the period was a smoke fog) but stated as if the poem said so. Harmless; "The brown fog is London's coal-smoke fog" would do.
4. "Eliot went into the City through it every working morning from 1917, to Lloyds Bank in Lombard Street." He joined Lloyds on 19 March 1917; the head office was 71 Lombard Street, on the block running back to Cornhill (his 1917 bank letterhead gives 17 Cornhill, the other side of the same block, but I saw that only in secondary reproduction, so do not add it). "Every working morning" overstates: he was away from the bank for three months in late 1921, the leave on which the poem was finished, and on other sick leave. "From 1917" without "every" is enough.
5. "'Unreal City' returns at line 207 (a winter noon) and, stripped to one word, at line 377." Right in the site's count (checked against the page anchors). Eliot's marginal number for the last is 376; the site runs one ahead there, as the brief says.
6. Quote and translation: the quote matches Baudelaire (the "!" is his; Eliot's note prints a full stop). "Accosts" for "raccroche" is right; the verb is the one used of street-walkers soliciting.

Draft:
TITLE: Baudelaire's swarming city
BODY: The opening of "Les Sept Vieillards" ("The Seven Old Men"), in the <i>Tableaux parisiens</i> section of <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i>: the poet, walking through a dirty yellow fog, meets the same hideous old man seven times over. In a talk on Dante in 1950 ("What Dante Means to Me") Eliot said that Baudelaire had shown him what poetry could make of the modern city, the fusion of "the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric".

The brown fog is London's coal-smoke fog. Eliot went in to the City through it from March 1917, when he joined Lloyds Bank at 71 Lombard Street. "Unreal City" returns at line 207, under the same fog at noon, and as the single word "Unreal" at line 377.

### london-bridge-dead
Verdict: correct

Checked: <i>Inferno</i> III, 31–42 and 55–57, and IV, 25–42, Petrocchi text (it.wikisource, Divina_Commedia/Inferno/Canto_III and Canto_IV). Eliot's 1950 talk (as above) for "my city clerks trooping over London Bridge from the railway station to their offices", the "deliberately modified" line, and his reason for giving references in the notes. The older reading "ch'io non avrei mai creduto" is in nineteenth-century editions (Lana and Tommaseo page scans on it.wikisource), so Eliot's note is not a misquotation.

Substance:
1. The quote gives the modern critical text ("ch'i' non averei creduto"); Eliot's note beside it has the older reading "ch'io non avrei mai creduto", the text of the Temple Classics edition he used. Both are legitimate. A reader who compares them may take one for a misprint; the editor may prefer to print the text Eliot printed, since the note sits under it. Not an error.
2. "the neutrals, the souls who lived 'without infamy and without praise'": right (III, 36, "che visser sanza 'nfamia e sanza lodo"). Add that they are in the vestibule, refused by Heaven and by Hell alike (III, 40–41); it bears on item 4.
3. "Line 64 ... is from the next canto, the Limbo of the virtuous unbaptised, where 'there was no weeping but only sighs, which made the eternal air tremble' (IV, 25–27)." Right (IV, 26–27). The English is the annotator's own rendering, not a named translator's; the quotation marks make it look like one. Eliot said in 1950 that he had "deliberately modified" this line of Dante; worth a clause.
4. "So the commuters ... are damned twice over, once as people who never chose anything, once as people who were merely born too early." Two misstatements. Neither crowd is damned in Dante's scheme: Hell itself will not receive the neutrals (III, 41), and the souls of Limbo suffer no torment, only hopeless longing ("sanza speme vivemo in disio", IV, 42). And Limbo is not for those "born too early": Virgil says they did not sin but lacked baptism, and that those who lived before Christianity "did not worship God rightly" (IV, 34–38); Dante puts Saladin, Avicenna and Averroes there as well. Give the facts and let the reader draw the parallel, or mark any parallel as a reading.
5. "commuters crossing from London Bridge station into the City between half past eight and nine": the half-hour is an inference from line 68 with no source; drop it. The railway station is Eliot's own gloss: "my city clerks trooping over London Bridge from the railway station to their offices" (1950). Cite it; a phrase of that length is within the rights rule.
6. "'Each man fixed his eyes before his feet' is what a crowd on a bridge in a fog actually looks like." Assertion without source. Cut, or mark as the annotator's observation.
7. Missing: Eliot's stated reason for the notes, in the same talk: he gave the references so that a reader who recognised the allusion would know he was meant to. One clause; it also serves the hypocrite-lecteur note.

Draft:
TITLE: The crowd at the gate of Hell
BODY: <i>Inferno</i> III, 55–57. Just inside the gate of Hell, before the river Acheron, Dante sees the souls of those who lived "without infamy and without praise" (III, 36), refused by Heaven and by Hell alike, and is astonished at how many there are. Line 64, "Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled", comes from the next canto, Limbo, where the unbaptised and the virtuous pagans live "without hope, in desire", and there is no weeping, only sighs that make the eternal air tremble (IV, 25–42). Eliot said in 1950 that he had altered Dante's line on purpose, and that he gave the references in his notes so that a reader who recognised them would know he was meant to. In the same talk he called this crowd "my city clerks trooping over London Bridge from the railway station to their offices". He was one of them: he worked at Lloyds Bank, at the top of King William Street, from 1917 to 1925.

### woolnoth
Verdict: correct

Checked: Wikipedia, St Mary Woolnoth (Hawksmoor; work began 1716, reopened Easter Day 1727; corner of Lombard Street and King William Street; two flat-topped turrets; C&SLR works 1897–1900; bones reburied at the City of London Cemetery, Manor Park; lift and stair shafts beneath the church floor). Wikipedia, Bank and Monument stations (C&SLR station opened 25 February 1900; ticket hall and lift entrance in the crypt; church underpinned). Wikipedia, City of London Cemetery (St Mary Woolnoth crypt transferred 1897–1900 for Bank station). Eliot Estate site: Lloyds 19 March 1917 to November 1925, Colonial & Foreign Department; Henry Eliot's 1921 photograph of the corner, "Lloyds on the left, St Mary Woolnoth on the right" (Houghton Library, MS Am 2560 (263)). Hours: letter to his mother, March 1917, "sitting in an office from 9.15 to 5" (<i>Letters</i> vol. 1; quoted in Lisa Levy, The Rumpus, 31 Jan. 2012). 71 Lombard Street as Lloyds' headquarters: Wikipedia, Lombard Street, London.

Substance:
1. "Nicholas Hawksmoor's St Mary Woolnoth (1716–27)": right.
2. "Lloyds Bank, where Eliot worked in the Colonial and Foreign Department from 1917 to 1925, was across the road." "Across the road" is right: the head office at 71 Lombard Street faced the church across Lombard Street, as Henry Eliot's 1921 photograph of the corner shows. Eliot was at Lloyds from 19 March 1917 to November 1925 and began in the Colonial and Foreign Department; whether he stayed in that department throughout I could not confirm (there is a recent article on his work for the bank's Intelligence Department). Safer: "where Eliot worked from 1917 to 1925".
3. "The church's clock struck the start of his working day." Not quite. His hours, he told his mother in March 1917, were "from 9.15 to 5". Nine o'clock would have found him on his way in. Either drop the sentence or make it exact.
4. "'A phenomenon which I have often noticed,' says Eliot's note ... the poem's driest joke about its own apparatus." The quotation is right. "Driest joke" is a reading (others take the note as plain testimony); mark it or cut it.
5. "the Bank station of the City and South London Railway was built beneath it in 1897–1900": right; the station opened on 25 February 1900.
6. "the bodies in the crypt were cleared out and reburied at Ilford": the reburial was in the City of London Cemetery at Manor Park. The cemetery lay in the old parish of Little Ilford and was commonly given as "Ilford" at the time, so this is not wrong, but Manor Park is what a reader will find. Suggest "in the City of London Cemetery at Manor Park".
7. "the crypt became the station's booking hall": right (a sub-surface ticket hall and lift entrance in the crypt, the church above underpinned on steel girders).
8. "The commuters come up out of it every morning." Misleading beside the poem: the crowd of lines 62–68 comes over the bridge on foot from the railway station, as Eliot said in 1950; nobody in the poem comes up out of the Tube. The station is worth a sentence as a fact about the building, not attached to the poem's crowd.

Draft:
TITLE: The dead sound at nine
BODY: King William Street runs up from London Bridge to the Bank, and at its top, on the corner of Lombard Street, stands Nicholas Hawksmoor's St Mary Woolnoth (1716–27), a squat, heavily rusticated church with two flat-topped turrets. Lloyds Bank, whose head office at 71 Lombard Street faced the church across the street, employed Eliot from March 1917 to November 1925; his hours, he told his mother in 1917, were "from 9.15 to 5".

Eliot's note on the dead sound of the final stroke, "A phenomenon which I have often noticed", vouches for the sound from his own hearing. The crypt has its own history: when the City and South London Railway built Bank station beneath the church in 1897–1900, the bodies in the crypt were reburied in the City of London Cemetery at Manor Park, and the crypt became the station's booking hall, the church above it underpinned on steel girders.

### stetson
Verdict: rewrite

Checked: Wikipedia, Battle of Mylae (260 BC; Gaius Duilius against Hannibal Gisco; Rome's first naval victory; the war was for Sicily). Cleanth Brooks, "The Waste Land: Critique of the Myth" (<i>Modern Poetry and the Tradition</i>, 1939), wording confirmed by archive.org full-text search of the reprints (Jay Martin's collection, 1968; Norton Critical Edition): "Mylae, as Mr. Matthiessen has pointed out, is the name of a battle between the Romans and the Carthaginians in the Punic War. The Punic War was a trade war—might be considered a rather close parallel to our late war. ... all the wars are one war; all experience, one experience." B. C. Southam, <i>A Guide to the Selected Poems of T. S. Eliot</i> (6th ed., 1994), note on l. 69, seen only in archive.org search snippets: Pound's "sombrero-stetson", a denial, John Hayward's note, the Australian felt slouch hat. TLS, 11 May 1973 (a letter in the correspondence on "The Waste Land as a Buddhist Poem"; the scan is restricted, so I have a snippet only and could not see the signature): "'Stetson' is not Ezra Pound. Eliot said he was not referring to anyone in particular, but simply meant any superior bank clerk: a person in a bowler hat, black jacket and striped trousers." Quoted by Donald J. Childs, "Stetson in The Waste Land", <i>Essays in Criticism</i> 38 (1988), 131–48, who links Stetson with Kipling's bank clerk Charlie Mears ("The Finest Story in the World"), as does Robert Crawford, <i>The Savage and the City in the Work of T. S. Eliot</i> (1987). Line 69 in the 1922 text: "There I saw one I knew".

Substance:
1. "Mylae is the sea battle of 260 BC in which Rome first beat Carthage at sea, early in the First Punic War": right.
2. "a trade war between two commercial empires": this is Cleanth Brooks's reading of 1939, following Matthiessen (1935), who first identified Mylae: "The Punic War was a trade war—might be considered a rather close parallel to our late war." The note gives it as history. Historians describe the First Punic War as a war for Sicily, and Rome in 264 BC was no commercial empire. Attribute it: "Cleanth Brooks read the Punic War as a trade war and so a parallel to 1914–18".
3. "The war dead and the office crowd are the same people, and all wars are the one war." Brooks again, near verbatim ("all the wars are one war; all experience, one experience"). Attribute.
4. "Stetson has often been taken for Pound, who wore wide-brimmed hats": fair; Southam's <i>Guide</i> records the identification and Pound's "sombrero-stetson". There is also a report of Eliot's denying it: the TLS correspondent of 1973 (above) has Eliot saying he meant no one in particular, "simply ... any superior bank clerk" in a bowler hat, black jacket and striped trousers. If the note uses this it should say "a correspondent in the TLS in 1973 reported Eliot as saying ..." and no more; I could not read the whole letter.
5. "nothing in the poem needs him to be anyone in particular": fine, and the 1973 report supports it.
6. "The name is a hat, an American hat, on a bridge in London." The name is a hat (John B. Stetson Company, Philadelphia), but the meeting is not on the bridge: "There I saw one I knew" (line 69) is at the top of King William Street, by St Mary Woolnoth, where the crowd has just arrived. "In King William Street" or "in the City".
7. Worth a clause, since a reader will meet them: the felt slouch hat of the Australian troops at Gallipoli (recorded in Southam), and Kipling's bank clerk Charlie Mears, who remembers rowing in an ancient galley (Crawford 1987; Childs 1988). Neither need be endorsed.

Draft:
TITLE: Stetson, and the ships at Mylae
BODY: Mylae was the sea battle of 260 BC in which Rome first beat Carthage at sea, early in the First Punic War. F. O. Matthiessen identified the battle in 1935, and Cleanth Brooks in 1939 read the Punic War as a trade war and so a parallel to the war of 1914–18: Eliot's point, on that reading, is that "all the wars are one war". The friend met in the City of London a few years after the Armistice was "in the ships" twenty-two centuries before.

Stetson has often been taken for Pound, who wore a wide-brimmed Stetson hat; Southam's <i>Guide</i> records the identification. A correspondent in the <i>Times Literary Supplement</i> in 1973 reported Eliot as saying that he meant no one in particular, only a superior bank clerk in a bowler hat. Other suggestions are the slouch hat of the Australian troops at Gallipoli, and Kipling's bank clerk Charlie Mears, who remembers rowing in an ancient galley (Robert Crawford, 1987; Donald Childs, 1988). Nothing in the poem needs him to be anyone in particular. The meeting is in King William Street, not on the bridge.

### corpse
Verdict: correct

Checked: Frazer, <i>The Golden Bough</i>, 1922 abridgement (Gutenberg #3623), ch. XXXIII "The Gardens of Adonis" and ch. XXXIX "The Ritual of Osiris"; and <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, 3rd ed. (1914), vol. II, text at archive.org (in.ernet.dli.2015.167743): "effigies of Osiris made of earth and corn", "corn-stuffed effigies of Osiris"; the phrase "Osiris beds" occurs in neither. Eliot's headnote in poem-part1.md ("the two volumes <i>Atthis Adonis Osiris</i>").

Substance:
1. "the volumes Eliot's headnote singles out (<i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>)": Frazer's title is <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris: Studies in the History of Oriental Religion</i> (Part IV of the third edition, two volumes, 1914). Eliot's note misspells and reorders it. The gloss is right to give Frazer's title; it could say in passing that Eliot's note garbles it, since the reader sees both.
2. "the 'gardens of Adonis': pots of quick-sprouting seed carried in the god's funeral procession and thrown into the sea": matches Frazer ("baskets or pots filled with earth, in which wheat, barley, lettuces, fennel, and various kinds of flowers were sown and tended for eight days ... carried out with the images of the dead Adonis, and flung with them into the sea or into springs"). Right.
3. "the 'Osiris beds,' effigies of the god filled with earth and corn and buried so that the sprouting grain showed his resurrection": the description is Frazer's, the phrase in quotation marks is not. Frazer writes of "effigies of Osiris made of earth and corn" buried at the festival of sowing in the month of Khoiak and taken up when the corn had sprouted, and of "corn-stuffed effigies" found in tombs, whose sprouting (he quotes Erman) "is intended to signify the resurrection of the god". "Osiris bed" is the Egyptologists' term from the tomb reports of 1907–15 (Davies, Quibell, Gardiner). Use Frazer's words or drop the quotation marks.
4. "The corpse in Stetson's suburban garden": "suburban" is not in the poem; the garden is not placed. Cut or mark as a guess.
5. "Will it bloom? Has the frost got at it? The question is asked in the voice of a man talking about bulbs, and it is the question of the whole poem: can anything come back?" The last clause is a reading (the death-and-rebirth reading that runs from Matthiessen and Brooks onward); mark it or cut it. The "voice of a man talking about bulbs" is a fair observation and needs no source.
6. Missing: Eliot's headnote says readers of Frazer "will immediately recognise in the poem certain references to vegetation ceremonies". This is the plainest of them; a clause saying so ties the gloss to the note the reader has just seen.

Draft:
TITLE: The buried god
BODY: Frazer's <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, the two volumes Eliot's headnote names (with the title garbled), describes the "gardens of Adonis": pots of earth sown with quick-sprouting wheat and barley, tended for eight days, then carried out with the images of the dead god and thrown into the sea or into springs. It also describes the effigies of Osiris made of earth and corn that were buried at the Egyptian festival of sowing and taken up when the grain had sprouted, and the corn-stuffed figures of the god found in tombs, whose sprouting, Frazer says, signified his resurrection. Eliot's headnote promises that readers of Frazer will "immediately recognise in the poem certain references to vegetation ceremonies". This is the plainest of them, asked in the voice of a man talking to a neighbour about his bulbs.

### dog
Verdict: correct

Checked: Webster, <i>The White Devil</i> (1612), Gutenberg #12915: the dirge is in Act V, scene iv, sung by Cornelia over Marcello, whom his brother Flamineo kills in V.ii ("Flamineo runs Marcello through"); stage direction, "Cornelia doth this in several forms of distraction"; the ten lines as quoted. Eliot, "Philip Massinger" (1920), <i>The Sacred Wood</i> (en.wikisource): "Immature poets imitate; mature poets steal; bad poets deface what they take, and good poets make it into something better, or at least something different." Brooks 1939 on the Dog, in archive.org full-text snippets of the Merrill Studies reprint and of <i>Notes and Queries</i>, 19 Aug. 1950: "the Dog (the capital letter is Eliot's) as Humanitarianism and the related philosophies ...". Frazer, <i>Golden Bough</i> abr. ch. XXXIX and <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i> II: the rising of Sirius, "the star of Isis", marked the rise of the Nile and the start of the sacred year. Representative Poetry Online (Toronto), note on l. 74: Sirius given as one of four readings.

Substance:
1. TITLE "Webster's dirge, with one word changed", and the last sentence, "The change of one word": wrong. Set the couplets side by side. Webster: "But keep the wolf far thence, that's foe to men, / For with his nails he'll dig them up again." Eliot: "Oh keep the Dog far hence, that's friend to men, / Or with his nails he'll dig it up again!" Five words change (wolf/Dog, thence/hence, foe/friend, For/Or, them/it) and "Oh" is added. The body itself names two changes. The title must go, and the Massinger sentence be reworded ("changes of a word here and there").
2. "Cornelia's mad song over her murdered son in <i>The White Devil</i> (1612), Act V scene iv": right. Marcello, killed by his brother Flamineo (V.ii); the stage direction has her sing "in several forms of distraction".
3. "Eliot swaps the wolf for a dog and 'foe' for 'friend,' and capitalises the Dog": right as far as it goes (see 1).
4. "The domestic pet, man's friend, becomes the thing that will not let the buried god stay buried long enough to sprout." A reading; mark it. The reading on record is Cleanth Brooks's (1939): the capitalised Dog as "Humanitarianism and the related philosophies", the well-meant modern thing that digs up what should be left to lie and grow.
5. "Readers who want more have found the Dog Star, Sirius, whose rising announced the flooding of the Nile and the return of Osiris." The Sirius reading is in several annotated editions and student guides (the Toronto RPO notes, for one). Its basis is in Frazer's own pages, where the rising of Sirius, "the star of Isis", marks the rising of the Nile and the goddess coming to wake her dead lover. "The return of Osiris" stretches that; "the flooding of the Nile" is enough. Say "some annotators" or name one.
6. Massinger: wording, essay and date are right; a 1920 text, free to quote.
7. Quote: matches Webster, the four omitted lines marked by the ellipsis. Some editions print "robin redbreast" unhyphenated; not an issue.

Draft:
TITLE: Webster's dirge, altered
BODY: Cornelia's song over her murdered son Marcello in Webster's <i>The White Devil</i> (1612), Act V scene iv, sung, the stage direction says, "in several forms of distraction". Eliot alters the couplet in five places: Webster's wolf becomes the Dog, with a capital; "foe to men" becomes "friend to men"; "thence" becomes "hence", "for" becomes "or", "them" becomes "it". Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the capitalised Dog as humanitarianism, the well-meant modern thing that digs up what should be left to lie and grow; some annotators see the Dog Star, Sirius, whose rising marked the flooding of the Nile in the Egyptian calendar Frazer describes. Eliot's own account of such borrowing is in "Philip Massinger" (1920): "Immature poets imitate; mature poets steal; bad poets deface what they take, and good poets make it into something better, or at least something different."

### hypocrite-lecteur
Verdict: correct

Checked: Baudelaire, "Au Lecteur", 1861 text (fr.wikisource, Les_Fleurs_du_mal/1861/Au_lecteur): stands at the head of the collection before "Bénédiction"; the "ménagerie infâme de nos vices" stanza, then "Il en est un plus laid, plus méchant, plus immonde !", then the final stanza exactly as the site quotes it, dashes included. Eliot's note 76 ("V. Baudelaire, Preface to <i>Fleurs du Mal</i>") and the 1922 punctuation of line 76 in poem-part1.md.

Substance:
1. Quote: matches the 1861 text.
2. Translation: "my double" for "mon semblable" is loose; "semblable" is one's like or fellow-creature, not a doppelgänger. "My likeness" (or "my fellow") is closer. "Boredom" for "Ennui" is the usual choice and fine.
3. "The last line of 'Au Lecteur,' the poem that opens <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i> (1857)": right; it is the prefatory poem in both the 1857 and 1861 editions, which is why Eliot's note calls it the preface.
4. "Baudelaire's catalogue of vices ends with the worst, boredom, and then turns on the reader and makes him an accomplice": right.
5. "Eliot ends the first section by doing the same thing to you. The 'you' that has been Stetson is suddenly whoever is holding the book, and the Frenchness of the line is part of the accusation: you were meant to recognise it." A standard reading, told in the second person. One fact to keep straight: in the 1922 text the line is still inside Stetson's speech, opening with a quotation mark like the lines before it and beginning "You!"; the turn to the reader comes through Baudelaire's line, not through a change of addressee in the text. "You were meant to recognise it" can be grounded in Eliot's 1950 remark about giving references in the notes so that a reader who recognised an allusion would know he was meant to (see london-bridge-dead).
6. Nothing missing.

Draft:
TITLE: The reader accused
BODY: The last line of "Au Lecteur", the prefatory poem of <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i> (1857), which Eliot's note calls its preface. Baudelaire's catalogue of vices ends with the ugliest of them, Ennui, and then turns on the reader as its accomplice. In the 1922 text the line is still Stetson's, inside the quotation marks and opening with "You!"; but Baudelaire's line is addressed to the reader, and a reader who knows it is addressed too. Eliot said in 1950 that he gave his references in the notes so that a reader who recognised an allusion would know he was meant to.

### summary
1. dog: the title and last sentence say "one word changed"; Eliot changes five (wolf/Dog, thence/hence, foe/friend, For/Or, them/it) and adds "Oh". Act V scene iv, Cornelia, Marcello: all right.
2. stetson: "a trade war between two commercial empires" and "all wars are the one war" are Cleanth Brooks's reading (1939, after Matthiessen 1935) given as history; the meeting is in King William Street, not "on a bridge". Eliot's reported denial ("any superior bank clerk ... in a bowler hat") comes from a TLS letter of 11 May 1973, cited by Childs (1988); usable only as a report.
3. london-bridge-dead: "damned twice over ... merely born too early" misstates Dante (the neutrals are refused by Hell itself; Limbo has no torment; its souls lacked baptism, and include Saladin and Averroes). The "half past eight" is invented. Eliot's own "my city clerks trooping over London Bridge from the railway station to their offices" (1950) is the source to cite.
4. woolnoth: Eliot's day at the bank began at 9.15 (letter to his mother, March 1917), so the clock did not strike the start of it; the reburial was in the City of London Cemetery at Manor Park; the poem's crowd does not come out of the Tube. "Across the road" and the dates are right; drop the department from "1917 to 1925".
5. corpse: "Osiris beds" is not Frazer's phrase (he writes "effigies of Osiris made of earth and corn", "corn-stuffed effigies"); "suburban" is not in the poem; "the question of the whole poem" is a reading.
6. unreal-city: the 1950 quotation is longer than the rights rule allows and lacks its source ("What Dante Means to Me"); "every working morning" overstates. Lines 207 and 377 are right in the site's count.
7. hypocrite-lecteur: "my double" should be "my likeness" for "mon semblable"; the body otherwise stands, with the note that in 1922 the line is still Stetson's.
