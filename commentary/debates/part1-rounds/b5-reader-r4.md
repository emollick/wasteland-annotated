### dead@62
Verdict: not yet
1. "as Eliot's note says: "I had not thought death had undone so many."" — the colon hands the poem's own line to Eliot's note, whose words are the Italian; and "<i>Inferno</i> III, the train of the dead" makes a canto sound like a crowd. Put: Dante's crowd on London Bridge. Line 63, "I had not thought death had undone so many," is from <i>Inferno</i> III, where Dante sees the train of the dead just inside the gate of Hell; Eliot's note gives the passage.

### dead@71
Verdict: not yet
1. "Eliot's headnote promises readers of Frazer ... and this is the plainest of them" — "promises" makes the headnote wry, and "the plainest" is a ranking given as a fact, which is what this pass is meant to remove. Put: "That corpse you planted last year in your garden." Eliot's headnote says that readers of Frazer will recognise "references to vegetation ceremonies" in the poem, and this is one of them: a god buried so that he will come up again, here a corpse in a garden.

### dead@74
Verdict: not yet
1. "Cornelia's dirge" — a name with no word of who she is, on a pass that is removing names given bare. Without adding a fact, put: The dirge Cornelia sings in Webster's <i>The White Devil</i> for "the friendless bodies of unburied men", with the wolf turned into a dog and the foe into a friend. For the scholar: the front-matter reader's own correction has her as a mother singing over her murdered son; if that is confirmed, the stop should carry it, thus: In Webster's <i>The White Devil</i>, Cornelia sings this dirge over her murdered son, for "the friendless bodies of unburied men"; Eliot turns the wolf into a dog and the foe into a friend.

### cities@8
Verdict: not yet
1. "the Hofgarten, in the city centre" — the lake is glossed as a lake, but the Hofgarten only by where it is, and a reader who takes "into the Hofgarten, / And drank coffee" for a café is not put right. The name means a garden, so this adds no fact. Put: The Starnbergersee, the lake south-west of Munich, and the Hofgarten, a garden in the city centre. Eliot spent the summer of 1911 in Munich.

### birds@23
Verdict: not yet
1. "Not a bird." — true, but the stop then never says why a cricket is on a walk through the poem's birds; the reason went out with the antithesis, and the front-matter reader's own correction kept it ("the first singing creature in the poem"). Put: "the cricket no relief." Not a bird, but the first creature in the poem that sings. Eliot's cricket stands in for the grasshopper of Ecclesiastes 12:5, the verse his note cites, where "the grasshopper shall be a burden."
