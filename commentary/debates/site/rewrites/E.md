# Batch E: the editor's rewrites after round one

Both sides passed the SHOWS field on nearly every card: the drawings are described plainly. Both flagged the same ANSWERS: studio remarks left on the page (an ink percentage, a group id, "No accent", a file name, "not to be confused with"), names dropped without a word of who they are (Pater, Frazer, Weston, Shackleton, Nerval, Larisch, Pamela Colman Smith), untranslated Latin, French and Sanskrit, and three winks. The scholar found two quotations that do not match the 1922 text (line 112's full stop; "ceu", not "uti", at 429), one wrong line range (the swallow is 429), one story credited to the wrong author (the Sibyl's long life is Ovid's, not Petronius's), and two readings stated as facts (the Sailor as the Hanged Man; the Lady of the Rocks as Leonardo's). Where they differed, the scholar's facts and the reader's wording are taken. The vignette titles follow the reader's plainer forms, which the scholar also offered.

Unchanged: crowd-on-london-bridge, stetson, lady-in-the-chair, closing-time, magnus-martyr, phlebas, falling-towers, bats-with-baby-faces, empty-chapel; and seven vignette titles (The Cannon Street Hotel; The Pagoda at Kew; A public bar in Lower Thames Street; The shelter on Margate Sands; St Magnus the Martyr, Lower Thames Street; St Mary Woolnoth, Lombard Street; The Metropole, Brighton).

Format: each card gives the field, then OLD and NEW. Where a field is not listed it stands.

## phoenician-sailor
ANSWERS OLD: 47–48, and Part IV (312–321). "Those are pearls that were his eyes" is Ariel's song from <i>The Tempest</i>. He is drawn inverted on purpose: he is the Hanged Man the reading cannot find, gone into the water instead.
ANSWERS NEW: 47–48, "Is your card, the drowned Phoenician Sailor, / (Those are pearls that were his eyes. Look!)", and Part IV, Death by Water (312–321). The pearls are from Ariel's song in <i>The Tempest</i> (I. ii). He is drawn head down, in the posture of the Tarot's Hanged Man, the card Madame Sosostris does not find (54–55). That is the drawing's suggestion, not Eliot's: his note on 46 links the Hanged Man to Frazer's Hanged God and to the hooded figure of Part V, and says nothing of the Sailor.

## belladonna
SHOWS OLD: … the pupils of her eyes are wide (belladonna drops); …
SHOWS NEW: A woman seated in a grotto of stone, in a black mantle and gown that leave only her face and hands bare; the pupils of her eyes are wide, as belladonna drops make them; her lips are violet; she holds up a sprig of deadly nightshade, leaves, bells and berries. Sea visible through the mouth of the cave.
ANSWERS OLD: 49–50. "The Lady of the Rocks" is Leonardo's Virgin of the Rocks (and Pater's Mona Lisa, older than the rocks among which she sits). Belladonna is the plant, the poison, the cosmetic and "beautiful lady".
ANSWERS NEW: 49–50, "Here is Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks, / The lady of situations." "The Lady of the Rocks" recalls Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i> and Walter Pater's page on the Mona Lisa in <i>The Renaissance</i> (1873): "She is older than the rocks among which she sits." <i>Belladonna</i> is Italian for "beautiful lady"; it is also the deadly nightshade, a poison, and the drops made from it that women once used to widen their pupils.

## three-staves
ANSWERS OLD: 51, with Eliot's note tying him to the Fisher King (so: a wound to lean on, a line to fish with, a rod of office set in the waste ground). The composition follows Pamela Colman Smith's Three of Wands.
ANSWERS NEW: 51, "Here is the man with three staves". Eliot's note: "The Man with Three Staves (an authentic member of the Tarot pack) I associate, quite arbitrarily, with the Fisher King himself", the wounded king of the Grail legend whose land lies waste. The drawing gives him a crutch for the wound, a rod to fish with, and a staff set in the dry ground, which has begun to sprout. The composition follows the Three of Wands as Pamela Colman Smith drew it for the Rider pack of 1909: a man with his back turned, on a height, looking out over the sea at ships.

## wheel
ANSWERS OLD: 51; the ship's wheel looks ahead to "you who turn the wheel and look to windward" (320). The four stations are the medieval regno, regnavi, sum sine regno, regnabo.
ANSWERS NEW: 51, "and here the Wheel". It is drawn as a ship's wheel because of line 320, "O you who turn the wheel and look to windward". The four figures follow the medieval pictures of Fortune's wheel, as in the Carmina Burana manuscript, where a king is shown at four points of the turn: <i>regno</i> (I reign) at the top, <i>regnavi</i> (I have reigned) falling, <i>sum sine regno</i> (I have no kingdom) beneath, <i>regnabo</i> (I shall reign) climbing.

## one-eyed-merchant
SHOWS OLD: Mr. Eugenides in profile: a profile shows one eye, which is the only kind of one-eyed man a printed card can have. Bowler, astrakhan collar, long overcoat, unshaven, a pocket full of currants and a handful held out in invitation. On his back a large roped bundle whose contents are not shown. A City wall and a doorway behind.
SHOWS NEW: Mr. Eugenides in profile, so that only one eye shows. Bowler hat, astrakhan collar, long overcoat, unshaven; a pocket full of currants and a handful held out. On his back a large roped bundle, its contents hidden. A wall and a doorway behind.
ANSWERS OLD: 52, and 207–214 (the Smyrna merchant, unshaven, currants, the Cannon Street Hotel). Eliot's note: the merchant "melts into" the Phoenician Sailor.
ANSWERS NEW: 52, "the one-eyed merchant". Eliot's note on 218 speaks of "the one-eyed merchant, seller of currants", who "melts into the Phoenician Sailor". The seller of currants is Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant, "Unshaven, with a pocket full of currants" (209–210), so the card draws him as Eugenides.

## hanged-man
SHOWS OLD: A dead tree with one long bough for a beam; a rope tied to it, its noose hanging open. The whole air is dense cross-hatching, and where the Hanged Man should hang, inverted, one leg crooked, the paper is left bare in his shape: the card shows the place he is missing from. Round the head that is not there, a gold halo. Far off at the bottom, on a road, two walkers and a hooded third. A green shoot on the dead bough.
SHOWS NEW: A dead tree with one long bough for a beam and a rope tied to it, the noose hanging open. The air is dense cross-hatching; where the Hanged Man would hang, head down with one leg crooked, the paper is left bare in his outline, with a gold halo where the head would be. Far off at the bottom, on a road, two walkers and a hooded third. A green shoot on the dead bough.
ANSWERS OLD: 54–55: "I do not find / The Hanged Man." Eliot's note ties him to Frazer's hanged god and to the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus (360–366), which is why the three walkers are there, and why the bough sprouts.
ANSWERS NEW: 54–55, "I do not find / The Hanged Man." Eliot's note on 46 says the card "fits my purpose in two ways": it is "associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer", the sacrificed god of <i>The Golden Bough</i>, killed so that the land may live again, and with "the hooded figure in the passage of the disciples to Emmaus in Part V" (360–366), where the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples (Luke 24). The walkers on the road and the shoot on the dead bough come from that note; the inverted pose, the crooked leg and the halo are Pamela Colman Smith's.

## crowd-in-a-ring
ANSWERS OLD: 56, "I see crowds of people, walking round in a ring", and ahead to the crowd flowing over London Bridge (62–65) and Dante's dead.
ANSWERS NEW: 56, "I see crowds of people, walking round in a ring." The crowd that flows over London Bridge follows at 62–65; Eliot's notes on 63 and 64 refer those lines to the crowds of the dead in Dante's <i>Inferno</i> (cantos iii and iv).

## back
ANSWERS OLD: Death by water, and the current under sea that picks the drowned man's bones. The pearl is the one from the Sailor's eye.
ANSWERS NEW: "Fear death by water" (55), and Part IV, Death by Water: "A current under sea / Picked his bones in whispers" (315–316). The pearl is one of the drowned sailor's eyes, "Those are pearls that were his eyes" (48).

## sibyl
ANSWERS OLD: The epigraph, from Petronius: the Sibyl of Cumae, who asked for life without asking for youth, hanging in her jar while the boys ask "Sibyl, what do you want?" and she answers "I want to die."
ANSWERS NEW: The epigraph, from Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i> (chapter 48). Trimalchio says he saw the Sibyl at Cumae with his own eyes, hanging in a jar, and that when the boys asked her "Sibyl, what do you want?" she answered "I want to die." How she came to it is told in Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> xiv: Apollo granted her as many years as the grains in a handful of sand, and she did not ask for youth.

## marie
ANSWERS OLD: 13–18, "Marie, / Marie, hold on tight. And down we went. / In the mountains, there you feel free." (The Countess Marie Larisch, the archduke's cousin.)
ANSWERS NEW: 13–18, "Marie, / Marie, hold on tight. And down we went. / In the mountains, there you feel free." Valerie Eliot's note in the 1971 facsimile of the drafts identifies Marie with the Countess Marie Larisch (1858–1940), a niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria and author of a memoir, <i>My Past</i> (1913), whom Eliot had met; the sledding, she says, was taken from his conversation with her.

SHOWS NEW: A girl standing in a garden with her arms full of hyacinth spikes, hair wet, looking straight out. Round her the hatching of the garden opens into a bare oval, the "heart of light" of line 41. Dark petals falling at the edges.

## philomel
SHOWS OLD: A picture over an antique mantel, framed as though a window gave upon the sylvan scene: Philomel running to the left, her arms already turning to wings, her head turned back; the barbarous king a black figure behind her, crowned, sword raised; in the tree above, the nightingale singing. The fire-opening dark below the shelf.
SHOWS NEW: A picture over an antique mantel, framed "as though a window gave upon the sylvan scene" (98). In it Philomel runs to the left, her arms already turning to wings, her head turned back; behind her the barbarous king, a black figure, crowned, with his sword raised; in the tree above, the nightingale sings. Below the mantelshelf, the dark of the fireplace.
ANSWERS OLD: 97–103, "The change of Philomel, by the barbarous king / So rudely forced; yet there the nightingale / Filled all the desert with inviolable voice". Ovid, Metamorphoses vi.
ANSWERS NEW: 97–103, "The change of Philomel, by the barbarous king / So rudely forced; yet there the nightingale / Filled all the desert with inviolable voice". Eliot's note refers to Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> vi: Tereus, king of Thrace, rapes his wife's sister Philomela and cuts out her tongue, and in the telling Eliot follows she is turned into a nightingale.

## bad-nerves
ANSWERS OLD: 111–138, "My nerves are bad to-night. Yes, bad. Stay with me. / Speak to me. Why do you never speak?", the wind under the door, rats' alley, "And we shall play a game of chess". No accent. The name on the band is the poem's line: My Nerves Are Bad To-night.
ANSWERS NEW: 111–138, "My nerves are bad to-night. Yes, bad. Stay with me. / Speak to me. Why do you never speak. Speak."; also "The wind under the door" (118), "rats' alley" (115) and "we shall play a game of chess" (137).

## fisher-king
ANSWERS OLD: 187–195, "While I was fishing in the dull canal / On a winter evening round behind the gashouse", and Eliot's note on Weston's From Ritual to Romance.
ANSWERS NEW: 187–195, "While I was fishing in the dull canal / On a winter evening round behind the gashouse". The Fisher King is the wounded king of the Grail legend, whose land lies waste until he is healed. Eliot's headnote says the poem's title, plan and much of its symbolism came from Jessie L. Weston's book on the legend, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920); his note on line 425, "Fishing, with the arid plain behind me", sends the reader to her chapter on the Fisher King, and his note on 46 attaches the king to the Man with Three Staves.

## eugenides
ANSWERS OLD: 207–214, "Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant / Unshaven, with a pocket full of currants". He is the one-eyed merchant of the reading seen from the front.
ANSWERS NEW: 207–214, "Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant / Unshaven, with a pocket full of currants". He is the one-eyed merchant of Madame Sosostris's pack (52), drawn there in profile and here full-face; Eliot's note on 218 calls that merchant "seller of currants".

## tiresias
ANSWERS OLD: 215–248, "I Tiresias, though blind, throbbing between two lives, / Old man with wrinkled female breasts". The snakes are from Ovid, in Eliot's note.
ANSWERS NEW: 215–248, "I Tiresias, though blind, throbbing between two lives, / Old man with wrinkled female breasts". Eliot's note on 218 quotes the passage of Ovid (<i>Metamorphoses</i> iii) in which Tiresias strikes two coupling snakes with his staff and is turned into a woman for seven years, then strikes them again and becomes a man once more. Hence the snakes at his feet.

## thames-daughters

ANSWERS NEW: 266–306, "The river sweats / Oil and tar / The barges drift / With the turning tide / Red sails / Wide". ## elizabeth-and-leicester
ANSWERS OLD: 279–291, "Elizabeth and Leicester / Beating oars / The stern was formed / A gilded shell / Red and gold" and the white towers.
ANSWERS NEW: 279–291, "Elizabeth and Leicester / Beating oars / The stern was formed / A gilded shell / Red and gold", and the "White towers" of line 289. Eliot's note on 279 quotes, from Froude's history of Elizabeth, a letter of the Spanish ambassador De Quadra about an afternoon in the royal barge when the Queen sat with Lord Robert Dudley, later Earl of Leicester, and there was talk of their marrying.

## road-with-no-water
SHOWS OLD: … two dry cracks of thunder in the sky and no cloud that will rain.
SHOWS NEW: A sandy road winding up between two masses of rock toward a gap; in the right-hand mass a black cave mouth with broken teeth hanging in it; cracked earth either side of the road; three flat-roofed mudcracked houses with a red face sneering from each door; dry grass; a cicada on a stone; two dry cracks of thunder in the sky and no cloud.

## the-third
ANSWERS OLD: 360–366, "Who is the third who walks always beside you? / When I count, there are only you and I together", with Eliot's note on Shackleton's party. No accent: the plainest card after the blank.
ANSWERS NEW: 360–366, "Who is the third who walks always beside you? / When I count, there are only you and I together". Eliot's note says the lines were "stimulated by the account of one of the Antarctic expeditions (I forget which, but I think one of Shackleton's)", whose party, at the end of its strength, "had the constant delusion that there was one more member than could actually be counted". His note on Part V as a whole names "the journey to Emmaus" (Luke 24), where the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples.

## thunder
SHOWS OLD: One great black cloud filling the top of the card, its underside lobed, and three forks of bare lightning out of it: the thunder's three words. …
SHOWS NEW: One great black cloud filling the top of the card, its underside lobed, and three forks of lightning out of it, one for each of the thunder's three words. Below, the white peaks of Himavant, the jungle crouched in humps, and Ganga sunken to a thread between wide cracked banks with limp leaves hanging at the edge. On the water a small boat responding to its oar and sail; on the near bank a gold key.
ANSWERS OLD: 396–423, "Ganga was sunken, and the limp leaves / Waited for rain, while the black clouds / Gathered far distant, over Himavant" and DA, DA, DA: the boat is Damyata, the key Dayadhvam ("I have heard the key / Turn in the door once").
ANSWERS NEW: 396–423, "Ganga was sunken, and the limp leaves / Waited for rain, while the black clouds / Gathered far distant, over Himavant". Ganga is the Ganges and Himavant the Himalaya. The thunder then speaks three times, "DA", and each time the speaker hears in it one of three Sanskrit words, <i>Datta</i>, <i>Dayadhvam</i>, <i>Damyata</i>, which Eliot's note translates "give, sympathise, control" and traces to a fable in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad. The key on the bank is for <i>Dayadhvam</i> ("I have heard the key / Turn in the door once", 412–413); the boat is for <i>Damyata</i> ("The boat responded / Gaily, to the hand expert with sail and oar", 419–420).

## london-bridge-falling
ANSWERS OLD: 427, "London Bridge is falling down falling down falling down". No accent. Not to be confused with crowd-on-london-bridge.svg (60).
ANSWERS NEW: 427, "London Bridge is falling down falling down falling down", the nursery rhyme. The bridge standing, with the crowd on it, is the card The Crowd on London Bridge (60–68).

## swallow
ANSWERS OLD: 428–429, "Quando fiam uti chelidon — O swallow swallow": the Pervigilium Veneris, where Philomela's sister becomes the swallow, and Tennyson's "O Swallow, Swallow".
ANSWERS NEW: 429, "Quando fiam ceu chelidon—O swallow swallow". The Latin is from the last stanza of the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i> ("The Vigil of Venus"), an anonymous Latin poem of late antiquity: "when shall I be as the swallow, that I may cease to be silent?", asked after the nightingale's song of Tereus's crime. Eliot's note adds "Cf. Philomela in Parts II and III"; in the usual telling of the story Philomela's sister Procne became the swallow. "O swallow swallow" echoes a song in Tennyson's <i>The Princess</i> (1847), "O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South".

## tower
ANSWERS OLD: 430, "Le Prince d'Aquitaine à la tour abolie", Nerval's El Desdichado: the disinherited prince, the ruined tower, "ma seule étoile est morte", "mon luth constellé / Porte le soleil noir de la Mélancolie". The Tower is also a trump of the real pack, struck by lightning.
ANSWERS NEW: 430, "Le Prince d'Aquitaine à la tour abolie" (the Prince of Aquitaine of the ruined tower), the second line of Gérard de Nerval's sonnet <i>El Desdichado</i> ("The Disinherited", in <i>Les Chimères</i>, 1854), which Eliot's note cites. The star, the lute and the black sun on the card are from the same sonnet: "ma seule étoile est morte" (my only star is dead) and "mon luth constellé / Porte le soleil noir de la Mélancolie" (my star-strewn lute bears the black sun of Melancholy). The Tower is also trump XVI of the real Tarot pack, where it is shown struck by lightning.

## Noted, not changed here
- The scholar found the drawings disagree with each other in two places (the merchant's bowler on one card and homburg on another; London Bridge with three arches on one card and five on another). That is for the artist, not the text; it is recorded for the art thread.
- "The Metropole, Brighton" keeps the usual identification (Southam); the poem says only "the Metropole". The map's caption already says "a weekend at the Metropole, Brighton"; if that caption changes, it should say "usually taken to be".
- The scholar's remark that "Glowed into words." closes a quotation with a full stop where the line runs on is a convention the site uses throughout; left as it is.
