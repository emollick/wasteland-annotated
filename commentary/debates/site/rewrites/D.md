# Rewrite, batch D: the listening room (listen.txt, 26 records)

Editor's merge of r1/D-scholar.md and r1/D-reader.md. The scholar's facts, the reader's wording. Only the
changed fields are given; the current text is in /mnt/project-files/commentary/site/listen.txt. Fields not
listed stand. id, group, embed and host are fixed; who and when are changed only where the scholar found
them wrong or incomplete. {link:ID|text} and {parts} placeholders are kept exactly.

Decisions:
- The five bodies that described a performance in a reviewer's voice (the Faber extract, Harper Audio,
  Guinness, Hughes, Mortensen) now say what the recording is, how much of the poem it covers and what
  state the sound is in.
- Irons and Atkins: the body now opens with what actually plays (the 2017 programme) and the title names
  both broadcasts; the 2012 reading is described in the second sentence. The record's embed cannot change.
- Three wrong statements are corrected: the Criterion has a public scan (HathiTrust); the 2012 reading is
  online in listeners' copies; the Poetry Archive credits its redating to Lacy Rumsey.
- "Whole poem" is said only where the scholar confirmed it (Guinness, LibriVox); Borris says "the poem".

### eliot-1933-poetryarchive
body: The earliest recording, cut on aluminium discs at Columbia University by W. Cabell Greet and George W. Hibbitt of Barnard College, in the year Eliot spent in America as Norton Professor at Harvard. Eliot was forty-four; the voice is younger and lighter than in the later recording. The Poetry Archive plays it in five parts on its own site, one page for each: {parts}. The Archive dates it 1933 and says the date was once thought to be 1935, crediting Lacy Rumsey of the ENS de Lyon for the correction; PennSound, which holds the same Columbia series, labels it 1935.

### eliot-1933-faber-soundcloud
title: The opening lines, from the 1933 reading
body: Sixty-four seconds of the Columbia recording, the opening lines of The Burial of the Dead, which Faber posted in April 2011 when its app of the poem was being made.

### eliot-1935-pennsound-columbia
title: The 1933 reading again, at PennSound
body: The same Columbia series in two complete versions, divided by section, on PennSound’s own page. PennSound holds it with the permission of the Eliot estate, for study and teaching. It gives the date as 1935, though its own headnote says the Columbia recordings were made in 1933, 1935 and later; the Poetry Archive’s redating to 1933 credits Lacy Rumsey and refers readers to Richard Swigg’s <i>Quick, Said the Bird</i> and Jason Camlot’s <i>Phonopoetics</i>.

### eliot-later-harperaudio-archive
when: The Caedmon recording, usually dated 1946; this copy put out online in January 1994
body: The later recording, the one issued on record, first by Caedmon and later by HarperCollins, which bought Caedmon, as Harper Audio. This copy was put out by Harper Audio on 18 January 1994 through the Internet Multicasting Service, an early internet radio channel, as sound files at 8 kHz, with HarperCollins’s consent to that distribution and no other; the Internet Archive keeps it as a thirty-one-minute MP3. The sound is poor, about the quality of a telephone line. The Poetry Archive dates the recording 1946; the item itself gives no year.

### eliot-1947-loc-national-gallery
body: A reading Eliot gave at the National Gallery of Art in Washington on 23 May 1947, with his own comments. Of The Waste Land he read only Part V; the rest of the recording is Prufrock, La Figlia che Piange, Sweeney Among the Nightingales, the Ariel poems, Landscapes, the last part of Ash-Wednesday and East Coker. The Library of Congress keeps it in its Archive of Recorded Poetry and Literature, and it streams on the Library’s page.

### guinness-argo-full-bookyards
title: Alec Guinness reads the whole poem, 1975
body: Guinness recorded The Waste Land and Four Quartets for Argo’s series British Poets of Our Time, made with the Arts Council of Great Britain and the BBC; the double album of 1975 gathers them with Prufrock and Journey of the Magi. The copy online is a listener’s upload to YouTube, so it is linked from here and not played on this page.

### irons-atkins-bbc-radio4-2012
title: Jeremy Irons, BBC Radio 4: with Eileen Atkins in 2012, alone in 2017
who: Jeremy Irons and Eileen Atkins; introduced by Rowan Williams, Jackie Kay, Matthew Hollis and Sean O’Brien
body: What plays below is the Internet Archive’s off-air recording of a Radio 4 programme of 1 January 2017: Irons reading the poem on his own, the third of five programmes in which Martha Kearney and guests, here Jeanette Winterson, Rowan Williams and Jackie Kay, introduced his readings of Eliot. Five years earlier, on 30 March 2012, Irons and Eileen Atkins had shared the poem’s voices between them on Radio 4; the BBC’s page has the details and no audio, and the copies on YouTube are listeners’ uploads, not played here.

### irons-cusack-borris-festival
when: Festival of Writing and Ideas, Borris House, County Carlow, June 2022
body: Irons and Cusack read the poem at the festival in June 2022, its centenary year, introduced by James Lever; fifty-two minutes, with the introduction and the audience left in. The festival posted its own recording in 2023.

### shaw-bbc-newsnight-clip
when: Newsnight, January 2015; the staging dates from 1995, the film from 1996
body: Deborah Warner directed Fiona Shaw in the poem as a piece of theatre: one performer on a bare stage, taking every voice. It opened in Brussels in May 1995, toured, and ran at Wilton’s Music Hall in the East End in 1997, which is why that hall is on the site’s map; {link:shaw-warner-film-cannes-1996|Warner’s film of it, made for the BBC, was shown at Cannes} in May 1996. The clip here is the BBC’s own: Shaw performing part of the poem on Newsnight in January 2015, fifty years after Eliot’s death. The film itself circulates only in unofficial copies, which are not played here.

### shaw-faber-waste-land-app
when: June 2011; updated for the centenary in January 2022 and released for iPhone
body: An edition of the poem for the iPad, made by Faber and Touch Press. It contains a performance by Fiona Shaw filmed for it; readings synchronised to the text by Eliot (two recordings), Ted Hughes, Alec Guinness, Viggo Mortensen, and Jeremy Irons with Eileen Atkins; the manuscript pages; and more than thirty-five short filmed commentaries, with Seamus Heaney and Jeanette Winterson among the speakers. The film below is Touch Press’s own demonstration of the app, not a reading of the poem.

### hughes-faber-soundcloud
title: Ted Hughes reads the pub scene from A Game of Chess
when: From the Penguin/Faber audiobook of 1996; an extract
body: The pub scene at the end of A Game of Chess, from Hughes’s reading of the whole poem for the Penguin/Faber audiobook of 1996. Faber posted the extract in April 2011, when its app was being made.

### mortensen-faber-soundcloud
title: Viggo Mortensen reads the opening of The Fire Sermon
body: An extract, the opening of The Fire Sermon, from the river to the fishing in the dull canal. Mortensen recorded the whole poem for Faber’s app in 2011; Faber posted this in April of that year.

### librivox-klett-2011
title: Elizabeth Klett reads the whole poem, for LibriVox
body: A LibriVox volunteer recording of the whole poem, released into the public domain, so anyone may copy and reuse it.

### tristan-1928-bayreuth-act1-sailor
who: Gustav Rödin, young sailor; Bayreuth Festival under Karl Elmendorff
body: Lines 31 to 34 quote the young sailor’s song at the start of Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde, sung unaccompanied from aloft, as if from the mast; Isolde, below, takes it as mockery of her. In the Archive’s track list the song is “Westwärts schweift der Blick”, track 2, and its return “Frisch weht der Wind”, track 5. The surface noise of the 78s is left in.

### tristan-1928-bayreuth-act3-shepherd
title: “Öd’ und leer das Meer”: the shepherd in Tristan, Act III
body: Line 42 quotes the shepherd’s answer to Kurwenal, Tristan’s servant, at the start of the last act of Tristan und Isolde: he has looked out to sea for Isolde’s ship and there is nothing. The prelude, track 52, and the shepherd’s cor anglais tune, track 53, come before it; the exchange is track 54.

### gotterdammerung-solti-1964-rhinemaidens
when: Recorded in 1964; the London Records (Decca) LP set of 1965, digitised by the Boston Public Library
body: The Thames-daughters’ refrain at lines 277–278 and 290–291 quotes the Rhinemaidens’ cry from the last act of Wagner’s Götterdämmerung, where they rise from the river to beg Siegfried for the ring. The scene begins on side nine, “Act III Scene 1”.

### shakespearian-rag-york-sheet-music
body: Line 128’s “O O O O that Shakespeherian Rag” adds a syllable to a song of 1912 advertised as a number from that year’s Ziegfeld Follies; the cover spells it “Shakesperian”. York University in Toronto has the sheet music, scanned with its cover. No recording from the time has turned up in the Library of Congress or the Internet Archive.

### xc715748-hermit-thrush-by-sa
body: A second recording. Eliot’s note on line 357 speaks of the hermit-thrush’s “water-dripping song”; the bird sings at lines 356 to 358. Licensed CC BY-SA, so this one may be reused with credit.

### xc664726-hermit-thrush-halley
body: A longer recording, two minutes and forty seconds. In his note on line 357 Eliot quotes Chapman’s Handbook of Birds of Eastern North America: “Its notes are not remarkable for variety or volume, but in purity and sweetness of tone and exquisite modulation they are unequaled.” Cornell’s Macaulay Library has {link:macaulay-hermit-thrush-song-article|an article on how the song differs across the continent}, with recordings from 1951 and 2006.

### bl-1922-first-edition-archive-duke
body: The book this site follows, scanned page by page by Duke University Libraries. The marginal line numbers are printed every ten lines. On page 42 the line “If there were water” is set like the run-over of the line before it, as if it were a line too long for the page and carried on, and the numbering skips it: from there the 1922 numbers run one behind the count used here.

### bl-1922-first-edition-archive-byu
title: The fourth printing, 1930: Brigham Young University’s copy
body: Not a second scan of the first printing. The library’s record calls it Gallup A6a and copy no. 596 of the thousand, while also noting a fourth printing; the title page reads Horace Liveright, the verso “Fourth Printing, April, 1930”, and there is no limitation leaf. The pages are the 1922 setting of type, page for page and with the same marginal numbers; “hot water” at line 135 and “Heimat” at line 32, where letters are missing in the Duke copy, print cleanly here. Copies of the first printing exist in two states: in the earlier, line 339 reads “Dead mountain mouth of carious teeth”; in the later the a of “mountain” had dropped out and it reads “mount in”. Duke’s copy is the later state, and this printing still reads “mount in”.

### dial-november-1922-archive
body: The poem as it first reached American readers, without the notes, in the magazine that gave Eliot its two-thousand-dollar award that year. Here “cruelest” has one l. The Criterion printing of October 1922, the earliest of all, is on HathiTrust, in full view from the United States.

### nypl-digital-waste-land-typescript
title: The manuscript, with Pound’s annotations
body: The typescript and manuscript pages, with Pound’s cuts and comments and Vivien Eliot’s notes, which Eliot sent to John Quinn in October 1922. After Quinn’s death they passed to his sister and then to his niece, who sold them to the Berg Collection in 1958; the library announced the purchase in 1968, after Eliot’s death. The library has digitised the pages but shows the images only in the building, so the link here goes to its record, not to the pages. The way to read them is Valerie Eliot’s facsimile edition of 1971, reissued by Faber in 2022 with the pages in colour.

### frazer-adonis-attis-osiris-1906
body: Eliot’s headnote names “the two volumes Adonis, Attis, Osiris”, which are Part IV of the third edition of The Golden Bough (1914). This is the book’s first edition of 1906, in one volume, where Frazer’s account of the dying and reviving gods, the gardens of Adonis and the images of the god thrown into the sea first appeared. Jessie Weston’s From Ritual to Romance is {link:gutenberg-4090-weston|on Project Gutenberg}, and so is {link:gutenberg-3623-frazer-golden-bough|the one-volume Golden Bough} of 1922.
