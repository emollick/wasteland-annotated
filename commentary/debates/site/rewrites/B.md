# Rewrite, batch B: pathway intros (paths.txt), the two drafts records (drafts.txt), the voices (voices.json), the timeline (timeline.json)

Editor's merge of r1/B-scholar.md and r1/B-reader.md. The scholar's facts, the reader's wording. Only the
editable fields are given (intro; title and body; label and note; what). Records not listed stand.

Decisions:
- "The title that was crossed out" becomes "The earlier title": nobody could confirm that the heading is
  struck through. Malory is left out (a critics' link; can return as "has been linked to").
- The Margate arrival date (15 October) is dropped: unconfirmed, and it does not fit "three weeks".
- "Cutting it roughly in half" is dropped: the count depends on what is counted.
- "The soldiers' song" is dropped: Eliot's note says only that the ballad was reported to him from Sydney.
- Line 12 gets the label "A voice in German" (there is no café in the poem) and its note says that giving
  it a second speaker is a reading. Likewise the fisher and the Fisher King.
- Verdenal is identified once, at his first appearance (1910–11), as a French medical student.
- Lombard Street is dropped from 1917: the sources disagree on the bank's address.

## paths.txt (intro only; titles unchanged)

### first
OLD: Twelve stops for someone meeting the poem for the first time. Each stop is a place to stand, not an explanation. Read the lines, read the note, move on.
NEW: Twelve stops through the poem for a reader meeting it for the first time. Each stop gives a passage and a short note on it, in the order of the poem. Read the lines first, then the note, then go on to the next stop.

### water
OLD: The poem's hydrology, from spring rain to the last rain. Follow the water and you follow the argument: water is what the waste land needs, water is what drowns you, and the two are never told apart.
NEW: The passages where water appears, in order, from the spring rain of the opening lines to the rain that comes at line 395 in Part V: the shower over the Starnbergersee, Madame Sosostris’s warning “Fear death by water,” the Thames of Part III, the drowning of Phlebas in Part IV, and the rock without water at the start of Part V. Eliot’s headnote to his notes credits Jessie L. Weston’s book on the Grail legend, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, one chapter of which is called “The Freeing of the Waters.”

### dead
OLD: Burials, drownings, bones and the crowd that death has undone. The poem's first section is called The Burial of the Dead, from the Book of Common Prayer, and the dead never quite stay in it.
NEW: The poem’s dead, in order: the crowd on London Bridge (lines 62–63), the corpse planted in the garden (71), Phlebas in Part IV, the “Dry bones” of line 391. Part I takes its title, The Burial of the Dead, from the burial service in the Book of Common Prayer.

### women
OLD: Eliot's note on Tiresias says "all the women are one woman." Here they are, in order of appearance, so that you can test the claim. Most of them speak; several are quotations; two are queens; one is a bird.
NEW: Eliot’s note on Tiresias (line 218) says that in the poem “all the women are one woman.” This pathway gives the poem’s women in the order they appear, so that you can judge the claim for yourself. Some speak in their own words; some are figures from older books, named or alluded to; Philomel, from Ovid, appears as a nightingale.

### cities
OLD: The poem's geography, in the order the poem gives it. It begins in Munich and ends on the Ganges, with London at its centre and five capitals falling in two lines.
NEW: The places the poem names, in the order it names them: Munich and the Starnbergersee at the start; London through the middle, with the bridge, the streets and churches of the City, the pubs and the river from Richmond to Greenwich; the Ganges and Himavant near the end; and in Part V the five cities named in two lines, “Jerusalem Athens Alexandria / Vienna London” (375–376).

### birds
OLD: Every bird in the poem is a musician, and every song is a complaint. Recordings, where they exist, are in the listening room.
NEW: The poem’s birds, in the order they appear, with the lines where each is heard: the nightingale into which Philomel was changed (99–103 and 203–206), the gulls that Phlebas forgot (313), the hermit-thrush of line 357, which Eliot’s note says he heard in Quebec County, the cock on the rooftree (392) and the swallow of line 429. Recordings of the birds themselves, where they exist, are in the listening room.

### nothing
OLD: One word, followed through the poem. It is said by a lover, a woman with bad nerves, a man who will not answer, a Thames-daughter, and the poor on Margate sands.
NEW: The word “nothing” wherever the poem uses it: at line 40, by the one who came back from the hyacinth garden and could not speak; at lines 120–126, between the woman with bad nerves and the man who does not answer her; and at lines 302 and 305, by the third Thames-daughter on Margate Sands.

### eyes
OLD: Eyes that fail, eyes turned to pearls, eyes fixed on feet, lidless eyes, a one-eyed merchant, a blind seer, eyes that were holden on the road to Emmaus. The poem's people cannot see, or see too much.
NEW: Eyes in the poem: the eyes that fail in the hyacinth garden (line 39), the pearls that were the drowned man’s eyes (48 and 125), the eyes each man fixes before his feet on London Bridge (65), the one-eyed merchant (52), the “lidless eyes” of line 138, and the blind Tiresias (218). Eliot’s note on Part V names the journey to Emmaus, where, in Luke 24, the disciples’ eyes “were holden,” held, so that they did not know who walked beside them.

### eliot
OLD: Places and days from the poet's own year, 1921 into 1922, that surface in the text. Not a key to the poem, but a map of where he was standing.
NEW: Places and dates from Eliot’s life in 1921 and 1922 that lie behind particular lines of the poem, such as Margate Sands (line 300) and the waters of Leman (182), each set beside the lines it bears on.

## drafts.txt (title and body)

### title-draft
OLD title: The title that was crossed out
NEW title: The earlier title
OLD body: Across the top of the typescript of the first two parts Eliot typed "He Do the Police in Different Voices," from Dickens's Our Mutual Friend. "The Waste Land" replaced it, with Weston's Grail country and Malory's "waste land" of the Dolorous Stroke behind it. The Dickens title survives in the notes only as a habit of mind: the poem reads the world's news aloud in every voice it can do.
NEW body: The typescripts of Parts I and II carry the heading “He Do the Police in Different Voices,” with “Part I” and “Part II” beneath it. The words are Betty Higden’s in Dickens’s <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (Book I, chapter 16), praising Sloppy, the foundling she has brought up, for the way he reads the newspaper aloud: “You mightn’t think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices.” The title page Eliot typed for the finished poem reads “The Waste Land,” and his headnote to the notes says that the title, the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism were suggested by Jessie L. Weston’s book on the Grail legend, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>. Dickens is not mentioned in the notes.

### epigraph-draft
title unchanged: The epigraph that was replaced
OLD body: Kurtz's "The horror! the horror!" from Conrad's Heart of Darkness stood at the head of the typescript. Pound doubted Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation"; Eliot called it "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," and then substituted the Sibyl from Petronius. The change turns a dying man's verdict on his life into an immortal's wish to be done with hers.
NEW body: The title page of the typescript carries, under “The Waste Land,” a passage from Conrad’s <i>Heart of Darkness</i> ending with the dying Kurtz’s words, “The horror! the horror!” Pound wrote, on 24 January 1922, that he doubted Conrad was “weighty enough to stand the citation”; Eliot answered that it was “much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative”; Pound then told him to do as he liked about Conrad. Eliot nevertheless replaced it with the sentence from Petronius’s <i>Satyricon</i> in which Trimalchio says that he saw the Sibyl of Cumae hanging in a jar, and that when the boys asked her what she wanted she answered, “I want to die.” The Sibyl had been granted long life, but not youth.

## voices.json (label and note; keys and colours unchanged; no tags in these strings)

### poem
label unchanged. note NEW: The voice that belongs to no named character. It opens the poem, describes the room at the start of Part II, introduces Madame Sosostris (43–46), speaks the lines on the drowned Phlebas in Part IV, and answers each DA of the thunder in Part V (402–409, 412–417, 419–423).

### marie
label unchanged. note NEW: A woman remembering a summer: coffee and talk in the Hofgarten in Munich, and a sled ride in the mountains as a child with her cousin the archduke. Lines 8–18, except the sentence of German at line 12, which this edition gives to another speaker. Valerie Eliot’s edition of the drafts records that Eliot had met Countess Marie Larisch, niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria, and that the sledding story was hers.

### stranger
label NEW: A voice in German
note NEW: Line 12, one sentence: “I am not Russian at all, I come from Lithuania, a real German.” The poem does not say who speaks it; giving it to a second speaker is a reading.

### prophet
label unchanged. note NEW: Lines 19–30: a voice speaking as the prophets do, addressing the reader as “Son of man” (Ezekiel’s phrase, as Eliot’s note says) and offering the shade of the red rock. His note on line 23 cites Ecclesiastes; “the shadow of this red rock” has been linked to Isaiah 32:2.

### lover
label unchanged. note NEW: Lines 37–41: the one who came back from the hyacinth garden and could not speak: “I could not / Speak, and my eyes failed.”

### shepherd
label unchanged. note NEW: Line 42: the shepherd in Tristan und Isolde, Act III, who has looked out for Isolde’s ship and reports the sea desolate and empty.

### sosostris
label unchanged. note NEW: The fortune-teller (the poem’s word is clairvoyante), reading the cards. Lines 43–46 introduce her in the poem’s own voice; she speaks from the middle of line 46 (“Here, said she”) to line 59.

### walker
label NEW: The walker in the City of London
note NEW: The “I” of three passages, read here as one voice: the crowd on London Bridge and the meeting with Stetson (60–76), Mr. Eugenides (207–211), and the music along the Strand and the bar in Lower Thames Street (257–265).

### she
label unchanged. note NEW: Lines 111–114, 117, 119, 121–123, 126 and 131–134, printed in quotation marks, unlike the man’s. The quotation marks are usually taken to mean that she speaks aloud.

### he
label unchanged. note NEW: Lines 115–116, 118, 120, 124–125, 127–130 and 135–138, printed without quotation marks, which is usually read as meaning that he does not speak aloud. Some of his replies are other people’s words: Webster (118) and Middleton (137–138), both cited in Eliot’s notes; Ariel’s song from The Tempest (125); “That Shakespearian Rag” (128–130).

### pub
label unchanged. note NEW: A woman in a London pub, telling what she said to her friend Lil about Lil’s husband Albert, home from the army. Lines 139–171, interrupted by the publican’s call.

### publican
label unchanged. note NEW: The landlord calling closing time: HURRY UP PLEASE ITS TIME, at lines 141, 152, 165, 168 and 169.

### ophelia
label unchanged. note NEW: Line 172: Ophelia’s good night in Hamlet (Act IV, scene 5), as she goes out, mad. Her drowning is reported two scenes later.

### fisher
label unchanged. note NEW: The speaker by the river in Part III, lines 173–198, who repeats Spenser’s refrain “Sweet Thames, run softly” and fishes in the dull canal behind the gashouse, speaking partly in others’ lines: Spenser (176, 183–184), the Psalmist’s “I sat down and wept” (182), Ferdinand in The Tempest (191–192, Eliot’s note). That he is the Fisher King of the Grail legend is a reading; Eliot’s notes name the Fisher King at lines 46 and 425.

### song
label NEW: Songs quoted
note NEW: Snatches of song: the Shakespeherian Rag (128–130); the ballad about Mrs Porter, which Eliot’s note says was reported to him from Sydney, Australia (199–201); and Verlaine’s line on children singing in the dome (202).

### nightingale
label unchanged. note NEW: Lines 203–206: the nightingale’s song as the Elizabethan poets wrote it, “jug jug” and “Tereu,” calling on Tereus, the king who raped Philomela; her story is told at lines 99–103, and “So rudely forc’d” repeats line 100.

### typist
label unchanged. note NEW: Line 252, her only words, in quotation marks: what the poem calls her “one half-formed thought.”

### thames
label unchanged. note NEW: The three Thames-daughters, as Eliot’s note calls them, singing together: lines 266–291. His note says that from line 292 to 306 they speak in turn. The refrain “Weialala leia” is the Rhine-daughters’ from Wagner’s Götterdämmerung, Act III, which the note cites.

### thames1
label unchanged. note NEW: Highbury, Richmond and Kew: lines 292–295. Eliot’s note sends the reader to La Pia in Dante’s Purgatorio, canto V: “Siena made me, Maremma unmade me.”

### ascetic
label unchanged. note NEW: Lines 307–311: Augustine’s Confessions (“To Carthage then I came,” 307, and 309–310) set beside the Buddha’s Fire Sermon (“Burning,” 308 and 311). Eliot’s note calls them “two representatives of eastern and western asceticism” and says that their collocation “is not an accident.”

### seer
label unchanged. note NEW: Lines 367–395, from “What is that sound high in the air” to “Bringing rain.” Eliot’s note on the hooded hordes cites Hermann Hesse’s Blick ins Chaos.

### thunder
label unchanged. note NEW: “DA,” at lines 401, 411 and 418. Eliot’s note says the fable of the thunder’s meaning is in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad.

### fisherking
label unchanged. note NEW: Lines 424–426: “I sat upon the shore / Fishing, with the arid plain behind me.” Eliot’s note on line 425 refers to Weston’s chapter on the Fisher King; the poem does not name the speaker.

### fragments
label unchanged. note NEW: Lines 427–432, a line each: the nursery rhyme “London Bridge is falling down” (427); Dante’s Purgatorio, canto XXVI (428); the Latin Pervigilium Veneris (429); Nerval’s sonnet “El Desdichado” (430); the poem’s own line, “These fragments I have shored against my ruins” (431); and Kyd’s Spanish Tragedy (432). Eliot’s notes give the sources of all but the nursery rhyme.

### upanishad
label unchanged. note NEW: The poem’s last two lines, 433–434. Eliot’s notes translate the three words as “Give, sympathise, control” and give their source as the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, one of the Hindu scriptures; “Shantih,” repeated three times, is “a formal ending to an Upanishad,” for which “The Peace which passeth understanding” is, his note says, “a feeble translation.”

## timeline.json (what only; when unchanged)

### 1910–11
NEW: A year in Paris; friendship with Jean Verdenal, a French medical student. August 1911 in Munich, where 'Prufrock' is finished.

### 1915
NEW: Jean Verdenal killed at the Dardanelles in May. Eliot marries Vivien Haigh-Wood in June. 'The Death of Saint Narcissus' is set in type for Poetry (Chicago) and withdrawn; its opening lines are reworked in lines 25–30 of the poem.

### 1917
NEW: Joins Lloyds Bank’s Colonial and Foreign Department in the City, in March. Prufrock and Other Observations published, dedicated to Verdenal.

### 1918
NEW: 'Dans le Restaurant' published in the Little Review; its last stanza, in French, is the source of the ten lines of 'Death by Water' that Pound let stand.

### 1921, October
NEW: Lloyds grants three months' sick leave. Eliot goes to Margate in October and stays until 12 November; on 4 November he tells Sydney Schiff that he has written 'some fifty lines' of Part III, sitting in a shelter on the seafront.

### 1921, November
NEW: To Paris, where Vivien stays; then alone to Lausanne for treatment by Dr Roger Vittoz. Parts IV and V written there.

### 1922, January
NEW: Back through Paris. Pound goes over the typescript there and again by post, cutting it hard; Eliot accepts nearly all of it. Pound's verdict, in a letter of 24 January: 'Complimenti, you bitch.'

### 1922, September
NEW: Smyrna, the home city of Mr. Eugenides, 'the Smyrna merchant' of line 209, burns, 13–22 September, a month before the poem is published; the merchant was already in the typescript Pound edited in January.

### 1922, November
NEW: American publication in the November number of The Dial, again without notes. The Dial's award for 1922, $2,000, is announced in the December number.

### 1922, December
NEW: Boni & Liveright publish the first book edition in New York on 15 December, 1,000 copies, the first printing to carry the notes. Eliot said in 1956 that he had expanded them to make the book long enough.

### 1923, January
NEW: Eliot inscribes Pound's copy of the Boni & Liveright edition to him as il miglior fabbro, 'the better craftsman', Dante's words for the poet Arnaut Daniel in Purgatorio XXVI; the phrase becomes the printed dedication in 1925.

### 1933
NEW: Eliot records the poem at Columbia University, on aluminium discs: the earliest surviving reading. The discs were long dated 1935, and PennSound still labels them so; the Poetry Archive dates them 1933, following Lacy Rumsey and the work of Richard Swigg (2012) and Jason Camlot (2019).

### 1956
NEW: In a lecture at Minneapolis, 'The Frontiers of Criticism', Eliot calls the notes 'bogus scholarship' and regrets having sent readers on 'a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail'.

### 1968
NEW: The New York Public Library announces that it holds the typescript, long thought lost, with Pound's and Vivien Eliot's comments written on it. Eliot had sent it to John Quinn in 1922; after Quinn's death in 1924 it went unseen until the library bought it in 1958.

### 1971
NEW: Valerie Eliot, his widow, publishes a facsimile edition of the drafts, showing Pound's cuts and comments.

### 2015
NEW: Christopher Ricks and Jim McCue publish their annotated edition, The Poems of T. S. Eliot, in two volumes.
