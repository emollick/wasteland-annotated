# Batch C1: the editor's rewrites after round one

Both sides passed two records (baudelaire-au-lecteur; the reader also passed webster-devils-law-case, the scholar gotterdammerung) and flagged the rest for the same fault: the last sentence of a note stops telling the reader what is in the source and tells them what to think of it, in an epigram or a pair of antitheses; names are dropped as if the reader had the writer's shelf; the one-line "what" is a list or an untranslated quotation. The scholar found one error of fact (Ophelia's exit is not her last), one misquotation of Eliot (the Middleton phrase is not in his essay), one miscount ("four words" for five), and several loose claims: Weston's Syrian merchants, "Harvard senior", "dry stone" from Ezekiel, the dedication printed as if it were in the 1922 text, "great-hearted" for Dante's spiriti magni, "dry cistern" for a wheel broken at the cistern. Where the two sides differed the scholar's facts and the reader's wording are taken. Nearly every note now says what Eliot's note cites and words the critics' links as links.

Format: each record gives the fields that change, OLD and NEW. A field not listed stands.

## weston
what OLD: The title, the plan, the Fisher King, the Chapel Perilous, the Syrian merchants.
what NEW: The book Eliot's headnote credits with the title and the plan: the Fisher King, the waste land, the Chapel Perilous.
note OLD: (ends: "Almost no medievalist now accepts the argument. It does not matter. … read the rest and you have a very good bad book.")
note NEW: Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance who argued, following Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i>, that the Grail stories preserved the remains of an ancient fertility cult: a king whose wound lays his land waste, a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics, and a rite of initiation descended from the mystery cults of Attis and Mithra, which she says were carried across the Roman Empire by Syrian merchants, slaves and soldiers (chapter XII). Medievalists have not accepted the argument. Eliot's headnote says the book suggested "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem," and recommends it "to any who think such elucidation of the poem worth the trouble"; in 1956 he regretted "having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail." The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel).

## frazer
what OLD: The dying and reviving god; the buried corpse that sprouts; the Hanged God; the effigy thrown into the sea.
what NEW: The god who dies with the harvest and returns in spring: the Hanged God, the buried effigy, the image thrown into the sea.
note NEW: Frazer's twelve-volume study of magic and religion, which Eliot's headnote says "has influenced our generation profoundly," argued that the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris, and by implication of Christ, tell one story: a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by ritual, a king sacrificed, an effigy buried or drowned. The two volumes Eliot names describe the "gardens of Adonis," pots of quick-sprouting seed carried out with images of the dead god and "flung with them into the sea," and the effigies of Osiris "made of earth and corn" that the priests buried so that grain would sprout from the god's body. Eliot's note on the Tarot says the Hanged Man "is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer"; the corpse planted in Stetson's garden (line 71) and Phlebas in the current (line 312) have been read against the same rites. Frazer took his title and his opening page from Turner's painting <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1834), which he described as a view of Lake Nemi; it in fact shows Lake Avernus and the Sibyl of Cumae, the prophetess of the poem's epigraph, holding the bough that admits Aeneas to the underworld.

## petronius
note OLD: (ends: "A prophetess kept as a curiosity; a poem introduced by a fat man's boast.")
note NEW: The speaker is Trimalchio, a freed slave grown enormously rich, boasting to his guests between courses; his dinner party fills most of what survives of the <i>Satyricon</i>. The Sibyl of Cumae, in Ovid's telling (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV), asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand and forgot to ask for youth to go with them; she foresees that she will shrink until only her voice is left. The typescript of the poem carried a different epigraph, from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, ending "The horror! the horror!"; Pound doubted that Conrad was weighty enough for the place, and Eliot put Petronius in its stead before publication.

## dante-purg26
what OLD: "il miglior fabbro" (the dedication) and "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina" (line 428).
what NEW: The dedication to Pound, "il miglior fabbro," and line 428, Arnaut stepping back into the refining fire.
note NEW: On the seventh terrace of Purgatory, where the lustful walk in flame, Dante meets the poet Guido Guinizelli, who points out the troubadour Arnaut Daniel as "miglior fabbro del parlar materno," the better craftsman of the mother tongue (line 117). Arnaut answers in his own Provençal, the only passage in the <i>Comedy</i> in a vernacular other than Italian, and steps back into the fire. Eliot took the title of his 1920 collection <i>Ara Vos Prec</i> from line 145, and line 428 of the poem from line 148, which his note quotes with the three lines before it. The dedication "For Ezra Pound / il miglior fabbro" was not printed in 1922: Eliot wrote it into Pound's copy of the Boni & Liveright edition in January 1923, and it first appeared in print in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>. Modern texts read "li affina"; Eliot's "gli" follows the older text he used.

## dante-inf3
note NEW: Inside the gate ("Abandon all hope") but before the river Acheron, Dante finds the souls who were neither good nor bad, "who lived without infamy and without praise," rejected by Heaven and refused by Hell, running for ever after a banner and stung by wasps. Eliot's note on line 63 quotes lines 55–57 for the crowd over London Bridge, and his note on line 46 says that the "crowds of people" in Madame Sosostris's cards "appear later": by his own account the ring of line 56 and the crowd of line 62 are one crowd. Eliot said in 1950 that Dante's poetry was still, "after forty years," "the most persistent and deepest influence" on his own verse.

## dante-inf4
what OLD: "Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled."
what NEW: Limbo's sighs, behind "Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled."
note NEW: Limbo, the first circle of Hell, holds the virtuous who died unbaptised, Homer, Virgil and Aristotle among them, the "great spirits" (<i>spiriti magni</i>) who are not tormented but live in desire without hope. Eliot's note on line 64 cites these lines. The comparison puts the sighs of the office crowd with those of Limbo, where no one is punished, rather than with the cries of the damned.

## dante-purg5
what OLD: La Pia's "Siena mi fé, disfecemi Maremma" behind "Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me."
what NEW: La Pia's "Siena made me, Maremma unmade me," the model for "Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me."
note NEW: On the lower slopes of Purgatory, among those who repented only at a violent death, a woman speaks seven lines and gives her name, her birthplace, the place of her death and, without quite saying it, that her husband had her killed. Early commentators named her Pia de' Tolomei of Siena and her husband Nello dei Pannocchieschi; nothing certain is known of her. Eliot's note on line 293 cites the passage for the first Thames-daughter, whose "Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me" follows the shape of "Siena mi fé, disfecemi Maremma"; unlike La Pia, she gives no name.

## dante-inf33
trans: unchanged ("nailing up"); the note carries the point.
note NEW: Count Ugolino della Gherardesca of Pisa, imprisoned in 1288 with two sons and two grandsons and left to starve, gnaws in Hell on the skull of Archbishop Ruggieri, who betrayed him, and tells Dante the story. "Chiavar" has been read both as locking and as nailing up the door; Eliot's "key" takes it the first way. His note on line 412 quotes the passage and then a sentence from <i>Appearance and Reality</i> (1893) by F. H. Bradley, the philosopher on whom Eliot wrote his doctoral thesis, in which each person's experience "falls within my own circle, a circle closed on the outside."

## chaucer
what OLD: The kind April that Eliot's first line contradicts.
what NEW: Chaucer's April of sweet showers and pilgrimages, behind Eliot's "cruellest month."
note NEW: The Prologue opens with April rain ending the drought of March, sap rising, birds singing through the night and people longing to go on pilgrimage. Eliot's notes say nothing of Chaucer; readers have long set "April is the cruellest month" against these lines, in which the same month and the same rain are welcome, and in which no one says, as Eliot's people do, that winter kept them warm (lines 5–6).

## whitman-lilacs
what OLD: Lilacs, spring, death, and a hermit thrush.
what NEW: Whitman's elegy for Lincoln: lilacs, spring, and the hermit thrush.
note NEW: Whitman's elegy for Lincoln, who was shot in April 1865, brings together the lilac, the evening star and the hermit thrush singing in a swamp. Eliot's poem opens with lilacs bred out of the dead land (line 2) and at line 357 wants the hermit-thrush's "water-dripping song"; his note on that line describes the bird and quotes Chapman's <i>Handbook of Birds of Eastern North America</i>, and says nothing of Whitman. In 1928 Eliot wrote that he "did not read Whitman until much later in life, and had to conquer an aversion to his form, as well as to much of his matter, in order to do so." The case that the poem drew on Whitman all the same is made at length in S. Musgrove's <i>T. S. Eliot and Walt Whitman</i> (1952).

## ezekiel
note NEW: Ezekiel, prophet of the Babylonian exile, is addressed by God some ninety times as "son of man," that is, mortal man. Eliot's note on line 20 cites 2:1, where God first speaks to him. The "heap of broken images" of line 22 has been traced to chapter 6, where the idols of Israel are to be destroyed ("your images shall be broken"), and the dry bones of line 391 to chapter 37, where the bones are joined, clothed with flesh and made to live.

## ecclesiastes
note NEW: Eliot's note on line 23 cites verse 5, where "the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail"; the poem has a cricket in the grasshopper's place. The chapter is an allegory of old age and death, and its pitcher broken at the fountain and wheel broken at the cistern have been heard again in the "empty cisterns and exhausted wells" of line 385.

## isaiah
note NEW: Eliot's notes do not cite Isaiah. The "shadow of a great rock in a weary land" of chapter 32 has been recognised behind the "shadow under this red rock" of line 25 since the earliest commentaries; in Isaiah the shadow comes with "rivers of water in a dry place," a promise, and the red rock gives shade but no water. In chapter 38 King Hezekiah, told to set his house in order because he will die, turns his face to the wall and weeps, and is granted fifteen more years. At line 426 the speaker, fishing on the shore, asks "Shall I at least set my lands in order?"; Eliot's note on the line before refers him to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King, and the poem gives no answer.

## luke24
note NEW: Eliot's headnote to Part V names "the journey to Emmaus" as the first of its three themes. On the day of the resurrection two disciples walk from Jerusalem to the village of Emmaus, and a stranger joins them on the road; they know him as Jesus only when he breaks bread at supper, and at that moment he vanishes. Eliot's lines take the moment on the road, before the recognition. His note on line 360 adds a second source for the figure who walks beside the travellers: an account of an Antarctic expedition, "I think one of Shackleton's," in which the exhausted party "had the constant delusion that there was one more member than could actually be counted."

## tristan
what OLD: The sailor's song (lines 31–34) and the shepherd's "Öd und leer das Meer" (line 42).
what NEW: The sailor's song (lines 31–34) and the shepherd's "Öd und leer das Meer," desolate and empty the sea (line 42).
note NEW: Eliot's notes cite the libretto by act and verse: I, verses 5–8, and III, verse 24. The young sailor's song is the first thing sung in the opera, from the masthead, unseen, above the deck where Isolde lies. The shepherd's "Öd und leer das Meer!" comes in Act III, answering Tristan's servant Kurwenal, who has asked whether any ship is in sight while Tristan lies wounded and waiting for Isolde. In the poem the hyacinth garden (lines 35–41) falls between the two quotations. Eliot heard <i>Tristan</i>, or its music in concert, in Boston in the autumn of 1909, at the start of his graduate year at Harvard, and wrote a poem called "Opera," dated November 1909, about it; Stravinsky, who met him in 1956, thought <i>Tristan</i> "must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life." Ludwig II of Bavaria, whose money paid for the first performance, was found dead in 1886 in the Starnberger See, the lake of line 8.

## gotterdammerung
note NEW: <i>Götterdämmerung</i> is the last of the four operas of Wagner's <i>Ring</i>. Its third act opens with the three Rhine-daughters swimming and singing for the gold that the dwarf Alberich stole from them at the start of the first opera, and asking the hero Siegfried, when he appears, to give back the ring made from it. Their refrain is the "la la" that Eliot's Thames-daughters sing over oil and tar. Eliot's note on line 266 reads: "The Song of the (three) Thames-daughters begins here. . . . V. <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, III, i: the Rhine-daughters."

## baudelaire-sept-vieillards
what OLD: "Unreal City."
what NEW: "Unreal City": the swarming Paris of "The Seven Old Men."
note NEW: The poet, walking through a dirty yellow fog on a Paris morning, meets a hideous old man, then another exactly like him, and another, seven in all, and flees home half mad. Eliot's note on line 60 quotes the first two lines. The dead acquaintance hailed on King William Street at nine in the morning has been read as Eliot's version of Baudelaire's daylight ghost.

## webster-white-devil
note NEW: Webster's tragedy is about Vittoria Corombona, the "white devil" of the title, and her lover Brachiano, who has her husband and his own wife murdered. Cornelia sings the dirge over her son Marcello, killed by his brother Flamineo; Flamineo makes the speech about widows in the last scene, rising from a feigned death after his sister Vittoria has fired what she took to be a loaded pistol at him. Eliot's notes cite the dirge at line 74 and Flamineo's speech at line 408. At line 74 the poem turns Webster's wolf, "foe to men," into a Dog, "friend to men." Eliot's "Whispers of Immortality" (1918) begins "Webster was much possessed by death / And saw the skull beneath the skin."

## webster-devils-law-case
note NEW: Act III scene ii. Romelio, disguised as a Jewish physician, stabs the wounded Contarino in his bed to finish him off; the two surgeons catch him at it, and he buys their silence. When he has gone the patient groans, and one surgeon asks the other whether "the wind" is "in that door still," that is, whether that is how things stand, whether he is still alive. The stab has drained the old wound, and Contarino recovers. Eliot's note quotes the line with "Webster" for its source and does not name the play.

## shakespeare-tempest
what OLD: Ariel's song, "Those are pearls that were his eyes"; Ferdinand on the bank; "This music crept by me upon the waters."
what NEW: Ariel's song ("Those are pearls that were his eyes") and Ferdinand, weeping on the bank for his father's wreck, hearing "This music crept by me upon the waters."
note NEW: Ferdinand, washed ashore after the shipwreck, believes his father Alonso drowned; the song Ariel sings to him, unseen, describes a drowned man turned to coral and pearl. The song is not true: Alonso is alive, and the play ends with father and son reunited. Eliot's notes cite the scene at lines 192 and 257, his note on line 126 refers the pearls back to lines 37 and 48, and his note on Tiresias says the Phoenician Sailor "is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples." In the poem the drowned stay drowned: the Sailor of line 47 and Phlebas in Part IV. Ferdinand's "the king my father's wreck" becomes "the king my brother's wreck" and "the king my father's death" for the fisherman on the canal bank behind the gashouse (lines 191–192).

## shakespeare-antony
note NEW: Antony's officer Enobarbus describes Cleopatra's first appearance to Antony, in her barge on the river Cydnus; Eliot's note on line 77 cites the speech. The opening of Part II follows its manner, blank verse and an inventory of gold, perfume and light, but moves the scene indoors, to a woman at her dressing table. The barge of Elizabeth and Leicester at line 279, taken from Froude, has been read alongside this one.

## shakespeare-hamlet
note NEW: Ophelia, mad after her father's death, leaves the stage with these words; she comes back once more in the same scene, with flowers, and her drowning is reported by Gertrude in scene vii. Eliot's notes say nothing of the line, which follows the pub's "Goonight Bill. Goonight Lou. Goonight May. Goonight." without a break and ends Part II.

## virgil-aeneid1
note NEW: Eliot's note on line 92 quotes lines 726–727, from the banquet at which Dido, queen of Carthage, falls in love with Aeneas; he will sail away, and she will kill herself on a pyre. In the poem the word names the ceiling of the room in Part II. Carthage returns at line 307, this time from Augustine's <i>Confessions</i>.

## milton-pl4
note NEW: Eliot's note on line 98 cites Book IV, line 140. Satan, arriving at Eden, looks in at Paradise from outside, the trees rising rank above rank into "a woody theatre"; "silvan scene" (the poem spells it "sylvan") and "theatre" are Milton's words for the view. In the poem the phrase describes the painting over the mantel, which shows the change of Philomel.

## ovid-met6
note NEW: Tereus, king of Thrace, is bringing his wife Procne's sister Philomela to visit her when he rapes her in a hut deep in the woods and cuts out her tongue; she weaves the crime into a cloth and sends it to her sister; Procne kills her son Itys and serves him to his father; Tereus, pursuing the sisters with a sword, and the sisters, fleeing, are all turned into birds. Ovid does not say which sister became the nightingale and which the swallow; the later tradition that Eliot follows makes Philomela the nightingale. Eliot's note on line 99 cites the story for the picture over the mantel in Part II; the "Jug Jug" of lines 103 and 204 is the nightingale's song as Elizabethan poets wrote it; and his note on line 429 refers the swallow back to "Philomela in Parts II and III." Arthur Golding's translation of 1567, the one Shakespeare knew, gives the story in Book VI.

## ovid-met3
what OLD: The man who had been a woman, and who sees the future.
what NEW: Tiresias, who lived seven years as a woman and was then struck blind and given prophecy.
note NEW: Book III, lines 324–331 of the episode (320–338) that Eliot's note on line 218 quotes in Latin, calling it "of great anthropological interest." Jupiter and Juno, disputing in jest which sex takes more pleasure in love, put the question to Tiresias, who has been both; he sides with Jupiter, Juno strikes him blind, and Jupiter gives him foresight in return. Eliot's note calls Tiresias "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest," and says that "what Tiresias sees, in fact, is the substance of the poem."

## middleton-wbw
note NEW: Livia, a rich widow acting as the Duke of Florence's procuress, keeps Bianca's mother-in-law at chess while the Duke takes Bianca in the gallery above; her remarks on the game double as commentary on what is happening upstairs. Eliot's note on line 137 cites "the game of chess in Middleton's <i>Women beware Women</i>"; the title of Part II has also been connected with Middleton's <i>A Game at Chess</i> (1624), an anti-Spanish satire that ran for nine days at the Globe before the Privy Council stopped it. Eliot's essay on Middleton (1927) calls him "a great observer of human nature, without fear, without sentiment, without prejudice."

## Unchanged
baudelaire-au-lecteur.

## Noted
- dante-inf33: the translation keeps "nailing up," the commoner rendering; the note now says that Eliot's "key" reads the verb the other way. The scholar offered "locking" and left the choice to the reader, who preferred the note to carry it.
- tristan: the scholar could confirm Stravinsky's sentence only at second hand (the T. S. Eliot Society newsletter citing Esquire, August 1965); the wording matches the site's and stands. Whether Eliot heard the opera staged or in concert in 1909 is not settled, and the note now says "or its music in concert."
- shakespeare-hamlet: the Millais sentence is cut; the painting is credited on the About page where the site shows it.
