### spenser-prothalamion
note: A “spousal verse,” as its title page calls it, for the double wedding of the Earl of Worcester’s daughters, Elizabeth and Katherine Somerset. The poet, out of favour at court, walks by the Thames and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans, who stand for the brides. Eliot’s note to line 176 cites the poem. He keeps Spenser’s refrain; his nymphs are twice said to have departed (lines 175 and 179), and his river carries nothing, not even the litter of summer nights. Spenser’s poet is himself unhappy, walking by the river to ease the “sullein care” of a fruitless wait at court.

### psalm137
what: “By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept.”
note: The psalm of the Jewish exiles in Babylon, whose captors ask them for a song, and who ask in return how they can sing the Lord’s song in a strange land. Eliot finished the poem at Lausanne, on Lake Geneva (in French, Lac Léman), in November and December 1921, on three months’ leave from Lloyds Bank; he takes the psalm’s first line and puts the lake in place of the rivers of Babylon. “Leman” is also an old English word for a lover, and the double sense has often been noticed.

### marvell-coy-mistress
what: “But at my back I always hear / Time’s wingèd chariot hurrying near.”
note: Marvell’s speaker urges his mistress not to wait: time is short, and in the grave there is no embracing. Eliot’s note cites the poem. Twice he begins a line with Marvell’s “But at my back,” and each time puts another sound where the chariot was: at lines 185–186 “The rattle of the bones,” at lines 196–198 “The sound of horns and motors,” which brings Sweeney to Mrs Porter. Eliot’s essay “Andrew Marvell,” in the Times Literary Supplement of 31 March 1921, while he was at work on the poem, praised in Marvell “a tough reasonableness beneath the slight lyric grace.”

### day-parliament-of-bees
what: “A noise of horns and hunting” that brings Actaeon to Diana.
note: Twelve “characters” of good and bad men, told as bees. The lines Eliot’s note quotes are spoken by Polypragmus, “a mere vainglorious reveller,” describing the shows he means to stage in his hall: a noise of horns and hunting is to bring Actaeon to Diana “in the spring,” where all shall see her naked, and Actaeon’s hounds are then to tear their master, as in Ovid’s <i>Metamorphoses</i>, where the hunter who sees Diana bathing is turned into a stag and killed by his own dogs. Eliot keeps the shape of Day’s sentence and puts “horns and motors” for the hunt, and Sweeney and Mrs Porter for Actaeon and Diana.

### verlaine-parsifal
what: “And, oh, those children’s voices singing in the dome!”: the last line of a sonnet on Wagner’s <i>Parsifal</i>.
trans: Parsifal has overcome the Maidens, their pretty chatter and amusing lust, and his own leaning toward the Flesh, a virgin boy’s, which all that tempts to love light breasts and that pretty chatter; he has overcome the beautiful Woman with the subtle heart, spreading her cool arms and her exciting bosom; he has overcome Hell and returns to his tent with a heavy trophy on his boyish arm, with the lance that pierced the supreme Side! He has healed the king, and here he is king himself, and priest of the most holy essential Treasure. In a robe of gold he adores, glory and symbol, the pure vessel where the real Blood shines. And, oh, those children’s voices singing in the dome!
note: Verlaine’s sonnet on Wagner’s last opera, written for the <i>Revue wagnérienne</i>, a Paris journal devoted to Wagner (January 1886), gives the plot in thirteen lines: the flower-maidens, Kundry, the recovered spear, the healed king. The fourteenth line is the boys’ choir in the dome of the Grail temple. Eliot’s note cites the sonnet. In the poem the line comes straight after the ballad about Mrs Porter, which Eliot’s note says was “reported to me from Sydney, Australia.” In Wagner, as in the romances Jessie Weston discusses in <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, the book Eliot’s headnote cites, the knight heals the wounded king; the poem’s Fisher King is still fishing at line 425, “with the arid plain behind me.”

### lyly-campaspe
note: The Elizabethan convention that the nightingale sings “jug jug” and cries “tereu,” calling on Tereus, the king who raped Philomela, is at its plainest in this song from Lyly’s court comedy. The songs in Lyly’s plays were first printed in the collected edition of 1632, and whether he wrote them is disputed. Eliot does not cite Lyly; “Jug Jug” at line 103 and the birdsong at lines 203–206 use the convention, with “So rudely forc’d” set between the notes.

### sappho-104
what: Two lines to the evening star, which brings the sheep, the goat and the child home.
note: Two lines that survive because Demetrius, an ancient Greek critic, quoted them in his treatise <i>On Style</i> (section 141), for the charm of their repeated “you bring.” Eliot’s note says his lines “may not appear as exact as Sappho’s,” and that he had in mind the “longshore” or “dory” fisherman “who returns at nightfall.” Byron imitated the lines in <i>Don Juan</i> (Canto III, stanza 107), and Stevenson’s “Requiem” has “Home is the sailor, home from sea”; both have been cited as the English between Sappho and Eliot’s lines 220–221.

### goldsmith-vicar
note: Olivia, the Vicar’s daughter, seduced and abandoned by Squire Thornhill, sings this at her mother’s request, the family sitting on the bank where she first met him. Goldsmith’s song says the only thing left to a betrayed woman is to die. Eliot’s note cites the song. He takes its first line and adds “and” to fill out his pentameter (Goldsmith’s is a four-beat line); the typist then “smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone.” The scene has been read as contempt for her, and also as the reverse: she does not die, as the song says a fallen woman must.

### froude-elizabeth
note: Froude quotes a dispatch of 30 June 1561 from the Spanish ambassador, Álvaro de la Quadra, to King Philip II, describing an afternoon on the Thames that June. “Lord Robert” is Robert Dudley, not yet Earl of Leicester; his wife, Amy Robsart, had died the previous September in a fall at Cumnor Place, and the talk of marriage came to nothing. Eliot’s note cites Froude for lines 279–289, the barge with its “gilded shell / Red and gold.”

### augustine-confessions
note: Augustine’s account, in Book III, of his student years at Carthage, “a cauldron of unholy loves,” and, in Book X, of the pull that beautiful things still have on a man trying to keep his eyes on God. In the Latin, <i>sartago</i>, a cauldron or frying pan, plays on <i>Carthago</i>. Eliot’s words are Pusey’s translation of 1838, with the first sentence rearranged in his note as “to Carthage then I came, where a cauldron of unholy loves sang all about mine ears.” His second Augustine note calls the pairing of Augustine and the Buddha, “these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism,” “not an accident.”

### buddha-fire-sermon
note: Preached, in the account Warren translates from the Mahāvagga, to a thousand former fire-worshipping ascetics on Gayā Head, a hill near Gaya in Bihar, soon after the Buddha’s enlightenment. Eliot studied Sanskrit and Indian philosophy at Harvard in 1911–13 and knew Warren’s <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896). His note says the sermon “corresponds in importance to the Sermon on the Mount,” sends the reader to Warren for the complete text, and calls Warren “one of the great pioneers of Buddhist studies in the Occident.” Part III takes its title from the sermon, and line 308 its word, “Burning.”

### brihadaranyaka
note: Eliot read the Upanishads in Sanskrit at Harvard with Charles Lanman in 1911–13. His note gives the reference as Brihadaranyaka 5, 1 (the passage is 5.2 in the standard numbering) and sends the reader to Paul Deussen’s German translation of 1897. In the fable each group hears in the one syllable the command it needs. Eliot puts the three commands in a different order, glosses them in his note as “Give, sympathise, control,” and follows each with a passage in which the speaker answers it. “Shantih,” his last note says, is “a formal ending to an Upanishad,” and “The Peace which passeth understanding” is “a feeble translation of the content of this word”; later editions soften this to “our equivalent to this word.”

### bradley
note: The Oxford philosopher on whom Eliot wrote his Harvard doctoral dissertation (finished 1916, never defended, published in 1964). Eliot’s note on “I have heard the key” first quotes Dante’s Ugolino, who hears the door of the tower locked below him (<i>Inferno</i> XXXIII), and then adds this passage from page 346 of Bradley’s <i>Appearance and Reality</i>. The two together gloss “each in his prison,” the lines that follow the thunder’s “Dayadhvam,” sympathise.

### hesse-blick-ins-chaos
what: The essay behind the “hooded hordes” and what Eliot’s note on Part V calls “the present decay of eastern Europe.”
note: Three essays written in the aftermath of the First World War, two of them on Dostoevsky, in which Hesse saw the “Russian man” of <i>The Brothers Karamazov</i> as the coming type of a Europe that had lost faith in its own order. Eliot’s note quotes the essay “The Brothers Karamazov, or the Downfall of Europe,” misprinting “in heiligem Wahn” as “im heiligem Wahn.” He visited Hesse at Montagnola in Switzerland in May 1922 and printed him in the first number of the <i>Criterion</i>, the magazine he founded that year.
(The note's content is the record's own; only the visit's place and month and the gloss on the Criterion are added.)

### shackleton-south
what: The crossing of South Georgia, when three exhausted men felt “that we were four, not three.”
note: In May 1916, after the <i>Endurance</i> had been crushed in the ice and the party had come through the open-boat voyage across the Southern Ocean, Shackleton and two of his men, Worsley and Crean, crossed the mountains of South Georgia on foot to reach the whaling station at Stromness. Eliot’s note says he had forgotten which expedition it was, “but I think one of Shackleton’s,” and remembers the party’s “constant delusion that there was one more member than could actually be counted”; Shackleton’s own words are that “it seemed to me often that we were four, not three.” Eliot’s headnote to Part V names the journey to Emmaus, in which two disciples walking from Jerusalem after the crucifixion are joined by a third whom they do not recognise as the risen Christ, as one of the section’s three themes; the lines beginning at 360 ask who the third is.

### chapman-birds
note: The field guide Eliot had as a boy, a fourteenth-birthday present from his mother in 1902; his copy survives, with his later inscription calling it “a much coveted birthday present.” Eliot’s note quotes the Handbook’s entry (its descriptive paragraphs are signed by Eugene P. Bicknell, one of Chapman’s contributors), gives the bird the scientific name of the day, <i>Turdus aonalaschkae pallasii</i> (now <i>Catharus guttatus</i>), and says he had heard it “in Quebec County.” The phrase “water-dripping song,” which the note puts in quotation marks, is not in Chapman’s entry.

### pervigilium-veneris
note: A Latin poem of ninety-three lines, written some time between the second and fifth centuries AD, most likely the fourth, for the eve of the spring festival of Venus; its refrain is “cras amet qui numquam amavit,” tomorrow let him love who has never loved. After the flowers and the nymphs, the last stanza turns to the poet’s own silence and envies the swallow, who is Procne, sister of the raped Philomela. Eliot’s note cites the poem and adds “Cf. Philomela in Parts II and III.” His 1922 text reads “ceu” where the poem has “uti,” and so do his manuscripts; the line was changed to “uti” in <i>Collected Poems 1909–1935</i> (1936).

### nerval-desdichado
what: “Le Prince d’Aquitaine à la tour abolie”: the Prince of Aquitaine with the ruined tower.
note: The first sonnet of Nerval’s <i>Chimères</i>, first published in December 1853, in the last years of his illness; he hanged himself in a Paris street on 26 January 1855. The title is usually traced to the shield of the Disinherited Knight in Walter Scott’s <i>Ivanhoe</i>; the Spanish word means “unfortunate” rather than “disinherited.” The “tour abolie” is often read as the Tower of the Tarot. In the last lines the poet says he has twice crossed Acheron, the river of the dead, and come back, and still plays Orpheus’s lyre. Eliot’s note cites the sonnet; he quotes its second line only.

### kyd-spanish-tragedy
note: Hieronimo, Knight Marshal of Spain, whose son Horatio has been murdered and hanged in an arbour, is asked by the murderers to write a play for the court. “I’ll fit you” means two things at once: I’ll give you what you want, and I’ll pay you back. The play he supplies is written, as the early quartos’ note to the reader says, “in sundry languages,” and in performing it the murderers are stabbed for real. “Hieronimo is mad againe” is from the title page of the 1615 quarto and of the reprints that followed it. Eliot’s note cites the play.

### london-bridge
note: The rhyme runs through building materials that fail until a watchman is set. Folklorists of James Frazer’s generation, Alice Gomme among them, connected it with the belief that a bridge was once made to stand by burying a victim in its foundations; Iona and Peter Opie, in <i>The Oxford Dictionary of Nursery Rhymes</i>, treat the connection with caution. Eliot’s crowd crossed the real London Bridge at line 62. Here the rhyme opens the run of quotations at lines 427–430, the “fragments” of line 431, and is the only one of the four in English.

### shakespearian-rag
note: Published in 1912 and advertised as a number from that year’s Ziegfeld Follies, the Broadway revue. Matthew Guerrieri’s search of the trade paper <i>Variety</i> (2017) found no sign that it was sung in the show; the first performance he could find was in vaudeville, at the Columbia Theatre, New York, on 5 January 1913. The lyric has Shakespeare’s characters dancing the ragtime steps of the day. Eliot’s “Shakespeherian,” with its extra syllable, is generally read as deliberate, giving the word the rag’s syncopation; the poem also reverses the song’s “intelligent … elegant.” No recording from the period is known.

### dans-le-restaurant
note: One of the four poems in French in Eliot’s <i>Poems</i> (1920). A waiter tells a customer about sheltering under willows in the rain with a little girl when he was seven, and being interrupted by a dog; the customer, disgusted, gives him ten sous for the bath-house. The last stanza, with no transition, is about the drowned Phoenician. Eliot’s English version of it in Part IV drops Cornwall and the cargo of tin and adds “Gentile or Jew.” In the draft these lines closed a long narrative of a shipwreck; Pound cut the narrative and insisted that Phlebas stay.

### death-of-st-narcissus
note: Set in type for <i>Poetry</i> (Chicago) in 1915 and withdrawn in proof; first published in 1950. The poem is about a saint who is sure he had been a tree, a fish and a young girl, and who dies pierced with arrows. The manuscript survived among the papers Eliot sent in 1922 to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector, with the <i>Waste Land</i> drafts, and is now in the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library. After these two lines the speaker promises to show the listener something other than his own shadow on the sand or against the fire. <i>The Waste Land</i> keeps the second line, with “gray” changed to “red,” and reshapes the first. The poem is still in copyright, so only its first two lines are given here.

### dickens-omf
passage: “And I do love a newspaper. You mightn’t think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices.”
note: Betty Higden, a poor old woman in the novel, is praising Sloppy, the foundling she has brought up, who reads her the newspaper and does all the voices in the police-court reports. “He Do the Police in Different Voices” is typed as a title at the head of the typescripts of Parts I and II in the drafts; it did not survive to publication. The novel opens on the Thames, with Gaffer Hexam and his daughter in a boat searching the water for drowned bodies.

### conrad-hod
what: The epigraph of the typescript, replaced by Petronius.
note: The last words of Kurtz, the trader whom the narrator, Marlow, has gone up the river to find, stood at the head of the typescript of Part I as the poem’s epigraph. Pound wrote that he doubted whether Conrad was “weighty enough to stand the citation”; Eliot answered that it was “much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative”; Pound withdrew the objection, and Eliot nevertheless replaced it with the passage from Petronius that stands there now. “The heart of light” at line 41 has been read as an inversion of Conrad’s title. The story is told on a yawl at anchor in the Thames estuary at dusk, with London upriver.

### tennyson
note: Eliot cites neither poem. The mad speaker of <i>Maud</i>, who imagines himself buried under a city street with the traffic going over him, has been cited for “a handful of dust” (line 30), and the passage has been compared with the “rats’ alley” of Part II. The Prince’s song in <i>The Princess</i>, with Swinburne’s “Itylus” (“Swallow, my sister, O sister swallow”), has been cited for “O swallow swallow” at line 429, the English half of a line whose Latin half is from the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i>. In 1936 Eliot wrote that Tennyson “had the finest ear of any English poet since Milton.”

### huxley-crome-yellow
note: Huxley’s country-house satire was published in November 1921. Its Mr. Scogan, a cynical guest who dresses up as an old gypsy woman to tell fortunes at the village fête under the name “Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana,” is the accepted source of Madame Sosostris: asked by Grover Smith, Eliot wrote to him on 10 March 1952 that he was “almost certain” he had taken the name from <i>Crome Yellow</i> without being conscious of doing so. Huxley and Eliot had known each other since the war years through Lady Ottoline Morrell’s circle at Garsington, her house near Oxford.

### eliot-frontiers
passage: “bogus scholarship” … “a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail”
note: A lecture given at the University of Minnesota on 30 April 1956, to an audience of about fourteen thousand, and printed in <i>On Poetry and Poets</i> (1957). Thirty-four years on, Eliot said he had meant only to list his references, with a view, in his words, to “spiking the guns” of critics who had called him a plagiarist; the poem’s first printings, in the <i>Criterion</i> and the <i>Dial</i>, had no notes; when Boni &amp; Liveright were to make it a book it was found too short, and he expanded the notes to fill the pages, with the result he called “bogus scholarship.” He regretted sending readers on “a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail,” and said the notes could not now be removed: a reader who bought his poems and found them missing would ask for his money back.
