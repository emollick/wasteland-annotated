# The brief for both sides

## What this is

An annotated edition of T. S. Eliot's *The Waste Land* is live at https://the-waste-land.netlify.app. The poem is the interface; the commentary sits beside it. The owner, Ethan, read it and said:

> really interesting but you have a major language issue in the work, which is that all of the commentary is written in the voice of an LLM "Where the poem is standing" and "The poem's argument is a weather report: rain that is unwelcome, rivers that are dirty, a sea that drowns, and then twenty-nine lines of rock before three words of rain." set up Eliot scholar agents and Reader agents to debate every section to make sure it is clear, good in its wording, and real

Those two examples are the standard for what has to go. The first is the map page's title and the Places lens's description. The second is the Water lens's own introduction.

The prose in the batch you are given is the site's own words, not the line-by-line commentary on the poem (five other threads are doing that). It is judged the same way.

## What "the voice of an LLM" means here

- Tour-guide framing headings and labels: "Where the poem is standing".
- The aphoristic "X is a Y:" opening followed by a rhythmic list of three or four: "The poem's argument is a weather report: rain that is unwelcome, rivers that are dirty, a sea that drowns".
- The tidy flourish that counts things to sound clever: "twenty-nine lines of rock before three words of rain".
- Metaphors that describe the poem instead of explaining it: "The section's weather is its argument", "the poem's hydrology".
- The last sentence that ties a bow, the epigram at the end of a note: "Some lines are just lines", "It does not matter.", "a very good bad book", "The lilacs and the thrush say otherwise."
- The knowing aside, the wink: "which is the only kind of one-eyed man a printed card can have".
- Paired antitheses for rhythm: "not a key to the poem, but a map of where he was standing"; "They are not summaries; they are routes."
- On the site's own pages, copy that sells the site or the poem rather than telling the reader what is there and how it works.
- Fragments stacked for effect. Colons doing the work of verbs. Sentences that end on a twist.

## What to aim at instead

The plainness of a good scholarly editor writing for readers: Christopher Ricks and Jim McCue's commentary in *The Poems of T. S. Eliot* (2015), B. C. Southam's *Guide to the Selected Poems*, Lawrence Rainey's *Annotated Waste Land*. For the site's own copy (what a page is, how a tool works, what a lens shows), the tone of a well-made book's front matter: a note on the text, a list of contents, an editor's preface. It says what is there. It does not perform.

A label may be one or two words ("Places", "The map", "Notes"). Short sentences are fine. Fragments and stacked clauses are not. A fact is stated once, with its source where a reader would want it. A reading is worded as a reading ("has been read as", "Eliot's note links", "perhaps"), never passed off as a fact. Nothing ends on a flourish. If a sentence could be cut without losing a fact or an instruction, cut it.

Not everything in the batch is bad. A good many records are already plain and right. Say so, briefly, and move on. Do not rewrite for the sake of rewriting; every change should be one you could defend to Ethan in a sentence.

## Facts and rights

- Line numbers are the site's own count, 1 to 434, counting every line of the poem. Eliot's 1922 marginal numbers run one behind from line 346 (his 433 is the site's 434).
- The text is the Boni & Liveright first edition of December 1922, with Eliot's notes.
- Rights: the 1922 poem and notes are public domain in the US and may be quoted freely, as may any source published before 1930. Eliot's texts after 1929 (the draft passages in the 1971 facsimile, *The Death of Saint Narcissus* in full, the 1956 lecture "The Frontiers of Criticism", his letters) may be described, or quoted in a phrase or two, never a passage. Pound's marginalia and letters as already quoted on the site are short phrases and stay within that.
- Every fact on the site was checked against primary sources on 20 September 2026. A change to a fact needs a source.

## The record format you will see

Files are records separated by `---`, with `key: value` lines; a value may run on over several lines. Some fields are fixed (ids, line numbers, anchors, keys) and must not change; the batch file says which are editable. `<i>…</i>` for titles and foreign words is allowed; no Markdown. In listen.txt, `{link:ID|text}` and `{parts}` are placeholders and stay.

## How the debate runs

Round 1: the scholar and the reader each read the whole batch and report. Round 2: the editor rewrites what either side flagged and both read the rewrites again. Round 3 only for what is still disputed. Three rounds at most per piece. On substance the scholar has the last word; on wording the reader. What is not settled is written down as such.
