title: Prothalamion
author: Edmund Spenser
date: 1596
lang: English
kind: poem
what: "Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song."
lines: 176, 183, 184
passage: Calme was the day, and through the trembling ayre<br>Sweete breathing Zephyrus did softly play<br>A gentle spirit, that lightly did delay<br>Hot Titans beames, which then did glyster fayre:<br>When I whom sullein care,<br>Through discontent of my long fruitlesse stay<br>In Princes Court, and expectation vayne<br>Of idle hopes, which still doe fly away,<br>Like empty shaddowes, did aflict my brayne,<br>Walkt forth to ease my payne<br>Along the shoare of silver streaming Themmes,<br>Whose rutty Bancke, the which his River hemmes,<br>Was paynted all with variable flowers,<br>And all the meades adornd with daintie gemmes,<br>Fit to decke maydens bowres,<br>And crowne their Paramours,<br>Against the Brydale day, which is not long:<br>Sweete Themmes runne softly, till I end my Song.
note:
A "spousal verse" for the double wedding of the Earl of Worcester's daughters, in which the poet, out of favour at court, walks by the Thames and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans who are the brides. Eliot keeps the refrain and empties the river. Notice that Spenser's poet is also unhappy, also walking off his "sullein care" by the river, before the wedding cheers him up.

trans: Parsifal has overcome the Maidens, their pretty chatter and amusing lust, and his own leaning, a virgin boy's flesh tempted to love light breasts and that pretty chatter; he has overcome the beautiful Woman with the subtle heart, spreading her cool arms and her exciting throat; he has overcome Hell and returns to his tent with a heavy trophy on his boyish arm, with the lance that pierced the supreme Side! Verlaine's sonnet on Wagner's last opera, written for the <i>Revue wagnérienne</i>, compresses the whole plot into thirteen lines and then, in the fourteenth, simply reports the sound of the boys' choir in the Grail temple. Eliot places that line after a barrack-room song about a brothel, so that the temptation and the temple stand one line apart. Parsifal is Weston's Grail knight; his healing of the king is the thing the poem never manages.

id: lyly-campaspe
title: Campaspe (the nightingale song)
author: John Lyly
date: 1584
lang: English
kind: play
what: "Jug jug" and "Tereu."
lines: 103, 204, 206
passage: What bird so sings, yet so does wail?<br>O ’tis the ravish’d nightingale.<br>Jug, jug, jug, jug, tereu! she cries,<br>And still her woes at midnight rise.
note:
The Elizabethan convention that the nightingale sings "jug jug" and calls "tereu," the vocative of Tereus, is at its plainest in this song from Lyly's court comedy; the songs in Lyly's plays were first printed in the collected edition of 1632, and whether he wrote them is disputed. Eliot's "Jug Jug to dirty ears" and the four-line burst of birdsong at lines 203–206 are Lyly's bird, with the pretty convention turned back into what it describes.

id: buddha-fire-sermon
title: The Fire Sermon (Ādittapariyāya Sutta)
author: The Buddha, translated by Henry Clarke Warren
date: 5th century BC; translation 1896
lang: Pali; English
kind: scripture
what: The title of Part III and "Burning burning burning burning."
lines: 308
passage: All things, O priests, are on fire. And what, O priests, are all these things which are on fire? The eye, O priests, is on fire; forms are on fire; eye-consciousness is on fire; impressions received by the eye are on fire; and whatever sensation, pleasant, unpleasant, or indifferent, originates in dependence on impressions received by the eye, that also is on fire. It is the text behind the whole third section, in which everything, from the river to the typist, is seen as burning.

id: brihadaranyaka
title: Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 5.2: what the thunder says
author: Anonymous (Vedic)
date: c. 700 BC
lang: Sanskrit
kind: scripture
what: "DA. Datta. Dayadhvam. Damyata." and "Shantih."
lines: 400, 402, 411, 418, 433, 434
passage: The threefold offspring of Prajāpati, gods, men and demons, lived with their father as students. When they had finished, the gods said: Teach us. He spoke one syllable, "Da," and asked: Did you understand? They said: We understood; you told us, dāmyata, restrain yourselves. Then the men: Teach us. "Da." We understood; you told us, datta, give. Then the demons: Teach us. "Da." We understood; you told us, dayadhvam, be compassionate. This is what the divine voice, the thunder, repeats: da, da, da: restrain yourselves, give, be compassionate. note:
Eliot read the Upanishads in Sanskrit at Harvard with Charles Lanman in 1911–13, and his note cites Paul Deussen's German translation of 1897. The fable is about hearing what you need to hear in a single syllable. Eliot reorders the three commands and gives each a confession. "Shantih," the peace that closes an Upanishad, is spoken three times at the end of a recitation; the poem ends the way a Vedic text ends, and the notes end by calling the English "a feeble translation."

id: pervigilium-veneris
title: Pervigilium Veneris (The Vigil of Venus)
author: Anonymous
date: 2nd–4th century AD
lang: Latin
kind: poem
what: "Quando fiam ceu chelidon": when shall I be like the swallow?
lines: 429
passage: iam loquaces ore rauco stagna cycni perstrepunt;<br>adsonat Terei puella subter umbram populi,<br>ut putes motus amoris ore dici musico,<br>et neges queri sororem de marito barbaro.<br>illa cantat, nos tacemus. quando ver venit meum?<br>quando fiam uti chelidon, ut tacere desinam?<br>perdidi Musam tacendo, nec me Phoebus respicit.<br>sic Amyclas, cum tacerent, perdidit silentium.<br>cras amet qui numquam amavit quique amavit cras amet.
trans: Now the chattering swans with hoarse voices make the pools ring; Tereus's girl sings under the poplar's shade, so that you would think the stirrings of love were spoken by her musical mouth, and not that a sister complained of a barbarous husband. She sings; we are silent. When does my spring come? When shall I become like the swallow, so that I may stop being silent? By silence I have lost the Muse, and Phoebus does not regard me. So Amyclae, when its people kept silent, was destroyed by silence. Tomorrow let him love who has never loved, and let him who has loved love tomorrow.
note:
A late Latin poem of ninety-three lines for the eve of the spring festival of Venus, with the refrain "cras amet qui numquam amavit." After all the flowers and nymphs, the last stanza turns to the poet's own silence, and envies the swallow, who is Procne, sister of the raped Philomela. Eliot's 1922 text reads "ceu" for the poem's "uti," and so do his manuscripts; the line was changed to "uti" in <i>Collected Poems 1909–1935</i> (1936). The whole of the poem's spring, its birds and its silence are in these nine lines.

author: Thomas Kyd
date: c. 1587

Set in type for <i>Poetry</i> (Chicago) in 1915 and withdrawn at proof stage, the poem about a saint who wanted to be a tree, a fish and a girl, and died pierced with arrows, survived among the manuscripts Eliot sent to John Quinn in 1922 along with the <i>Waste Land</i> drafts. note:
A lecture given at the University of Minnesota in 1956 to an audience of nearly fourteen thousand, in which Eliot, thirty-four years on, described the notes as padding that had got out of hand: he had meant only to list his references, to spike the guns of critics who had called him a plagiarist, and then, when the poem proved too short to make a book on its own, expanded them to fill pages. He also admitted they could not now be removed: readers had "bought" the notes with the poem. Take the notes as part of the poem and as a joke at its expense; both are true.
