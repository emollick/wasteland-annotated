id: weston
title: From Ritual to Romance
author: Jessie L. Weston
date: 1920
lang: English
kind: anthropology
what: The title, the plan, the Fisher King, the Chapel Perilous, the Syrian merchants.
lines: 0, 46, 386, 424
note:
Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance who came to believe, under the influence of Frazer, that the Grail stories preserved the remains of an ancient fertility cult: a wounded king, a land gone waste, a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics, and a ritual of initiation whose secrets travelled Europe with Syrian merchants. Almost no medievalist now accepts the argument. It does not matter. Eliot's note says the book suggested "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism," and thirty-four years later he said he regretted "having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail." Read chapters II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel) and you have the poem's skeleton; read the rest and you have a very good bad book.

id: frazer
title: The Golden Bough (Adonis, Attis, Osiris)
author: Sir James George Frazer
date: 1906–15 (third edition); one-volume abridgement 1922
lang: English
kind: anthropology
what: The dying and reviving god; the buried corpse that sprouts; the Hanged God; the effigy thrown into the sea.
lines: 46, 71, 312
note:
Frazer's twelve-volume comparative study of magic and religion, which Eliot's headnote says "has influenced our generation profoundly," argued that behind the myths of Adonis, Attis, Osiris and, by implication, Christ lay a single agricultural drama: the god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped along by ritual (a sacrificed king, an effigy buried or drowned). The volumes Eliot names describe the "gardens of Adonis," pots of quick-sprouting seed thrown into the sea, and the effigies of Osiris "made of earth and corn," buried to sprout. Stetson's planted corpse, Phlebas in the current, the Hanged Man and the whole question of whether April can bring anything back are Frazer's. The book's title and its opening page come from Turner's painting <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1834), which Frazer took for a view of Nemi; it shows the Sibyl at Lake Avernus.

title: Satyricon, chapter 48
author: Petronius
date: 1st century AD
lang: Latin, with Greek
kind: prose
what: The epigraph: the Sibyl who wanted to die.
lines: 0
passage: Nam Sibyllam quidem Cumis ego ipse oculis meis vidi in ampulla pendere, et cum illi pueri dicerent: Σίβυλλα τί θέλεις; respondebat illa: ἀποθανεῖν θέλω.
trans: For I myself, with my own eyes, saw the Sibyl at Cumae hanging in a jar, and when the boys said to her, "Sibyl, what do you want?" she answered, "I want to die."
note:
The speaker is Trimalchio, the freed slave turned millionaire whose dinner party is the surviving centre of the <i>Satyricon</i>, showing off between courses. The Sibyl of Cumae, in Ovid's telling, asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand and forgot to ask for youth, and shrank with age until only her voice was left. A prophetess kept as a curiosity; a poem introduced by a fat man's boast.

author: Dante Alighieri
date: c. 1315
lang: Italian and Provençal
kind: poem
what: "il miglior fabbro" (the dedication) and "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina" (line 428).
lines: 0, 428
passage: «Tan m’abellis vostre cortes deman,<br>qu’ieu no me puesc ni voill a vos cobrire.<br>Ieu sui Arnaut, que plor e vau cantan;<br>consiros vei la passada folor,<br>e vei jausen lo joi qu’esper, denan.<br>Ara vos prec, per aquella valor<br>que vos guida al som de l’escalina,<br>sovenha vos a temps de ma dolor!».<br>Poi s’ascose nel foco che li affina.
trans: "So much does your courteous question please me that I cannot and will not hide myself from you. I am Arnaut, who weep and go singing; in thought I see my past folly, and I see with joy, ahead, the joy I hope for. note:
On the seventh terrace of Purgatory, where the lustful walk in flame, Dante meets Guido Guinizelli, who points out Arnaut Daniel as "miglior fabbro del parlar materno," the better craftsman of the mother tongue (line 117). Arnaut answers in his own Provençal, the only passage in the <i>Comedy</i> in a vernacular other than Italian, and steps back into the fire. Eliot took the dedication to Pound from line 117, the title of his 1920 collection <i>Ara Vos Prec</i> from line 145, and line 428 of the poem from line 148. Modern texts read "li affina"; Eliot's "gli" follows the editions he owned.

trans: Westward the gaze wanders; eastward the ship runs. Fresh blows the wind toward home: my Irish child, where do you linger? Is it the breath of your sighs that fills my sails? Woe, woe, you wind! Woe, ah woe, my child! Irish maid, you wild, lovely maid! . . . SHEPHERD: Desolate and empty the sea!
note:
Eliot's note cites the opera by act and verse as if it were a book, and it was: the libretto was sold and read. Eliot heard <i>Tristan</i> in Boston in 1909, as a Harvard senior, and wrote a poem called "Opera" about it; Stravinsky, who met him in 1956, came away thinking that <i>Tristan</i> "must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life." Ludwig II, who paid for the first performance, drowned in the Starnberger See of line 8.

title: Metamorphoses, Book VI: Tereus, Procne and Philomela
author: Ovid
date: AD 8
lang: Latin
kind: poem
what: "The change of Philomel."
lines: 99, 203, 429
note:
Tereus of Thrace, escorting his wife's sister Philomela to visit Procne, rapes her in a hut in the woods and cuts out her tongue; she weaves the crime into a cloth and sends it to her sister; Procne kills her son Itys and serves him to his father; Tereus, pursuing the sisters with a sword, and the sisters, fleeing, all become birds. Ovid does not say which sister became the nightingale; later tradition made Philomela the nightingale and Procne the swallow, and Eliot follows it. The story sits above the mantel in Part II, breaks into birdsong in Part III, and ends the poem as a swallow. Arthur Golding's 1567 translation, the one Shakespeare read, is the English to go to.

lines: 218
passage: Nam duo magnorum viridi coeuntia silva<br>corpora serpentum baculi violaverat ictu<br>deque viro factus, mirabile, femina septem<br>egerat autumnos; octavo rursus eosdem<br>vidit et “est vestrae si tanta potentia plagae,”<br>dixit “ut auctoris sortem in contraria mutet,<br>nunc quoque vos feriam!” percussis anguibus isdem<br>forma prior rediit genetivaque venit imago.
trans: For once, in a green wood, he had struck with his staff the bodies of two great serpents as they coupled, and, from a man made a woman, wonderful to tell, had lived seven autumns; in the eighth he saw the same snakes again and said, "If a blow to you has such power that it changes its author's condition into its opposite, I will strike you now as well!" He struck the snakes, and his former shape returned, the form he was born with.
note:
Lines 324–331 of the passage Eliot's note quotes at length, calling it "of great anthropological interest." Jupiter and Juno, disputing which sex takes more pleasure in love, consult the one person who has been both; his answer costs him his sight and earns him prophecy. Eliot's note makes him the poem's uniting consciousness. Ovid makes him a man who lost an argument with a goddess.

passage: LIVIA: Alas, poor widow, I shall be too hard for thee.<br>MOTHER: Y’are cunning at the game, I’ll be sworn, madam.<br>LIVIA: It will be found so, ere I give you over. . . . Did not I say my duke would fetch you o’er, widow? . . . I’ve given thee blind mate twice.
note:
Livia, a procuress, keeps Bianca's mother-in-law busy at chess while the Duke of Florence takes Bianca in the gallery above; the lines about the game double as commentary on the seduction. Eliot's section title comes from this scene and from Middleton's <i>A Game at Chess</i> (1624), an anti-Spanish satire so popular that it ran nine days and was closed by the Privy Council. Eliot's 1927 essay on Middleton praised him for "the perpetual instinct of the man of genius."
