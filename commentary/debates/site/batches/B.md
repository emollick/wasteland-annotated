## site/paths.txt (editable: title, intro)
id: first
title: A first reading
intro: Twelve stops for someone meeting the poem for the first time. Each stop is a place to stand, not an explanation. Read the lines, read the note, move on.
---
id: water
title: Water
intro: The poem's hydrology, from spring rain to the last rain. Follow the water and you follow the argument: water is what the waste land needs, water is what drowns you, and the two are never told apart.
---
id: dead
title: The dead
intro: Burials, drownings, bones and the crowd that death has undone. The poem's first section is called The Burial of the Dead, from the Book of Common Prayer, and the dead never quite stay in it.
---
id: women
title: The women
intro: Eliot's note on Tiresias says "all the women are one woman." Here they are, in order of appearance, so that you can test the claim. Most of them speak; several are quotations; two are queens; one is a bird.
---
id: cities
title: Cities
intro: The poem's geography, in the order the poem gives it. It begins in Munich and ends on the Ganges, with London at its centre and five capitals falling in two lines.
---
id: birds
title: Birds
intro: Every bird in the poem is a musician, and every song is a complaint. Recordings, where they exist, are in the listening room.
---
id: nothing
title: Nothing
intro: One word, followed through the poem. It is said by a lover, a woman with bad nerves, a man who will not answer, a Thames-daughter, and the poor on Margate sands.
---
id: eyes
title: Eyes
intro: Eyes that fail, eyes turned to pearls, eyes fixed on feet, lidless eyes, a one-eyed merchant, a blind seer, eyes that were holden on the road to Emmaus. The poem's people cannot see, or see too much.
---
id: eliot
title: The poem in Eliot's life
intro: Places and days from the poet's own year, 1921 into 1922, that surface in the text. Not a key to the poem, but a map of where he was standing.

## site/drafts.txt (editable: title, body)
id: title-draft
part: 0
line: 0
title: The title that was crossed out
body:
Across the top of the typescript of the first two parts Eliot typed "He Do the Police in Different Voices," from Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i>. "The Waste Land" replaced it, with Weston's Grail country and Malory's "waste land" of the Dolorous Stroke behind it. The Dickens title survives in the notes only as a habit of mind: the poem reads the world's news aloud in every voice it can do.
---
id: epigraph-draft
part: 0
line: 0
title: The epigraph that was replaced
body:
Kurtz's "The horror! the horror!" from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i> stood at the head of the typescript. Pound doubted Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation"; Eliot called it "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," and then substituted the Sibyl from Petronius. The change turns a dying man's verdict on his life into an immortal's wish to be done with hers.

## site/timeline.json (editable: what)
- 1888: Thomas Stearns Eliot born in St Louis, Missouri, 26 September.
- 1910–11: A year in Paris; friendship with Jean Verdenal. August 1911 in Munich, where 'Prufrock' is finished.
- 1911–14: Harvard graduate study: Sanskrit and Pali with C. R. Lanman, the Upanishads, Buddhism, and F. H. Bradley's philosophy.
- 1914: Eliot arrives in England; meets Ezra Pound in September.
- 1915: Jean Verdenal killed at the Dardanelles in May. Eliot marries Vivien Haigh-Wood in June. 'The Death of Saint Narcissus' set in type and withdrawn.
- 1917: Joins Lloyds Bank, Colonial and Foreign Department, Lombard Street. Prufrock and Other Observations published, dedicated to Verdenal.
- 1918: 'Dans le Restaurant' written; its last stanza will become 'Death by Water'.
- 1919: December: Eliot tells his mother his New Year's resolution is 'to write a long poem I have had on my mind for a long time'.
- 1921, spring: Parts I and II drafted, under the title 'He Do the Police in Different Voices'.
- 1921, summer: Eliot's mother, sister and brother visit London for ten weeks. His health breaks down.
- 1921, October: Lloyds grants three months' sick leave. Eliot goes to Margate, 15 October to 12 November, and writes fifty lines of Part III in a shelter on the front.
- 1921, November: To Paris, leaving Vivien; then to Lausanne under Dr Roger Vittoz. Parts IV and V written.
- 1922, January: Back through Paris. Pound edits the typescript, in person and then by post, cutting it roughly in half. 'Complimenti, you bitch.'
- 1922, June: Eliot reads the poem aloud at the Woolfs'. Virginia Woolf: 'He sang it & chanted it rhythmed it.'
- 1922, September: Smyrna burns, 13–22 September.
- 1922, October: First publication, without notes, in the first number of The Criterion, Eliot's own new magazine.
- 1922, November: American publication in The Dial. The Dial's $2,000 award for 1922 goes to Eliot.
- 1922, December: Boni & Liveright publish the first book edition in New York, 1,000 copies, with the notes written to fill it out.
- 1923, January: Eliot inscribes Pound's copy of the Boni & Liveright edition to him as il miglior fabbro, the phrase that becomes the printed dedication in 1925.
- 1923, September: The Hogarth Press edition, hand-set by Virginia and Leonard Woolf at Richmond, about 460 copies.
- 1925: Poems 1909–1925 prints the dedication to Pound for the first time. Eliot leaves Lloyds for Faber & Gwyer.
- 1933: Eliot records the poem at Columbia University, on aluminium discs, the earliest surviving reading (once dated 1935; redated by recent scholarship).
- 1940s: Eliot records the poem again; this later reading, dated 1946 by the Poetry Archive and issued on record, is the one most often heard.
- 1956: 'The Frontiers of Criticism', Minneapolis: the notes were 'bogus scholarship', and he regrets the 'wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail'.
- 1965: Eliot dies in London, 4 January.
- 1968: The New York Public Library announces that it holds the lost typescript, with Pound's and Vivien's pencil on it.
- 1971: Valerie Eliot's facsimile edition of the drafts.
- 2015: Christopher Ricks and Jim McCue's annotated edition, The Poems of T. S. Eliot.
- 2022: Centenary. The 1922 text is in the public domain in the United States; in the United Kingdom it remains in copyright until the end of 2035.
