Read as a first-time listener, against the live page. One thing the page settles: in each record the body comes first and the player sits under it behind a "Play here" toggle, so "What plays below" and "The film below" are right, and the body is what a listener reads before pressing play. ## Objections

### eliot-1935-pennsound-columbia
Problem: The Rumsey credit is now given twice in three records (here and in eliot-1933-poetryarchive); the brief says a fact is stated once. It belongs in the first record, whose title asserts 1933; this record's business is PennSound's label and the books.
Corrected:
body: The same Columbia series in two complete versions, divided by section, on PennSound’s own page. PennSound holds it with the permission of the Eliot estate, for study and teaching. It gives the date as 1935, though its own headnote says the Columbia recordings were made in 1933, 1935 and later; for the date of 1933 the Poetry Archive refers readers to Richard Swigg’s <i>Quick, Said the Bird</i> and Jason Camlot’s <i>Phonopoetics</i>.

### eliot-later-harperaudio-archive
Problem: The first sentence cannot be read at one pass ("the one issued on record, first by Caedmon and later by HarperCollins, which bought Caedmon, as Harper Audio": "later" twice, "record" against "recording", and "as Harper Audio" stranded at the end). The second sentence carries five facts and ends on "and no other", which reads as a twist. Split both; nothing is lost.
Corrected:
body: The later recording, the one issued on record. Caedmon issued it first; HarperCollins, which later bought Caedmon, reissued it under its Harper Audio name. This copy was put out by Harper Audio on 18 January 1994 through the Internet Multicasting Service, an early internet radio channel, as sound files at 8 kHz; HarperCollins consented to that distribution only. The Internet Archive keeps it as a thirty-one-minute MP3. The sound is poor, about the quality of a telephone line. The Poetry Archive dates the recording 1946; the item itself gives no year.

### irons-atkins-bbc-radio4-2012
Problem: The title does not work. "Jeremy Irons, BBC Radio 4: with Eileen Atkins in 2012, alone in 2017" is a riddle rather than a name: a colon, then a balanced pair, which is the brief's paired antithesis, and at thirteen words it is five longer than any other title on the page. Worse, the small type under it still describes the 2012 broadcast (Atkins; four introducers; "First broadcast 30 March 2012"), and the disc label will read "Jeremy Irons and Eileen Atkins, 2012", while the body's first words say what plays is 2017. A record cannot be one broadcast in its title, who, when and label and another in its player. Since the player is fixed, the record is the 2017 programme; the 2012 reading, with every fact the scholar confirmed, goes in the body's third sentence. The body's middle sentence also needs untangling ("Martha Kearney and guests, here Jeanette Winterson, Rowan Williams and Jackie Kay, introduced").
Corrected:
title: Jeremy Irons reads the poem, BBC Radio 4, 2017
who: Jeremy Irons, presented by Martha Kearney with Jeanette Winterson, Rowan Williams and Jackie Kay
when: Broadcast 1 January 2017
body: Irons reads the poem on his own in this Radio 4 programme, broadcast on 1 January 2017 and kept by the Internet Archive in a recording made off the air. It was the third of five programmes in which Martha Kearney and her guests introduced his readings of Eliot; the guests here are Jeanette Winterson, Rowan Williams and Jackie Kay. Five years earlier, on 30 March 2012, Irons and Eileen Atkins had shared the poem’s voices between them in another Radio 4 broadcast, introduced by Rowan Williams, Jackie Kay, Matthew Hollis and Sean O’Brien. The BBC’s page for that reading, linked here, has the details and no audio; the copies of it on YouTube are listeners’ uploads and are not played here.

### irons-cusack-borris-festival
Problem: "in June 2022, its centenary year" attaches "its" to the festival; and "; fifty-two minutes, with the introduction and the audience left in" is a clause without a verb. The recording's being the festival's own is the reason it can be played here, so it should sit with the length.
Corrected:
body: In June 2022, the poem’s centenary year, Irons and Cusack read it at the festival, introduced by James Lever. The festival’s own recording, posted in 2023, runs fifty-two minutes, with the introduction and the audience left in.

### shaw-bbc-newsnight-clip
Problem: "as a piece of theatre: one performer on a bare stage, taking every voice" is the brief's colon doing a verb's work; the history then runs 1995, 1997, 1996; and "The clip here is the BBC’s own: Shaw performing" is a second such colon.
Corrected:
body: Deborah Warner directed Fiona Shaw in the poem as a piece of theatre, with Shaw alone on a bare stage taking every voice. It opened in Brussels in May 1995 and toured, and {link:shaw-warner-film-cannes-1996|Warner’s film of it, made for the BBC, was shown at Cannes} in May 1996. In 1997 the staging ran at Wilton’s Music Hall in the East End, which is why that hall is on the site’s map. The clip here is the BBC’s own, of Shaw performing part of the poem on Newsnight in January 2015, fifty years after Eliot’s death. The film itself circulates only in unofficial copies, which are not played here.

### gotterdammerung-solti-1964-rhinemaidens
Problem: "lines 277–278 and 290–291" uses dashes where every other record on the page writes "lines 31 to 34", "lines 356 to 358". House style only.
Corrected:
body: The Thames-daughters’ refrain at lines 277 to 278 and 290 to 291 quotes the Rhinemaidens’ cry from the last act of Wagner’s Götterdämmerung, where they rise from the river to beg Siegfried for the ring. The scene begins on side nine, “Act III Scene 1”.

### bl-1922-first-edition-archive-duke
Problem: The third sentence explains the run-over twice ("set like the run-over of the line before it, as if it were a line too long for the page and carried on") and then ends on a colon. One explanation, in plain words, and a full stop.
Corrected:
body: The book this site follows, scanned page by page by Duke University Libraries. The marginal line numbers are printed every ten lines. On page 42 the line “If there were water” is set as if it were the end of the line before it, carried over for want of room, and the numbering skips it. From there the 1922 numbers run one behind the count used here.

### bl-1922-first-edition-archive-byu
Problem: "Not a second scan of the first printing." opens on a fragment and a twist before the reader has been told anyone thought it was one. "Gallup A6a" and "limitation leaf" are names with no word of what they are. The rest is sound and stays.
Corrected:
body: The library’s record calls this copy no. 596 of the thousand and cites Gallup A6a, the 1922 edition’s entry in Donald Gallup’s bibliography of Eliot, while also noting a fourth printing. It is not a second scan of the first printing. The title page reads Horace Liveright, the verso “Fourth Printing, April, 1930”, and it has no limitation statement, the note that numbered each copy of the first printing. The pages are the 1922 setting of type, page for page and with the same marginal numbers; “hot water” at line 135 and “Heimat” at line 32, where letters are missing in the Duke copy, print cleanly here. Copies of the first printing exist in two states: in the earlier, line 339 reads “Dead mountain mouth of carious teeth”; in the later the a of “mountain” had dropped out and it reads “mount in”. Duke’s copy is the later state, and this printing still reads “mount in”.

### dial-november-1922-archive
Problem: "Here “cruelest” has one l" gives the reader nothing to compare it with; say what the book prints. "The Criterion printing" is a name with no word of what the Criterion is, and "in full view" is HathiTrust's term, not a reader's.
Corrected:
body: The poem as it first reached American readers, without the notes, in the magazine that gave Eliot its two-thousand-dollar award that year. Here the first line’s “cruellest” is spelled “cruelest”, with one l. The poem’s earliest printing of all was in The Criterion, the London magazine Eliot edited, in October 1922; that issue is on HathiTrust, where it can be read in full from the United States.

### nypl-digital-waste-land-typescript
Problem: Three names with no word of who they are: John Quinn (why did Eliot send him the manuscript?), the Berg Collection (of what?), Valerie Eliot (who?). Vivien Eliot likewise. A few words each; the sentence about the link, which is the thing to know before clicking, is right and stays.
Corrected:
body: The typescript and manuscript pages, with Pound’s cuts and comments and notes by Eliot’s wife Vivien, which Eliot sent in October 1922 to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector. After Quinn’s death they passed to his sister and then to his niece, who sold them to the New York Public Library’s Berg Collection in 1958; the library announced the purchase in 1968, after Eliot’s death. The library has digitised the pages but shows the images only in the building, so the link here goes to its record, not to the pages. The way to read them is the facsimile edition that Eliot’s widow, Valerie Eliot, published in 1971, reissued by Faber in 2022 with the pages in colour.

### frazer-adonis-attis-osiris-1906
Problem: Frazer's book is now explained; Weston's arrives with no word of why it is here. Four words fix it.
Corrected:
body: Eliot’s headnote names “the two volumes Adonis, Attis, Osiris”, which are Part IV of the third edition of The Golden Bough (1914). This is the book’s first edition of 1906, in one volume, where Frazer’s account of the dying and reviving gods, the gardens of Adonis and the images of the god thrown into the sea first appeared. The other book the headnote names, Jessie Weston’s From Ritual to Romance, is {link:gutenberg-4090-weston|on Project Gutenberg}, and so is {link:gutenberg-3623-frazer-golden-bough|the one-volume Golden Bough} of 1922.
