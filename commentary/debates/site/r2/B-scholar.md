# Round 2, scholar, batch B

Checked against: the site's data.js (paths, lines, spans, notes) fetched 20 September 2026; the Boni & Liveright 1922 text (Internet Archive OCR, three scans) for the notes and for lines 132 and 141; Our Mutual Friend, Book I, ch. 16; Weston's From Ritual to Romance (Gutenberg); the Poetry Archive's Eliot page; the Historic England list entry for the Nayland Rock shelter; Kent Literature's quotations from the Letters; Wikipedia as a pointer only.

Answers to the editor's questions, in brief:
- Mrs Porter. The 1922 note reads, exactly: "I do not know the origin of the ballad from which these lines are taken; it was reported to me from Sydney, Australia." (Semicolon in the first edition and on the site; the Gutenberg transcription has a colon.) The rewrite's paraphrase is right.
- Verdenal. A French medical student, met at the pension in Paris in 1910–11; killed 2 May 1915 at the Dardanelles. Confirmed.
- Saint Narcissus. Set up in galley for Poetry (Chicago), meant for the October 1915 number, withdrawn; the proof is in the Harriet Monroe collection at Chicago; first printed 1950. The reworking in lines 25–30 is right. Confirmed.
- Vivien's two lines. Confirmed from secondary sources, not from the facsimile itself: see Still unsettled.
- Margate. The arrival date should go back in, as "the middle of October": see the objection to 1921, October.
- Pathway fit. Six of the nine intros do not match their stops: see water, cities, birds, nothing, eyes, eliot.

## Objections

### water
Problem: The pathway's eighteen stops run from line 4 to line 424, not to the rain at 395: after the rain come the sunken Ganges (396), the boat (419) and the fisher on the shore (424). And there is no stop at the shower over the Starnbergersee (lines 8–9); the stops before Madame Sosostris are the dry stone (24) and the shepherd's empty sea (42). Source: data.js, paths[water].
Corrected: The passages where water is present or lacking, in the order of the poem, from the spring rain of line 4 to the fisher on the shore at line 424: the dry stone of line 24 and the empty sea of line 42, the drowned sailor in Madame Sosostris’s pack and her warning “Fear death by water” (47–55), the Thames of Part III and the waters of Leman (182), the drowning of Phlebas in Part IV, the rock without water in Part V (331–359), the rain of line 395 and the sunken Ganges (396), the boat of line 419. Eliot’s headnote to his notes credits Jessie L. Weston’s book on the Grail legend, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, one chapter of which is called “The Freeing of the Waters.”

### cities
Problem: Four things. The poem does not name Munich; it names the Starnbergersee and the Hofgarten, which is in Munich. "The pubs": the pathway has no stop at the pub scene, and the only pub on it is the public bar in Lower Thames Street (stop at 258, line 260). The Strand (same stop) is not in the City of London. The five cities (375–376) come before the Ganges (396), not after. The pathway also stops at Lithuania (12), Mylae (70), Leman (182), Smyrna (209), the Metropole at Brighton (213), Thebes (245), Margate (293) and Carthage (307), none of which the intro mentions. Source: data.js, paths[cities]; the poem.
Corrected: The places the poem names, in the order it names them: the Starnbergersee and the Hofgarten, in Munich, at the start; London through the middle, with the bridge, the streets and churches, a public bar in Lower Thames Street and the river from Richmond to Greenwich, and among the London stops the places the poem’s people come from or remember, Lithuania, Mylae, Leman, Smyrna, Thebes, Margate and Carthage; then, in Part V, the five cities named in two lines, “Jerusalem Athens Alexandria / Vienna London” (375–376), and the Ganges and Himavant (396–398).

### birds
Problem: Three of the pathway's nine stops are not birds, and their own notes say so: the cricket of line 23, the cicada of line 354 and the bats of line 380. The swallow at 429 is addressed, not heard. The facts about the birds are right ("Quebec County" is the note's wording). Source: data.js, paths[birds]; Eliot's note on 357.
Corrected: The poem’s birds, in the order they appear, with the lines where each is heard or named: the nightingale into which Philomel was changed (99–103 and 203–206), the gulls that Phlebas forgot (313), the hermit-thrush of line 357, which Eliot’s note says he heard in Quebec County, the cock on the rooftree (392) and the swallow of line 429. Three stops are for other creatures the poem sets beside them: the cricket of line 23, the cicada of line 354 and the bats of line 380. Recordings of the birds themselves, where they exist, are in the listening room.

### nothing
Problem: The count is right (the word occurs at 40, 120, 122, 123, 126, 302 and 305, and nowhere else), but the pathway has a seventh stop, at line 402, the thunder's "what have we given?", where the word does not occur; the intro should say so or the reader will take the stop for a mistake. Source: data.js, paths[nothing]; the poem.
Corrected: The word “nothing” wherever the poem uses it: at line 40, by the one who came back from the hyacinth garden and could not speak; at lines 120–126, between the woman with bad nerves and the man who does not answer her; and at lines 302 and 305, by the third Thames-daughter on Margate Sands. A last stop, at line 402, is the thunder’s first question, “what have we given?”, where the word does not occur.

### eyes
Problem: Small. The list is out of the poem's order (65 before 52), and line 125 is not a stop (the stops are the epigraph, 39, 48, 52, 65, 81, 138, 215, 218, 232 and 360). The pathway opens with the epigraph, where Trimalchio says he saw the Sibyl with his own eyes ("oculis meis vidi"). The Luke and Part V quotations are exact. Source: data.js, paths[eyes]; Luke 24:16; Eliot's headnote to Part V.
Corrected: Eyes in the poem, from the epigraph, where Trimalchio says he saw the Sibyl with his own eyes, to Part V: the eyes that fail in the hyacinth garden (line 39), the pearls that were the drowned man’s eyes (48), the one-eyed merchant (52), the eyes each man fixes before his feet on London Bridge (65), the “lidless eyes” of line 138, and the blind Tiresias (218). Eliot’s note on Part V names the journey to Emmaus, where, in Luke 24, the disciples’ eyes “were holden,” held, so that they did not know who walked beside them.

### eliot
Problem: The pathway's ten stops are not confined to 1921 and 1922: Munich in August 1911 (line 8), the hyacinth garden with Emily Hale or Verdenal, before the war (35), and Gloucester, Massachusetts, and Cape Ann from his boyhood summers (312, 419). Source: data.js, paths[eliot].
Corrected: Places and dates from Eliot’s life that lie behind particular lines of the poem, from the summers of his boyhood at Gloucester, Massachusetts, and Munich in 1911 to Margate and Lausanne in the autumn of 1921 and Paris in January 1922, each set beside the lines it bears on.

### title-draft
Problem: Sloppy was not brought up by Betty Higden. In the same chapter she says he was "a love-child ... parents never known; found in the street. He was brought up in the— ... the House", that is, the poorhouse; he lives with her and turns her mangle. The quotation is exact (Book I, chapter 16), and everything else checks: the headnote's wording, and no Dickens anywhere in the notes. Source: Our Mutual Friend, I.xvi; the 1922 headnote.
Corrected: The typescripts of Parts I and II carry the heading “He Do the Police in Different Voices,” with “Part I” and “Part II” beneath it. The words are Betty Higden’s in Dickens’s <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (Book I, chapter 16), praising Sloppy, the foundling who lives with her and turns her mangle, for the way he reads the newspaper aloud: “You mightn’t think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices.” The title page Eliot typed for the finished poem reads “The Waste Land,” and his headnote to the notes says that the title, the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism were suggested by Jessie L. Weston’s book on the Grail legend, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>. Dickens is not mentioned in the notes.

### poem
Problem: In the site's own spans the answer to the first DA runs to line 410 ("In our empty rooms"), not 409; my round-one numbers were wrong. The spans also give this voice the opening of Part V (322–330) and the lines that bring on the thunder (396–400), which the note leaves out. Source: voices.json, spans.
Corrected: The voice that belongs to no named character. It opens the poem, describes the room at the start of Part II, introduces Madame Sosostris (43–46), speaks the lines on the drowned Phlebas in Part IV and the opening of Part V (322–330), brings on the thunder (396–400), and answers each DA (402–410, 412–417, 419–423).

### prophet
Problem: Small. Eliot's note on line 20 is the bare citation "Cf. Ezekiel 2:1"; it does not call "Son of man" Ezekiel's phrase, and in Ezekiel the words are God's address to the prophet. And the poem does not say that it addresses the reader; "you" is unnamed. Source: Eliot's note on 20; Ezekiel 2:1.
Corrected: Lines 19–30: a voice speaking as the prophets do, addressing its hearer as “Son of man,” the words with which God addresses Ezekiel (Eliot’s note cites Ezekiel 2:1), and offering the shade of the red rock. His note on line 23 cites Ecclesiastes; “the shadow of this red rock” has been linked to Isaiah 32:2.

### pub
Problem: Small. The poem does not place the pub in London; the setting is inferred from the landlord's call and the speech. "A woman in a pub" loses nothing. Source: the poem, lines 139–171, which name no place.
Corrected: A woman in a pub, telling what she said to her friend Lil about Lil’s husband Albert, home from the army. Lines 139–171, interrupted by the publican’s call.

### fisher
Problem: Small. "The Psalmist's 'I sat down and wept'" puts the poem's words in the Psalmist's mouth: Psalm 137 has "we sat down, yea, we wept"; the first person singular is Eliot's. Source: Psalm 137:1 (Authorized Version).
Corrected: The speaker by the river in Part III, lines 173–198, who repeats Spenser’s refrain “Sweet Thames, run softly” and fishes in the dull canal behind the gashouse, speaking partly in others’ lines: Spenser (176, 183–184), Psalm 137 at 182 (“we sat down, yea, we wept”), and Ferdinand in The Tempest (191–192, Eliot’s note). That he is the Fisher King of the Grail legend is a reading; Eliot’s notes name the Fisher King at lines 46 and 425.

### song
Problem: The site's spans give lines 127–130 to the man who does not answer; this voice's span is 199–202. The note claims lines the lens colours as his, and his note claims them too. The spans are fixed, so the note must give way. Source: voices.json, spans.
Corrected: Lines 199–202: the ballad about Mrs Porter, which Eliot’s note says was reported to him from Sydney, Australia (199–201), and Verlaine’s line on children singing in the dome (202). The Shakespeherian Rag at 128–130 is given to the man who does not answer.

### thames1
Problem: Small. Eliot's note quotes Dante's Italian, "Siena mi fe', disfecemi Maremma"; the English after the colon reads as if it were the note's words. The poem's "Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me" echoes the Italian, so it is worth having. Source: Eliot's note on 293.
Corrected: Highbury, Richmond and Kew: lines 292–295. Eliot’s note sends the reader to La Pia in Dante’s Purgatorio, canto V, and quotes her line “Siena mi fe’, disfecemi Maremma,” Siena made me, Maremma unmade me.

### fragments
Problem: Small. "All but the nursery rhyme" implies a note on line 431, the poem's own line, which has none; the notes give the sources of the four quotations (site lines 428, 429, 430 and 432). Source: Eliot's notes on (his numbering) 427, 428, 429 and 431.
Corrected: Lines 427–432, a line each: the nursery rhyme “London Bridge is falling down” (427); Dante’s Purgatorio, canto XXVI (428); the Latin Pervigilium Veneris (429); Nerval’s sonnet “El Desdichado” (430); the poem’s own line, “These fragments I have shored against my ruins” (431); and Kyd’s Spanish Tragedy (432). Eliot’s notes give the sources of the four quotations.

### 1921, October
Problem: The arrival date was dropped because it did not fit "three weeks", but "three weeks" is the secondary accounts' phrase and the primary evidence points the other way. Vivien, writing from the Albemarle Hotel on 26 October 1921, says of Eliot "It is not quite a fortnight yet but he looks already younger and fatter and nicer" (Letters vol. 1, quoted at kentliterature.com), which puts the arrival about 13–15 October; Rainey (Revisiting The Waste Land) has the Eliots going to Margate "about the middle of October 1921". The site's 15 October is consistent with both; if the owner's check took it from Letters vol. 1, restore it. The Schiff quotation is exact ("I have written only some fifty lines"). The 12 November departure I could not confirm (see Still unsettled), but nothing I found contradicts it.
Corrected: Lloyds grants three months' sick leave. Eliot goes to Margate in the middle of October and stays until 12 November; on 4 November he tells Sydney Schiff that he has written 'some fifty lines' of Part III, sitting in a shelter on the seafront.

### 1968
Problem: "Went unseen until the library bought it in 1958" skips the provenance that explains the loss. After Quinn's death in 1924 the manuscript passed to his sister, Julia Anderson, and then to her daughter, Mary Conroy, who found it in storage in the early 1950s and sold it to the Berg Collection on 4 April 1958 on terms of secrecy; the library announced the purchase in October 1968. Source: NYPL, Berg Collection; Valerie Eliot's introduction to the facsimile (1971); Wikipedia's John Quinn page as a pointer.
Corrected: The New York Public Library announces that it holds the typescript, long thought lost, with Pound's and Vivien Eliot's comments written on it. Eliot had sent it to John Quinn in 1922; after Quinn's death in 1924 it passed to his sister and then to his niece, who sold it to the library in 1958 on condition of secrecy, and the purchase was not made public for ten years.

### 1971
Problem: Small. Valerie Eliot edited the facsimile; Faber and Faber published it in London and Harcourt Brace Jovanovich in New York. Source: the title page of The Waste Land: A Facsimile and Transcript (1971).
Corrected: Valerie Eliot, his widow, edits a facsimile edition of the drafts, showing Pound's cuts and comments.

## Still unsettled

- Vivien Eliot's two lines (site 153 and 164). I could not see the facsimile page (the NYPL images are script-rendered; the Norton PDF that turned up online has no text layer for the draft pages). The secondary sources agree on the substance: on the Part II typescript Vivien's pencil replaced Eliot's typed "No, ma'am, you needn't look old-fashioned at me" with "If you dont like it you can get on with it" (line 153), and his "You want to keep him at home, I suppose" with the line that became 164 (tseliot.com's page on Vivien; Mark Ford, NYRB, 20 October 2022). They differ on her exact words for 164: Wikipedia's page on Vivien (following Seymour-Jones) gives "What you get married for if you don't want to have children", against the printed "if you don't want children?". If the site quotes her pencil, it needs the facsimile (1971, pp. 14–15) or Ricks and McCue; if it only says that the two lines were hers, that is safe now.
- The Margate departure, 12 November. Not confirmed by anything I reached: Hollis says Eliot "left the Albemarle in the middle of November"; his letter to Aldington of 17 November finds him "in transit between Margate ... and Lausanne"; Rainey has him leaving England on 18 November. The date is the site's, and stands under the brief's rule; the source to cite is Letters vol. 1 (the letters of 12–17 November).
- The inscription in Pound's copy (1923, January). Wikipedia says the inscription reads "For Ezra Pound / il miglior fabbro"; my recollection of Valerie Eliot's introduction and of Ricks and McCue is "for E. P. / miglior fabbro / from T. S. E. / Jan. 1923", without "il". I could not settle it online. The entry as written is safe if Wikipedia is right; if Ricks and McCue give the shorter form, change "to him as il miglior fabbro" to "to him as miglior fabbro, the phrase from Dante that becomes the printed dedication, il miglior fabbro, in 1925".
- "He Do the Police in Different Voices". Still unverified whether the heading itself is struck through on either typescript; nothing now depends on it, since the title is "The earlier title".
- Outside the batch, for whoever has the pathway stops: the eliot pathway's stop at line 66 still says "Lloyds Bank, Lombard Street", which the editor dropped from the 1917 entry as unconfirmed; the water pathway's stop at 395 calls the rain "The only rain that falls", though a shower of rain falls at line 9; and the birds pathway's stop at 392 and the nothing pathway's stop at 402 are readings stated as facts. The stops are not in paths.txt and I have not touched them.
