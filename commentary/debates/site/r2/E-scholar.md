## Objections

### phoenician-sailor
Problem: "and says nothing of the Sailor" is untrue. Eliot's note on 46 does name him: "The Phoenician Sailor and the Merchant appear later; also the 'crowds of people,' and Death by Water is executed in Part IV." What the note does not do is connect him with the Hanged Man.
Corrected: ANSWERS: 47–48, "Is your card, the drowned Phoenician Sailor, / (Those are pearls that were his eyes. Look!)", and Part IV, Death by Water (312–321). The pearls are from Ariel's song in <i>The Tempest</i> (I. ii). He is drawn head down, in the posture of the Tarot's Hanged Man, the card Madame Sosostris does not find (54–55). That is the drawing's suggestion, not Eliot's: his note on 46 links the Hanged Man to Frazer's Hanged God and to the hooded figure of Part V, and says of the Sailor only that he and the Merchant "appear later".
Source: Eliot's note on 46 (Boni & Liveright 1922; Gutenberg #1321).

### hanged-man
Problem: "the inverted pose, the crooked leg and the halo are Pamela Colman Smith's" gives her what every pack has. The Hanged Man hangs by one foot with the other leg bent in the Visconti-Sforza and Marseille cards; what Smith's card of 1909 added, on Waite's instruction, is the nimbus about the head (and the living tree). SHOWS stands.
Corrected: ANSWERS: 54–55, "I do not find / The Hanged Man." Eliot's note on 46 says the card "fits my purpose in two ways": it is "associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer", the sacrificed god of <i>The Golden Bough</i>, killed so that the land may live again, and with "the hooded figure in the passage of the disciples to Emmaus in Part V" (360–366), where the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples (Luke 24). The walkers on the road and the shoot on the dead bough come from that note; the inverted pose and the crooked leg are traditional to the card, and the halo follows Pamela Colman Smith's drawing of 1909.
Source: Waite, The Pictorial Key to the Tarot (1911), XII: "a nimbus about the head"; the Marseille pattern, Le Pendu.

### crowd-in-a-ring
Problem: "the crowds of the dead in Dante's Inferno (cantos iii and iv)" is loose for the second note. Eliot's note on 63 cites Inferno iii. 55–7, the long train of the dead; his note on 64 cites iv. 25–7, which is not a crowd but the sighs that tremble the air of Limbo.
Corrected: ANSWERS: 56, "I see crowds of people, walking round in a ring." The crowd that flows over London Bridge follows at 62–65; Eliot's notes on 63 and 64 cite Dante's <i>Inferno</i>, iii. 55–7 (the long train of the dead outside the gate of Hell) and iv. 25–7 (the sighs of Limbo).
Source: Eliot's notes on 63 and 64.

### fisher-king
Problem: "the poem's title, plan and much of its symbolism came from" Weston overstates the headnote, which says "were suggested by" and "a good deal of the incidental symbolism". Where the note can be quoted it should be.
Corrected: ANSWERS: 187–195, "While I was fishing in the dull canal / On a winter evening round behind the gashouse". The Fisher King is the wounded king of the Grail legend, whose land lies waste until he is healed. Eliot's headnote says that "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem were suggested by" Jessie L. Weston's book on the Grail legend, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920); his note on line 425, "Fishing, with the arid plain behind me", sends the reader to her chapter on the Fisher King, and his note on 46 attaches the king to the Man with Three Staves.
Source: Eliot's headnote to the notes; his notes on 46 and on 424 (site's 425).

### swallow
Problem: three small things of fact. "Of late antiquity" is one guess at a date the Oxford Classical Dictionary leaves open between the second and the fifth century (the fourth most likely). "In the usual telling of the story" is the Latin telling (Hyginus, Fabulae 45); the Greek accounts make Philomela the swallow, so the card should say which it means. And "the nightingale's song of Tereus's crime" is not quite the Pervigilium, whose nightingale sings so sweetly "you would deny that a sister complains of a barbarous husband"; the poem calls her "the girl of Tereus" and that is enough. A reader who looks the Latin up will also find "uti" where Eliot printed "ceu", and should be told why.
Corrected: ANSWERS: 429, "Quando fiam ceu chelidon—O swallow swallow". The Latin is from the last stanza of the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i> ("The Vigil of Venus"), an anonymous Latin poem of uncertain date, probably of the fourth century AD. The poet has listened to the nightingale, "the girl of Tereus", and asks: "when shall I become as the swallow, that I may cease to be silent?" (The poem reads <i>uti chelidon</i>; Eliot printed <i>ceu</i>.) Eliot's note adds "Cf. Philomela in Parts II and III"; in the Latin telling of the story, which Eliot follows, Philomela became the nightingale and her sister Procne the swallow (the older Greek accounts give the birds the other way about). "O swallow swallow" echoes a song in Tennyson's <i>The Princess</i> (1847), "O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South".
Source: Oxford Classical Dictionary, "Pervigilium Veneris"; the poem's last stanzas (thelatinlibrary.com/pervig.html); Hyginus, Fabulae 45; Apollodorus iii. 14. 8; The Princess (1847), iv; the site's text at 429.

### thunder
Problem: trivial, but a quotation should be exact. Eliot's note, as the site prints it, reads "(Give, sympathise, control)", capital G. SHOWS stands.
Corrected: ANSWERS: 396–423, "Ganga was sunken, and the limp leaves / Waited for rain, while the black clouds / Gathered far distant, over Himavant". Ganga is the Ganges and Himavant the Himalaya. The thunder then speaks three times, "DA", and each time the speaker hears in it one of three Sanskrit words, <i>Datta</i>, <i>Dayadhvam</i>, <i>Damyata</i>, which Eliot's note translates "Give, sympathise, control" and traces to a fable in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad. The key on the bank is for <i>Dayadhvam</i> ("I have heard the key / Turn in the door once", 412–413); the boat is for <i>Damyata</i> ("The boat responded / Gaily, to the hand expert with sail and oar", 419–420).
Source: Eliot's note on 401 (his numbering), as printed on the site.
