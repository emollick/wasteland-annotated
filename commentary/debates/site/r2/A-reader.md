## Objections

### city-lede
Problem: "whose clock the poem hears" is a metaphor standing in for a fact, in a sentence that is otherwise a plain inventory; the fact is that the clock strikes nine at line 68.
Corrected: A mile of the City, from the Cannon Street Hotel to Billingsgate, with the buildings the poem names drawn as they stood in 1922: St Mary Woolnoth, Hawksmoor’s church at the top of King William Street, whose clock strikes nine at line 68; London Bridge, Rennie’s bridge of 1831, which the crowd crosses in Part I; St Magnus the Martyr, Wren’s church at the foot of the bridge; and the Cannon Street Hotel, where Mr. Eugenides asked the speaker to lunch. Click a building for its lines.

### world-lede
Problem: "from the Starnbergersee near Munich to the Ganges, joined by a line in the order the poem names them" tells a first-time reader that the Ganges is the last place the poem names, which the scholar ruled out in round one; and on the map as built the line begins at Cumae, the Sibyl's town in the epigraph, and ends at Himavant, so "the first place named" needs a qualifier. The world map also has three grey dots (Paris, St Louis, Gloucester, Massachusetts) with no legend line of its own, unlike the London map.
Corrected: The places the poem names outside London, joined by a line in the order they come: from Cumae, the Sibyl’s town in the epigraph, to the Ganges and Himavant, the Himalayas, in Part V. The Starnbergersee near Munich (line 8) is the first place named in the poem’s own lines; Ludwig II of Bavaria drowned in it in 1886. Grey dots are places from Eliot’s own life that the poem does not name.

### drafts-lede
Problem: "John Quinn" is a name dropped with no word of who he was, at the moment the reader most needs one (why did the typescript go to him?), and "Vivien Eliot" wants "his wife" for a reader who does not know the biography. Everything else reads at one pass.
Corrected: The poem was typed out at roughly twice its published length of four hundred and thirty-four lines. Ezra Pound did most of the cutting, in Paris in January 1922 and by letter after Eliot went home; Eliot’s wife Vivien added comments and two lines of the pub scene; Eliot himself struck out the first opening of Part I. In 1922 Eliot gave the typescript to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector who had arranged the poem’s American publication. After Quinn’s death in 1924 it went unseen until the New York Public Library bought it in 1958, and its whereabouts were made public only in 1968, three years after Eliot’s death. It was printed in facsimile in 1971. The cut passages are still in copyright, so they are described here, not reproduced; the descriptions follow the facsimile edition page by page.
(The descriptor for Quinn is mine and needs the scholar's confirmation and source: Valerie Eliot's introduction to the facsimile (1971); The Letters of T. S. Eliot, vol. 1; Rainey, The Annotated Waste Land (2005), introduction.)

### drafts-lede-2
Problem: "Pound's own account" does not say of what, and "signed Sage Homme, man-midwife" reads as if "man-midwife" were part of the signature rather than its translation.
Corrected: Pound’s own account of his part is in the letter of 24 January 1922 in which he answered the revised typescript Eliot had sent from London. The verses in it are signed <i>Sage Homme</i>, that is, man-midwife, and say that “on each Occasion / Ezra performed the Caesarean Operation.”

### paths-lede
Problem: "each a sequence of about a dozen stops" is wrong at both ends: the cards on this same page say 12, 18, 15, 16, 15, 9, 7, 11 and 10 stops.
Corrected: Nine pathways through the poem, each a sequence of between seven and eighteen stops. Choose one and the poem page opens with a small guide in the corner that takes you from stop to stop.

### walk-sub
Problem: the same count; the chooser lists the pathways without their counts, so the range is worth giving here.
Corrected: Each pathway takes you through the poem in a sequence of stops, between seven and eighteen of them. A small guide in the corner of the poem page moves you from one stop to the next.

### about-how-1
Problem: "Everything is on the poem page" is not true of a site with six other pages, and a first-time reader takes it literally; what is meant is that the poem is not split across pages. "The row of lenses ... chooses" gives the row a will; "sets" is the plain verb.
Corrected: The whole poem is on one page. The row of lenses above the text sets what is shown beside each line: Eliot’s notes, the sources, the speakers, the translations, water and drought, the places, the hours, or the cuts from the drafts. The keys 1 to 9 switch lenses; Escape closes anything open. Marked phrases open a card in the margin (or under the line, on a phone). The thin strip at the edge of the screen stands for the whole poem, one mark per line, coloured by the lens in use; click it to move to a line. The dark setting, called the violet hour after line 215, follows your system’s preference and can be switched with the small dot in the running head.

### legend-tongues-text
Problem: The first sentence is still three verbless fragments joined by semicolons, and "Arnaut Daniel's speech in Dante" drops a name with no word of who he is.
Corrected: The epigraph is in Latin and Greek. The poem has phrases in German, French, Italian, Latin and Sanskrit, and Eliot’s note on line 428 quotes the Provençal that Dante gives the troubadour Arnaut Daniel to speak. The cries of birds, the bells and the river’s song are marked as well. Click any coloured phrase for a translation. Eliot printed none of them in translation; the translations here were made for this edition.
("troubadour" is my handle for the name; the scholar to confirm.)

### legend-places-text
Problem: The third sentence carries the route, the station, the note to line 68, the clock and the bank in one run of sixty words, on a small card; at one reading I lost the subject at the semicolon. Nothing in it is wrong.
Corrected: Place names are marked. Click one for its lines and its spot on the map. The route of Part I, over London Bridge and up King William Street to St Mary Woolnoth, is the way City workers came in from London Bridge station. Eliot’s note to line 68 says the “dead sound” of the church clock at nine was “a phenomenon which I have often noticed”; Lloyds Bank, where he worked from 1917 to 1925, was a short walk from the church. The places along the river in Part III run from Richmond to Margate.

### legend-clock-text
Problem: The paragraph enumerates five of the seasons, and the card then prints the full list of fifteen under the dial, so the fact is stated twice and the paragraph's list is the shorter and less complete one; "autumn without the word" is a turn to end on; and the card never says that the dots on the face can be clicked, which they can.
Corrected: The hours the poem names, set on a clock face, with a list of them under it and then a list of the seasons it names, from “April” in the first line onward. Autumn is never named; the fallen leaves of Part III (line 173) imply it, and they are in the list. Click a dot on the face, or an entry in either list, to go to its line.

### legend-drafts-text
Problem: "the couplets about Fresca" drops a name a first-time reader cannot place (a person? a place?), and "Vivien Eliot" wants "his wife" here as on the drafts page.
Corrected: Where the typescript differed from the printed poem, a note in typewriter face stands in the text and describes the passage: the cancelled opening of Part I, the couplets about a woman called Fresca that opened Part III, the sea voyage that opened Part IV, and the smaller cuts and changes made by Pound, by Eliot’s wife Vivien and by Eliot himself. The cut passages are still in copyright, so they are described, not quoted.

### tarot-phoenician-sailor-note
Problem: "he is Phlebas" identifies one name with another; a reader who has not reached Part IV does not know who Phlebas is.
Corrected: <p>Not a card of any Tarot pack. Eliot’s note to line 46 says the Phoenician Sailor “appears later” and that “Death by Water is executed in Part IV”: he is Phlebas the Phoenician, the drowned man of Part IV (line 312). The note to line 218 adds that the one-eyed merchant “melts into the Phoenician Sailor, and the latter is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples.” Line 48, “Those are pearls that were his eyes,” is from Ariel’s song in <i>The Tempest</i>, sung to Ferdinand about his father, who is thought drowned.</p>

### tarot-one-eyed-merchant-deck
Problem: Two colons in one sentence, the second doing the work of a verb.
Corrected: In this pack: Mr. Eugenides in profile, so that only one eye shows, in a bowler hat and an astrakhan collar, with a handful of currants held out and on his back a roped bundle whose contents are not shown.

### tarot-blank-note
Problem: "Jessie Weston, in From Ritual to Romance" is a name and a title with no word of what the book is or why it is on this card; Eliot's own headnote to the Notes supplies the word.
Corrected: <p>“This card, / Which is blank, is something he carries on his back, / Which I am forbidden to see” (lines 52–54). Madame Sosostris cannot read it, and the poem never says what it is. In <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, the book on the Grail legend that Eliot’s notes name as the source of the poem’s title and plan, Jessie Weston suggested that the mystery cult behind the legend was carried into Europe by Syrian merchants; the merchant’s unseen burden has been read in that light.</p>

### tarot-hanged-man-note
Problem: "Frazer's Hanged God is in The Golden Bough, his study of the sacrificed gods of old religion" gives the surname twice and the man no first name or standing, and "old religion" is vague; Eliot's headnote to the Notes acknowledges the book, which is the handle a reader needs.
Corrected: <p>Card XII of the major arcana: a man hanging by one foot from a living tree, his face calm, a halo round his head. Eliot’s note says the card “fits my purpose in two ways: because he is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer, and because I associate him with the hooded figure in the passage of the disciples to Emmaus in Part V.” The Hanged God is from <i>The Golden Bough</i>, James Frazer’s study of myth and ritual, which Eliot’s notes also acknowledge; he is one of the sacrificed gods the book is about. Madame Sosostris does not find him: “I do not find / The Hanged Man” (lines 54–55).</p>
(The description of Frazer is mine; the scholar to confirm the wording.)
