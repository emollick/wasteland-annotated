# Round 2, reader, batch C1

## Accepted
petronius, dante-purg26, dante-purg5, whitman-lilacs, ezekiel, ecclesiastes, luke24, gotterdammerung, webster-devils-law-case, shakespeare-hamlet, virgil-aeneid1, milton-pl4, middleton-wbw (on wording; two matters of fact under Still unsettled), and baudelaire-au-lecteur, unchanged since round one.

These read clearly at one go, say what the source is and what happens in it, and end on a fact or a citation rather than a bow. Two idioms I let stand rather than chase: "cites the passage for the first Thames-daughter" (dante-purg5) and "cites the story for the picture over the mantel" (ovid-met6), where "cites X for Y" is a shorthand a reader can follow; and "have been heard again in" (ecclesiastes), which is mild. The "what" lines that changed in this batch are all accepted, including those of the records objected to below; every objection is to a note.

## Objections

### weston
Problem: The first sentence is about seventy-five words long, with a colon, a list of three and a relative clause hung on the third item; I read it twice to see where the list ended and what "which" referred to. "In 1956 he regretted" reads as if the regret were an event; it was said in a lecture. Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance. Following Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i>, she argued that the Grail stories preserved the remains of an ancient fertility cult: a king whose wound lays his land waste, a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics, and a rite of initiation descended from the mystery cults of Attis and Mithra. Those cults, she says, were carried across the Roman Empire by Syrian merchants, slaves and soldiers (chapter XII). Medievalists have not accepted the argument. Eliot's headnote says the book suggested "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem," and recommends it "to any who think such elucidation of the poem worth the trouble"; in a lecture of 1956 he said he regretted "having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail." The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel).

### frazer
Problem: "helped by ritual, a king sacrificed, an effigy buried or drowned" reads at first as three parallel things rather than ritual and two examples of it. "Have been read against the same rites" is critics' shorthand; a reader does not know what "read against" means. Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: Frazer's twelve-volume study of magic and religion, which Eliot's headnote says "has influenced our generation profoundly," argued that the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris, and by implication of Christ, tell one story, that of a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by rites in which a king is sacrificed or an effigy is buried or drowned. The two volumes Eliot names describe the "gardens of Adonis," pots of quick-sprouting seed carried out with images of the dead god and "flung with them into the sea," and the effigies of Osiris "made of earth and corn" that the priests buried so that grain would sprout from the god's body. Eliot's note on the Tarot says the Hanged Man "is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer"; the corpse planted in Stetson's garden (line 71) and Phlebas in the current (line 312) have been connected with the same rites. Frazer took his title and his opening page from Turner's painting <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1834), which he described as a view of Lake Nemi; it in fact shows Lake Avernus and the Sibyl of Cumae, the prophetess of the poem's epigraph, holding the bough that admits Aeneas to the underworld.

### dante-inf3
Problem: "'appear later': by his own account the ring of line 56 and the crowd of line 62 are one crowd" is a colon doing a verb's work, the tell the brief names. "Quotes lines 55–57 for the crowd" is compressed. Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: Inside the gate ("Abandon all hope") but before the river Acheron, Dante finds the souls who were neither good nor bad, "who lived without infamy and without praise," rejected by Heaven and refused by Hell, running for ever after a banner and stung by wasps. Eliot's note on line 63 quotes lines 55–57 as the source of the crowd flowing over London Bridge, and his note on line 46 says that the "crowds of people" in Madame Sosostris's cards "appear later," so that by his own account the ring of line 56 and the crowd of line 62 are the same crowd. Eliot said in 1950 that Dante's poetry was still, "after forty years," "the most persistent and deepest influence" on his own verse.

### dante-inf4
Problem: "holds the virtuous who died unbaptised, Homer, Virgil and Aristotle among them, the 'great spirits' (spiriti magni) who are not tormented" stacks three appositives and reads as four kinds of soul. "The comparison puts the sighs of the office crowd with those of Limbo" is not something a person would say. Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: Limbo, the first circle of Hell, holds the virtuous who died unbaptised, Homer, Virgil and Aristotle among them; Dante calls them the "great spirits" (<i>spiriti magni</i>), and they are not tormented but live in desire without hope. Eliot's note on line 64 cites these lines. The comparison likens the sighs of the office crowd to those of Limbo, where no one is punished, not to the cries of the damned.

### dante-inf33
Problem: "'Chiavar' has been read both as locking and as nailing up the door" assumes the reader can find the verb in the Italian and knows that the translation above has taken one side; a reader who has just read "nailing up" and then the poem's "key" is left to work out the connection. In the Bradley sentence "in which" sits so far from "a sentence" that it reads as if the doctoral thesis were being quoted. Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: Count Ugolino della Gherardesca of Pisa, imprisoned in 1288 with two sons and two grandsons and left to starve, gnaws in Hell on the skull of Archbishop Ruggieri, who betrayed him, and tells Dante the story. The verb in the passage, <i>chiavar</i>, has been read both as locking and as nailing up the door; the translation above has "nailing up," and Eliot's "key" takes it as locking. His note on line 412 quotes the passage and then a sentence from <i>Appearance and Reality</i> (1893) by F. H. Bradley, the philosopher on whom Eliot wrote his doctoral thesis; in it Bradley says that each person's experience "falls within my own circle, a circle closed on the outside."

### chaucer
Problem: The second sentence runs on into "and in which no one says, as Eliot's people do, that winter kept them warm," a negative built to land a point: the sentence that ends on a twist. Said straight, it is one fact about the poem. Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: The Prologue opens with April rain ending the drought of March, sap rising, birds singing through the night and people longing to go on pilgrimage. Eliot's notes say nothing of Chaucer; readers have long set "April is the cruellest month" against these lines, in which the same month and the same rain are welcome. In Eliot's poem it is winter that "kept us warm" (lines 5–6).

### isaiah
Problem: "Eliot's note on the line before refers him to Weston" sends the speaker, not the reader, to Weston. "A promise," dropped in as an appositive, dangles. The note ends on "and the poem gives no answer," which is a fact but placed as a bow; it sits better inside the sentence, with the citation last. Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: Eliot's notes do not cite Isaiah. The "shadow of a great rock in a weary land" of chapter 32 has been recognised behind the "shadow under this red rock" of line 25 since the earliest commentaries; in Isaiah the shadow is promised together with "rivers of water in a dry place," and the red rock of the poem gives shade but no water. In chapter 38 King Hezekiah, told to set his house in order because he will die, turns his face to the wall and weeps, and is granted fifteen more years. At line 426 the speaker, fishing on the shore, asks "Shall I at least set my lands in order?" and gets no answer; Eliot's note on the line before sends the reader to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King.

### tristan
Problem: Two small things. "The first thing sung in the opera, from the masthead, unseen, above the deck where Isolde lies" stacks phrases after a comma with "unseen" attached to nothing. "Wrote a poem called 'Opera,' dated November 1909, about it" trails its "about it." Everything else, the Ludwig sentence included, I accept now that it is plain. Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: Eliot's notes cite the libretto by act and verse: I, verses 5–8, and III, verse 24. The young sailor's song is the first thing sung in the opera; the sailor sings it unseen, from the masthead, above the deck where Isolde lies. The shepherd's "Öd und leer das Meer!" comes in Act III, answering Tristan's servant Kurwenal, who has asked whether any ship is in sight while Tristan lies wounded and waiting for Isolde. In the poem the hyacinth garden (lines 35–41) falls between the two quotations. Eliot heard <i>Tristan</i>, or its music in concert, in Boston in the autumn of 1909, at the start of his graduate year at Harvard, and wrote a poem about it, "Opera," dated November 1909; Stravinsky, who met him in 1956, thought <i>Tristan</i> "must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life." Ludwig II of Bavaria, whose money paid for the first performance, was found dead in 1886 in the Starnberger See, the lake of line 8.

### baudelaire-sept-vieillards
Problem: "The dead acquaintance hailed on King William Street" names no one (a reader sent here from line 60 does not know this is Stetson) and states as a fact that he is dead, which is a reading. The poem itself supplies the plain description: he is hailed as a shipmate from Mylae. Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: The poet, walking through a dirty yellow fog on a Paris morning, meets a hideous old man, then another exactly like him, and another, seven in all, and flees home half mad. Eliot's note on line 60 quotes the first two lines. Stetson, hailed on King William Street at nine in the morning as a shipmate from the battle of Mylae (lines 69–70), has been read as Eliot's version of Baudelaire's daylight ghost.

### webster-white-devil
Problem: Cornelia arrives with no word of who she is; the reader has to assemble the family from "her son," "his brother" and "his sister." "Fired what she took to be a loaded pistol at him" puts "at him" where it is hard to attach. Nothing cut; one relation made explicit (Cornelia is Vittoria's mother, which the note already implies).
Corrected:
note: Webster's tragedy is about Vittoria Corombona, the "white devil" of the title, and her lover Brachiano, who has her husband and his own wife murdered. Vittoria's mother Cornelia sings the dirge over her son Marcello, killed by his brother Flamineo; Flamineo makes the speech about widows in the last scene, rising from a feigned death after Vittoria has fired at him what she took to be a loaded pistol. Eliot's notes cite the dirge at line 74 and Flamineo's speech at line 408. At line 74 the poem turns Webster's wolf, "foe to men," into a Dog, "friend to men." Eliot's "Whispers of Immortality" (1918) begins "Webster was much possessed by death / And saw the skull beneath the skin."

### shakespeare-tempest
Problem: Two colons doing verbs' work ("The song is not true: Alonso is alive"; "the drowned stay drowned: the Sailor of line 47"), and "the drowned stay drowned" is a flourish. The sentence carrying three citations is heavy; I have split it. A reader who arrives from line 125 meets "his note on line 126" with no hint that the number is Eliot's, so I have said which pearls the note is about. Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: Ferdinand, washed ashore after the shipwreck, believes his father Alonso drowned; the song Ariel sings to him, unseen, describes a drowned man turned to coral and pearl. The song is not true. Alonso is alive, and the play ends with father and son reunited; in the poem the Phoenician Sailor of line 47 and Phlebas in Part IV stay drowned. Eliot's notes cite the scene at lines 192 and 257, and his note on line 126 refers the pearls of line 125 back to lines 37 and 48; his note on Tiresias says the Phoenician Sailor "is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples." Ferdinand's "the king my father's wreck" becomes "the king my brother's wreck" and "the king my father's death" for the fisherman on the canal bank behind the gashouse (lines 191–192).

### shakespeare-antony
Problem: "Taken from Froude" is a name with no word of who he is, the tell the brief names; Eliot's own note supplies the answer. "Follows its manner, blank verse and an inventory of gold, perfume and light, but moves the scene" leaves the appositive floating between the verbs. "Read alongside" is vaguer than "compared with." Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: Antony's officer Enobarbus describes Cleopatra's first appearance to Antony, in her barge on the river Cydnus; Eliot's note on line 77 cites the speech. The opening of Part II keeps the speech's manner, its blank verse and its inventory of gold, perfume and light, but moves the scene indoors, to a woman at her dressing table. The barge of Elizabeth and Leicester at line 279, for which Eliot's note cites the historian J. A. Froude, has been compared with this one.

### ovid-met6
Problem: "Bringing his wife Procne's sister Philomela to visit her when he rapes her" uses "her" for two different women in one clause; I stopped to sort out who was visited and who was raped. Only the first sentence changes. Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: Tereus, king of Thrace, is bringing his wife Procne's sister Philomela to visit her when he rapes Philomela in a hut deep in the woods and cuts out her tongue; she weaves the crime into a cloth and sends it to her sister; Procne kills her son Itys and serves him to his father; Tereus, pursuing the sisters with a sword, and the sisters, fleeing, are all turned into birds. Ovid does not say which sister became the nightingale and which the swallow; the later tradition that Eliot follows makes Philomela the nightingale. Eliot's note on line 99 cites the story for the picture over the mantel in Part II; the "Jug Jug" of lines 103 and 204 is the nightingale's song as Elizabethan poets wrote it; and his note on line 429 refers the swallow back to "Philomela in Parts II and III." Arthur Golding's translation of 1567, the one Shakespeare knew, gives the story in Book VI.

### ovid-met3
Problem: The note opens on a fragment ("Book III, lines 324–331 of the episode (320–338) that Eliot's note on line 218 quotes in Latin, calling it…") that carries two facts and has no main verb; the brief allows a bare locator, not a fragment this long. Nothing cut.
Corrected:
note: The passage is Book III, lines 324–331; Eliot's note on line 218 quotes the whole episode (lines 320–338) in Latin, calling it "of great anthropological interest." Jupiter and Juno, disputing in jest which sex takes more pleasure in love, put the question to Tiresias, who has been both; he sides with Jupiter, Juno strikes him blind, and Jupiter gives him foresight in return. Eliot's note calls Tiresias "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest," and says that "what Tiresias sees, in fact, is the substance of the poem."

Nothing else in the batch would I refuse to sign. The fourteen objections above are, in ten cases, a clause or a sentence; the ones that change what a reader understands are baudelaire-sept-vieillards, shakespeare-antony, isaiah, dante-inf33 and shakespeare-tempest.
