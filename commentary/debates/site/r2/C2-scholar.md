Points settled on the way:
- "Gayā Head, a hill near Gaya in Bihar" (buddha): right. Gayāsīsa is Brahmayoni Hill outside Gaya, Bihar; Warren's text has the Buddha and the thousand former matted-hair ascetics "at Gayā, on Gayā Head."
- "spiking the guns" (eliot-frontiers): Eliot's own words. The lecture reads "with a view to spiking the guns of critics of my earlier poems who had accused me of plagiarism." The money-back sentence is "anyone who bought my book of poems, and found that the notes to The Waste Land were not in it, would demand his money back"; the paraphrase is faithful.
- Marvell line citations: lines 185–186 are "But at my back in a cold blast I hear / The rattle of the bones"; 196–198 "But at my back from time to time I hear / The sound of horns and motors, which shall bring / Sweeney to Mrs. Porter in the spring." Both citations right; both lines begin "But at my back," as the note says.
- "bosom" for "gorge" (verlaine): right; in the poetic register "gorge" is a woman's breast, not her throat.
- "Gaffer Hexam and his daughter" (dickens): right. Our Mutual Friend, Book I ch. 1: Jesse "Gaffer" Hexam and Lizzie in a boat between Southwark Bridge and London Bridge, looking for bodies.
- Polypragmus, "a mere vainglorious reveller" (day): Day's own label for the Plush Bee, whose planned ceiling-show is the Actaeon passage. Twelve characters is the title page's own count.
- De Quadra's dispatch (froude): Froude, vol. VII ch. iv, gives it as De Quadra to Philip, 30 June 1561, and Eliot's note says "letter of De Quadra to Philip of Spain"; the record follows its source.

## Objections

### brihadaranyaka
Problem: The quoted gloss is misspelt. Eliot's 1922 note reads "(Give, sympathize, control)" with a z (Boni & Liveright 1922, Wikisource transcription "The Waste Land (Eliot, 1922)", page scan djvu/45ff.; Gutenberg #1321 likewise). Inside quotation marks it must match.
Corrected:
note: Eliot read the Upanishads in Sanskrit at Harvard with Charles Lanman in 1911–13. His note gives the reference as Brihadaranyaka 5, 1 (the passage is 5.2 in the standard numbering) and sends the reader to Paul Deussen's German translation of 1897. In the fable each group hears in the one syllable the command it needs. Eliot puts the three commands in a different order, glosses them in his note as "Give, sympathize, control," and follows each with a passage in which the speaker answers it. "Shantih," his last note says, is "a formal ending to an Upanishad," and "The Peace which passeth understanding" is "a feeble translation of the content of this word"; later editions soften this to "our equivalent to this word."

### london-bridge
Problem: "the only one of the four in English" is contradicted by the site's own tennyson record: line 429 ends with the English "O swallow swallow," which that record gives as a quotation from The Princess. The rhyme is the only whole line in English.
Corrected:
note: The rhyme runs through building materials that fail until a watchman is set. Folklorists of James Frazer's generation, Alice Gomme among them, connected it with the belief that a bridge was once made to stand by burying a victim in its foundations; Iona and Peter Opie, in <i>The Oxford Dictionary of Nursery Rhymes</i>, treat the connection with caution. Eliot's crowd crossed the real London Bridge at line 62. Here the rhyme opens the run of quotations at lines 427–430, the "fragments" of line 431; the others are in Italian, Latin and French, with three English words, "O swallow swallow," set in the Latin line.

### pervigilium-veneris

Wikipedia's summary: "variously assigned to the 2nd, 4th or 5th century." Change the date field, not the note.
Corrected:
date: 2nd–5th century AD, probably 4th
(note as rewritten stands.)

### verlaine-parsifal
Problem: In the translation (my own, from round one) "which all that tempts to love light breasts" makes the leaning the thing tempted; in the French ("de garçon vierge que cela tente") it is the boy who is tempted. One word. In the note, "(January 1886)" sits after "a Paris journal devoted to Wagner," where it reads as the journal's date; the sonnet appeared in the Revue wagnérienne of 8 January 1886.
Corrected:
trans: Parsifal has overcome the Maidens, their pretty chatter and amusing lust, and his own leaning toward the Flesh, a virgin boy's, whom all that tempts to love light breasts and that pretty chatter; he has overcome the beautiful Woman with the subtle heart, spreading her cool arms and her exciting bosom; he has overcome Hell and returns to his tent with a heavy trophy on his boyish arm, with the lance that pierced the supreme Side! He has healed the king, and here he is king himself, and priest of the most holy essential Treasure. In a robe of gold he adores, glory and symbol, the pure vessel where the real Blood shines. And, oh, those children's voices singing in the dome!
note: Verlaine's sonnet on Wagner's last opera, published in January 1886 in the <i>Revue wagnérienne</i>, a Paris journal devoted to Wagner, gives the plot in thirteen lines: the flower-maidens, Kundry, the recovered spear, the healed king. The fourteenth line is the boys' choir in the dome of the Grail temple. Eliot's note cites the sonnet. In the poem the line comes straight after the ballad about Mrs Porter, which Eliot's note says was "reported to me from Sydney, Australia." In Wagner, as in the romances Jessie Weston discusses in <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, the book Eliot's headnote cites, the knight heals the wounded king; the poem's Fisher King is still fishing at line 425, "with the arid plain behind me."

### spenser-prothalamion
Problem: "his river carries nothing" is more than the poem says. Lines 177–179 say the river bears no bottles, papers, boxes, cigarette ends "or other testimony of summer nights"; that it carries nothing at all is the old note's "empties the river" in new words.
Corrected:
note: A "spousal verse," as its title page calls it, for the double wedding of the Earl of Worcester's daughters, Elizabeth and Katherine Somerset. The poet, out of favour at court, walks by the Thames and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans, who stand for the brides. Eliot's note to line 176 cites the poem. He keeps Spenser's refrain; his nymphs are twice said to have departed (lines 175 and 179), and where Spenser's river carries the swans, his bears no litter of summer nights. Spenser's poet is himself unhappy, walking by the river to ease the "sullein care" of a fruitless wait at court.

### death-of-st-narcissus
Problem: "his own shadow on the sand or against the fire" misplaces the second shadow. In the poem the two shadows are the listener's shadow sprawling over the sand at daybreak and his shadow leaping behind the fire against the red rock (Poems Written in Early Youth, 1950; Facsimile, 1971, p. 91). "Against the fire" is not there.
Corrected:
note: Set in type for <i>Poetry</i> (Chicago) in 1915 and withdrawn in proof; first published in 1950. The poem is about a saint who is sure he had been a tree, a fish and a young girl, and who dies pierced with arrows. The manuscript survived among the papers Eliot sent in 1922 to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector, with the <i>Waste Land</i> drafts, and is now in the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library. After these two lines the speaker promises to show the listener something other than his own shadow, sprawling on the sand at daybreak or leaping behind the fire. <i>The Waste Land</i> keeps the second line, with "gray" changed to "red," and reshapes the first. The poem is still in copyright, so only its first two lines are given here.

### eliot-frontiers
Problem: Facts and quotations all check (see above). Two small things. "Boni &amp; Liveright" carries an HTML entity; the rest of sources.txt writes a plain "&" (e.g. the Dante record), so it will either print as "&amp;" or be the one escaped ampersand in the file. And Eliot's sentence says the plagiarism charge was made by "critics of my earlier poems"; the note's "critics who had called him a plagiarist" lets a reader think the charge was against The Waste Land.
Corrected:
note: A lecture given at the University of Minnesota on 30 April 1956, to an audience of about fourteen thousand, and printed in <i>On Poetry and Poets</i> (1957). Thirty-four years on, Eliot said he had meant only to list his references, with a view, in his words, to "spiking the guns" of critics who had called his earlier poems plagiarism; the poem's first printings, in the <i>Criterion</i> and the <i>Dial</i>, had no notes; when Boni & Liveright were to make it a book it was found too short, and he expanded the notes to fill the pages, with the result he called "bogus scholarship." He regretted sending readers on "a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail," and said the notes could not now be removed: a reader who bought his poems and found them missing would ask for his money back.
Source: "The Frontiers of Criticism" (1956), in On Poetry and Poets (1957), the paragraph beginning "Here I must admit that I am, on one conspicuous occasion, not guiltless"; Ackroyd, T. S. Eliot (1984), p. 317, for the audience.
