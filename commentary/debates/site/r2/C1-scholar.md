## Objections

### frazer
Problem: "Frazer took his title and his opening page from Turner's painting" is not established. The book is named for the bough of Aeneid VI as the Nemi legend uses it; Turner supplies the frontispiece and the first paragraph, not the name. The sentence also leaves out the frontispiece, which is the fact that makes the misidentification worth telling.
Corrected:
note: Frazer's twelve-volume study of magic and religion, which Eliot's headnote says "has influenced our generation profoundly," argued that the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris, and by implication of Christ, tell one story: a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by ritual, a king sacrificed, an effigy buried or drowned. The two volumes Eliot names describe the "gardens of Adonis," pots of quick-sprouting seed carried out with images of the dead god and "flung with them into the sea," and the effigies of Osiris "made of earth and corn" that the priests buried so that grain would sprout from the god's body. Eliot's note on the Tarot says the Hanged Man "is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer"; the corpse planted in Stetson's garden (line 71) and Phlebas in the current (line 312) have been read against the same rites. Frazer put a reproduction of Turner's painting <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1834) at the front of his first edition and opened his first page with it, describing it as a view of Lake Nemi; it in fact shows Lake Avernus and the Sibyl of Cumae, the prophetess of the poem's epigraph, holding the bough that admits Aeneas to the underworld.
Source: Frazer, The Golden Bough, ch. I, first paragraph ("Who does not know Turner's picture of the Golden Bough? ... the little woodland lake of Nemi"); Wikipedia, The Golden Bough (painting): frontispiece of the 1890 edition, Lake Avernus, exhibited 1834.

### petronius
Problem: "his dinner party fills most of what survives of the Satyricon" is false: the Cena is sections 26–78 of the 141 that survive, about a third. What is true, and what the reader wanted, is that it is the longest continuous episode. The rest of the note is right.
Corrected:
note: The speaker is Trimalchio, a freed slave grown enormously rich, boasting to his guests between courses; his dinner party is the longest continuous episode in what survives of the <i>Satyricon</i>. The Sibyl of Cumae, in Ovid's telling (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV), asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand and forgot to ask for youth to go with them; she foresees that she will shrink until only her voice is left. The typescript of the poem carried a different epigraph, from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, ending "The horror! the horror!"; Pound doubted that Conrad was weighty enough for the place, and Eliot put Petronius in its stead before publication.
Source: Satyricon, sections 26–78 of 141 surviving (Wikipedia, Satyricon); Ovid, Met. XIV.130–153; the 1971 facsimile; Pound to Eliot, January 1922.

### dante-inf3
Problem: "by his own account the ring of line 56 and the crowd of line 62 are one crowd" makes Eliot say what his note does not. The note says only that the "crowds of people" "appear later"; the crowd over London Bridge is the natural place to find them, but the hooded hordes of line 369 are crowds too. It is an inference and should be worded as one. (My own side proposed this sentence in round one; I withdraw it.)
Corrected:
note: Inside the gate ("Abandon all hope") but before the river Acheron, Dante finds the souls who were neither good nor bad, "who lived without infamy and without praise," rejected by Heaven and refused by Hell, running for ever after a banner and stung by wasps. Eliot's note on line 63 quotes lines 55–57 for the crowd over London Bridge, and his note on line 46 says that the "crowds of people" of Madame Sosostris's card "appear later," which has generally been taken to mean this crowd. Eliot said in 1950 that Dante's poetry was still, "after forty years," "the most persistent and deepest influence" on his own verse.
Source: Eliot's note on line 46, as quoted; "What Dante Means to Me" (1950).

### dante-inf4
Problem: "where no one is punished" is the wrong word. Dante's Limbo has grief without torment ("duol sanza martìri", IV.28), but its souls are lost and live "without hope in desire"; that is their punishment.
Corrected:
note: Limbo, the first circle of Hell, holds the virtuous who died unbaptised, Homer, Virgil and Aristotle among them, the "great spirits" (<i>spiriti magni</i>) who are not tormented but live in desire without hope. Eliot's note on line 64 cites these lines. The comparison puts the sighs of the office crowd with those of Limbo, where there is grief but no torment, rather than with the cries of the damned.
Source: Inferno IV.28, 42, 119.

### dante-inf33
Problem: Eliot's note quotes a passage of Bradley, four sentences with an omission, not "a sentence". Everything else checks.
Corrected:
note: Count Ugolino della Gherardesca of Pisa, imprisoned in 1288 with two sons and two grandsons and left to starve, gnaws in Hell on the skull of Archbishop Ruggieri, who betrayed him, and tells Dante the story. "Chiavar" has been read both as locking and as nailing up the door; Eliot's "key" takes it the first way. His note on line 412 quotes these lines and then a passage from <i>Appearance and Reality</i> (1893) by F. H. Bradley, the philosopher on whom Eliot wrote his doctoral thesis, in which each person's experience "falls within my own circle, a circle closed on the outside."
Source: Eliot's note on his line 411 (Gutenberg #1321).

### isaiah
Problem: "since the earliest commentaries" is a claim about the history of the criticism that I cannot source and the site did not check; "has long been recognised" is what can be defended. "Refers him to Weston's chapter" reads as if the note addressed the speaker; the note cites the chapter. (Both phrases were my side's in round one.)
Corrected:
note: Eliot's notes do not cite Isaiah. The "shadow of a great rock in a weary land" of chapter 32 has long been recognised behind the "shadow under this red rock" of line 25; in Isaiah the shadow comes with "rivers of water in a dry place," a promise, and the red rock gives shade but no water. In chapter 38 King Hezekiah, told to set his house in order because he will die, turns his face to the wall and weeps, and is granted fifteen more years. At line 426 the speaker, fishing on the shore, asks "Shall I at least set my lands in order?"; Eliot's note on the line before cites Weston's chapter on the Fisher King, and the poem gives no answer.
Source: Isaiah 32:1–2, 38:1–5; Eliot's note on his line 424.

### baudelaire-sept-vieillards
Problem: "The dead acquaintance" states a reading in the subject of a sentence that otherwise words the parallel as a reading. That Stetson is dead is inferred from Mylae and the undone crowd; the poem calls him "one I knew".
Corrected:
note: The poet, walking through a dirty yellow fog on a Paris morning, meets a hideous old man, then another exactly like him, and another, seven in all, and flees home half mad. Eliot's note on line 60 quotes the first two lines. Stetson, hailed in the crowd on King William Street at the stroke of nine, has been read as Eliot's version of Baudelaire's daylight ghost.
Source: lines 66–69.

### shakespeare-tempest
Problem: "his note on line 126": in the site's count "Those are pearls that were his eyes" is line 125 (the record's own lines field), and the site keys Eliot's notes to its own count elsewhere (412 for his 411, 408 for his 407). Eliot's "126" is his mis-keying, one ahead of the count from here to his "138" for the chess line at 137. The number should go, or be the site's.
Corrected:
note: Ferdinand, washed ashore after the shipwreck, believes his father Alonso drowned; the song Ariel sings to him, unseen, describes a drowned man turned to coral and pearl. The song is not true: Alonso is alive, and the play ends with father and son reunited. Eliot's notes cite the scene at lines 192 and 257, his note on the pearls in Part II (line 125) refers back to lines 37 and 48, and his note on Tiresias says the Phoenician Sailor "is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples." In the poem the drowned stay drowned: the Sailor of line 47 and Phlebas in Part IV. Ferdinand's "the king my father's wreck" becomes "the king my brother's wreck" and "the king my father's death" for the fisherman on the canal bank behind the gashouse (lines 191–192).
Source: the count from Gutenberg #1321; Eliot's notes 126, 192, 218, 257.

### shakespeare-hamlet
Problem: Ophelia's line follows "Ta ta. Goonight. Goonight." (line 171), not "Goonight Bill. Goonight Lou. Goonight May. Goonight." (170); the note quotes the wrong line as the one it follows without a break.
Corrected:
note: Ophelia, mad after her father's death, leaves the stage with these words; she comes back once more in the same scene, with flowers, and her drowning is reported by Gertrude in scene vii. Eliot's notes say nothing of the line, which follows the pub's goodnights of lines 170–171 ("Goonight Bill. Goonight Lou. Goonight May. Goonight. / Ta ta. Goonight. Goonight.") without a break and ends Part II.
Source: lines 170–172 (Gutenberg #1321); Hamlet IV.v and IV.vii.

### middleton-wbw
Problem: The what-line (unchanged) says "a rape upstairs" and the note says the Duke "takes" Bianca; the reader asked in round one for one word and the rewrite did not choose. The Duke's "I can command: think upon that" (II.ii) is coercion, and rape is the word the standard accounts use; I take the what-line's word. For the editor's information only: Eliot's own number for this note is 138 (the chess line is 137 in the count, as the record says), the same slip as his 126 for the pearls at 125; "Eliot's note on line 137" is right by the site's convention.
Corrected:
note: Livia, a rich widow acting as the Duke of Florence's procuress, keeps Bianca's mother-in-law at chess while the Duke rapes Bianca in the gallery above; her remarks on the game double as commentary on what is happening upstairs. Eliot's note on line 137 cites "the game of chess in Middleton's <i>Women beware Women</i>"; the title of Part II has also been connected with Middleton's <i>A Game at Chess</i> (1624), an anti-Spanish satire that ran for nine days at the Globe before the Privy Council stopped it. Eliot's essay on Middleton (1927) calls him "a great observer of human nature, without fear, without sentiment, without prejudice."
Source: Women Beware Women II.ii; Wikipedia, Women Beware Women ("He ultimately rapes Bianca"); Eliot's note 138; Eliot, "Thomas Middleton" (1927).
