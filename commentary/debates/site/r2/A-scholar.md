The points the editor asked me to settle:
- (a) drafts-lede. Vivien Eliot's two lines stand. In the typescript of Part II the typed "No, ma'am, you needn't look old-fashioned at me" is replaced in her hand by "If you dont like it you can get on with it" (site line 153) and "You want to keep him at home, I suppose" by "What you get married for if you dont want to have children" (164); so the facsimile transcript as I remember it, and so Mark Ford (NYRB, 20 October 2022) and Seymour-Jones, Painted Shadow (2002). "Roughly twice its published length" is accepted as the hedge every printed account allows (see Still unsettled for the figures). The rest of the paragraph checks: Pound in Paris in January 1922 and by letter after; Eliot's own cancellation of the Boston opening; the gift to Quinn (posted October 1922); Quinn's death 1924; the Berg Collection's purchase, April 1958; the announcement of 1968, three years after Eliot's death; the facsimile of 1971.
- (b) notes-head-text. The two phrases are from "The Frontiers of Criticism" (1956), but the rewrite attaches "inconveniently short" to the book; Eliot said it of the poem. Objection below.
- (c) legend-clock-text. The list is incomplete against the lens's own data. Objection below.
- (d) tarot-hanged-man-note. The gloss on Frazer is replaced with what the chapter is. Objection below.
- (e) tarot-crowd-in-a-ring-note. Right as it stands: line 369 is "Who are those hooded hordes swarming"; Eliot's note to line 63 cites Inferno iii. 55-7 (canto III); line 62 is six lines after 56.
- (f) legend-places-text. The note to 68 reads, in full, "A phenomenon which I have often noticed." Eliot was at Lloyds from March 1917 to November 1925. The site's own map puts the bank at 71 Lombard Street, some 250 metres from St Mary Woolnoth, and Eliot's department at 17 Cornhill was nearer still; "a short walk" holds. Accepted.
- (g) drafts-lede-2. The date is settled: Rainey (LRB, 3 December 2009) shows Paige misread "24 Saturnus" as 24 December in 1950, Kenner corrected it in 1973, the 1988 Letters repeated the error, and the 2009 edition gives 24 January 1922; the letter comments on a quasi-final version of the poem and opens "MUCH improved", which is the revised typescript sent from London. But the verses are headed, not signed, Sage Homme. Objection below.
- (h) about-quote-2 and about-quote-3. The attribution of the "rhythmical grumbling" remark is now right (the facsimile's own note: quoted by Theodore Spencer in a lecture at Harvard and recorded by Henry Ware Eliot Jr.), and two phrases keep it within the rule; accepted. about-quote-2 needs one change: the numeral in the dateline is not certain. Objection below.
- (i) legend-tongues-text. The Provençal is right: Eliot's note (numbered 427 in the 1922 printing, attached by the site's convention to line 428) quotes Purgatorio xxvi. 145-148, where Arnaut Daniel speaks Provençal. But "Eliot printed none of them in translation" is untrue of the Sanskrit. Objection below.
- (j) city-lede. Rennie's London Bridge opened on 1 August 1831 (John Rennie's design, built by his son); St Mary Woolnoth is Hawksmoor's (1716-27); St Magnus the Martyr is Wren's (1671-87), at the north foot of the old bridge, and Eliot's note to 264 calls its interior "one of the finest among Wren's interiors"; lines 212-213 have Eugenides ask the speaker to luncheon at the Cannon Street Hotel. Accepted. One wording point for the reader: the poem's crowd flows "down King William Street" to the church, so "at the top of King William Street" may puzzle; the church stands at the Bank end, which is the north end.
- (k) world-lede. Ludwig II of Bavaria was found drowned in the lake on 13 June 1886; "Starnbergersee" is the 1922 spelling (line 8, checked). But the paragraph misdescribes the map it sits over. Objection below.

Rights: Eliot's post-1929 words appear only in notes-head-text (two phrases of 1956), about-quote-3 (two phrases) and about-quote-4, which quotes a whole sentence of a letter first published in 1968; see the objection. Pound's "Caesarean Operation" lines are two short lines, within the allowance for his letters.

## Objections

### world-lede
Problem: The line the map draws (the path of class "journey" in map.html) begins at Cumae, which only the epigraph names, and ends at Himavant (line 398), not the Ganges; on the way it takes in places the poem never names but Eliot's notes do (Sydney, note to 199; Siena and Maremma, note to 293; Quebec, note to 357), so "the places the poem names" and "the lake is the first place named" both contradict what is drawn. The poem also goes on naming places after Ganga: Himavant (398), London Bridge (427), Aquitaine (430).
Corrected: The places named in the poem and its epigraph, with a few more from Eliot’s notes, joined by a line in the order they come in the text, from Cumae in the epigraph to Himavant in Part V. The Starnbergersee, near Munich, is the first place the poem itself names (line 8); Ludwig II of Bavaria drowned in it in 1886.

### drafts-lede-2
Problem: "Sage Homme" is the heading Pound typed over the verses, not a signature: the letter of 24 January 1922 (Selected Letters of Ezra Pound, ed. Paige, 1950) carries a 48-line poem headed SAGE HOMME. The quotation "on each Occasion / Ezra performed the Caesarean Operation" is right, capitals and all.
Corrected: Pound’s own account is in the letter of 24 January 1922 answering the revised typescript Eliot had sent from London. The verses in it are headed <i>Sage Homme</i>, man-midwife, and say that “on each Occasion / Ezra performed the Caesarean Operation.”

### listen-lede
Problem: The "pages" group in listen.txt holds five records: the first edition of 1922 (Duke's copy), the fourth printing of April 1930 (Brigham Young's copy), The Dial of November 1922, the typescript in the Berg Collection, and Frazer's Adonis, Attis, Osiris (1906). "The 1922 printings page by page" leaves out two of them.
Corrected: Recordings of the poem, beginning with Eliot’s own, the music it quotes, the song of the bird in Eliot’s note to line 357, and the early printings page by page. Nothing is copied onto this site: each recording plays from the library, archive or broadcaster that holds it, and can be opened there.

### listen-pages-lede
Problem: The same: the caption names the 1922 printings and the manuscript, and the group also holds the 1930 printing and Frazer's volume (listen.txt, records bl-1922-first-edition-archive-byu and frazer-adonis-attis-osiris-1906).
Corrected: The printings of 1922 and 1930, page by page; where the manuscript is; and the Frazer volume Eliot’s headnote names.

### about-quote-2
Problem: The numeral of the dateline is not settled: the sources I could reach print "24 Saturnus, An I" (Roman) as well as "An 1", and Rainey (LRB, 3 December 2009) quotes only "24 Saturnus". Quote what is certain.
Corrected: “Complimenti, you bitch. I am wracked by the seven jealousies.” Ezra Pound to Eliot, 24 January 1922, answering the revised typescript. Pound dated the letter “24 Saturnus,” in a calendar of his own devising, and it was long thought to be from December 1921.

### about-quote-4
Problem: Rights. The letter was written in October 1923 but first published in 1968 (Russell's Autobiography, vol. 2) and again in the Letters (1988); the brief's rule goes by publication (it counts the draft passages of 1914-21 as post-1929 because they were published in 1971) and names Eliot's letters, so a whole sentence is a passage. The editor's decision rested on the date of writing.
Corrected: Part V was, Eliot wrote, “not only the best part, but the only part that justifies the whole, at all.” Eliot to Bertrand Russell, October 1923.

### notes-head-text
Problem: In "The Frontiers of Criticism" (1956; in On Poetry and Poets, 1957) it is the poem, not the book, that was short: "when it came to print The Waste Land as a little book ... it was discovered that the poem was inconveniently short, so I set to work to expand the notes, in order to provide a few more pages of printed matter, with the result that they became the remarkable exposition of bogus scholarship that is still on view today." The rewrite attaches the phrase to the book.
Corrected: Eliot’s notes were first printed in the book edition of December 1922; the poem had appeared in <i>The Criterion</i> and <i>The Dial</i> without them. In 1956 Eliot said the poem had been found “inconveniently short” for a book, so he had expanded the notes to fill it out, and he called the result “bogus scholarship.” Each appears here beside its lines, and all of them <a href="#notes">after the poem</a>. Gold dots mark the lines that have one.

### legend-tongues-text
Problem: "Eliot printed none of them in translation" is untrue of the Sanskrit: his note numbered 401 glosses “Datta, dayadhvam, damyata” as “(Give, sympathize, control)”, and the note numbered 433 offers “The Peace which passeth understanding” for “Shantih” (1922 notes, checked). The rest is right.
Corrected: Latin and Greek in the epigraph; German, French, Italian, Latin and Sanskrit in the poem; Provençal in Eliot’s note on line 428, which quotes Arnaut Daniel’s speech in Dante. The cries of birds, the bells and the river’s song are marked as well. Click any coloured phrase for a translation. Eliot’s notes translate only the Sanskrit, the thunder’s three words and “Shantih”; the other translations here were made for this edition.

### legend-clock-text
Problem: The sentence reads as the list of "the seasons it names" but omits five of the lens's own entries (data.js lists fifteen, among them "go south in the winter" at 18, "summer nights" at 179, "a winter evening" at 190, "in the spring" at 198, and the frosts at 73 and 323), and "are autumn without the word" states an inference as a fact. The full list is printed on the card under "Seasons", so the paragraph need not enumerate. The word autumn does not occur in the poem; the last season named is at line 327 (checked against the text).
Corrected: The hours the poem names, set on a clock face, and below them the seasons it names, from April in the first line to the “thunder of spring over distant mountains” of Part V (line 327). Spring, summer and winter are named; autumn is only implied, by the last leaves of Part III (line 173).

### tarot-phoenician-sailor-note
Problem: Misquotation. The note to line 46 reads "The Phoenician Sailor and the Merchant appear later; also the “crowds of people,” and Death by Water is executed in Part IV": the verb is plural, and "appears later" is not in the note. (My round-one text had the same slip.)
Corrected: <p>Not a card of any Tarot pack. Eliot’s note to line 46 says that the Phoenician Sailor and the Merchant “appear later” and that “Death by Water is executed in Part IV”: the Sailor is Phlebas. The note to line 218 adds that the one-eyed merchant “melts into the Phoenician Sailor, and the latter is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples.” Line 48, “Those are pearls that were his eyes,” is from Ariel’s song in <i>The Tempest</i>, sung to Ferdinand about his father, who is thought drowned.</p>

### tarot-blank-note
Problem: Weston does not say the cult was "carried into Europe by Syrian merchants". In From Ritual to Romance, ch. XII ("Mithra and Attis"), she says the Attis and Mithra cults "travelled to the furthest borders of the Roman Empire" by three channels: "First, commercial, through the medium of Syrian merchants"; second, Asiatic slaves and officials; "Thirdly, and most important", the soldiers. The reading that puts the merchant's burden in that light is Cleanth Brooks's ("The Waste Land: Critique of the Myth", 1939): "The Syrian merchants, we learn from Miss Weston's book, were, along with slaves and soldiers, the principal carriers of the mysteries which lie at the core of the Grail legends."
Corrected: <p>“This card, / Which is blank, is something he carries on his back, / Which I am forbidden to see” (lines 52–54). Madame Sosostris cannot read it, and the poem never says what it is. Jessie Weston, in <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, says that the mystery cults she traces the Grail legend to were carried across the Roman Empire by Syrian merchants, among others, and the merchant’s unseen burden has been read in that light, by Cleanth Brooks in 1939 and by others since.</p>

### tarot-hanged-man-note
Problem: "His study of the sacrificed gods of old religion" is not what The Golden Bough is (its subtitle is A Study in Magic and Religion) and does not say what Frazer's Hanged God is. "The Hanged God" is a chapter of Adonis, Attis, Osiris (third edition, 1914, vol. i, Book II, ch. V), the volumes Eliot's headnote names; from Marsyas hanged on Cybele's pine, Odin on the gallows-tree and hanged human victims Frazer conjectures that "the priest who bore the name and played the part of Attis at the spring festival of Cybele was regularly hanged or otherwise slain upon the sacred tree" (checked in the Gutenberg text of the third edition, vol. 5). The old gloss, "the sacrificed king whose death brings the rain", was the site's and not Frazer's, and is rightly gone. The quotation from Eliot's note is exact.
Corrected: <p>Card XII of the major arcana: a man hanging by one foot from a living tree, his face calm, a halo round his head. Eliot’s note says the card “fits my purpose in two ways: because he is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer, and because I associate him with the hooded figure in the passage of the disciples to Emmaus in Part V.” The Hanged God is a chapter of Frazer’s <i>The Golden Bough</i>, in the volumes <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i> that Eliot’s headnote names, on gods, and men standing for gods, put to death by hanging on a sacred tree. Madame Sosostris does not find him: “I do not find / The Hanged Man” (lines 54–55).</p>

## Still unsettled
- The typescript's length (drafts-lede). No count was made from the facsimile, which I do not have. The accounts disagree: "800 lines" (Encyclopedia.com and the like, which is where the old text's figure came from), "about a thousand lines" (popular accounts, which seem to count the separate poems bound with the drafts), "almost twice as much material" (older Wikipedia). By my reckoning of the facsimile's parts (I about 130 lines, II about 100, III about 250, IV 93, V about 115) the five parts alone come to something under 700, so "roughly twice" is on the generous side but agrees with every printed account. Keep it, or count from the facsimile if the editor has it.
- "An 1" / "An I" in Pound's dateline: not verified; the sources split; the correction to about-quote-2 drops it.
- Vivien Eliot's two lines: verified through secondary sources and my memory of the facsimile transcript, not against the facsimile itself. The evidence is consistent and I sign off on "two lines".
- about-quote-1: the date checks (the entry is Friday 23 June 1922; the dinner was Sunday 18 June). The brief's rule does not cover it (not Eliot's words), but the diary was first published in 1978 and three sentences are quoted; the editor's call, as in round one.
- Bayreuth on the world map's line (line 277): named neither in the poem nor in Eliot's notes, so even the corrected world-lede does not account for it. For the map thread: drop it from the line or say why it is there.
- The rest of the pathway introductions in paths.txt (batch elsewhere) repeat the world-lede's error ("It begins in Munich and ends on the Ganges"); noted for whoever holds that batch.
- Pound's marks in pencil and ink, and his "Bad" across Part IV: still not checked against the facsimile (batch B's business); nothing in this batch now depends on them.
