# Round 2, reader, batch E

Read as the reader: a person has clicked a card and wants to know what it shows, which lines it belongs to and where it comes from. Every line number and quotation in the NEW texts was checked against the site's numbered text (112 has the full stop; 429 reads "ceu"; 41, 98, 115, 118, 137, 209–210, 289, 320, 412–413, 419–420 and 425 are as quoted). On the live page a pack card's note renders as one paragraph in small type under the card's name, so length was judged against that.

## Accepted
belladonna SHOWS; wheel; one-eyed-merchant SHOWS; blank; hanged-man SHOWS; back; hyacinth-girl; madame-sosostris; philomel SHOWS; bad-nerves; eugenides; tiresias; typist; thames-daughters; elizabeth-and-leicester; road-with-no-water; the-third; thunder SHOWS; and all six vignette titles (Elizabeth and Leicester below the white towers; Trams and dusty trees at Highbury; The crowd on London Bridge; A barge with red sails on Greenwich reach; A narrow canoe by Richmond; The dull canal behind the gashouse).

These are clear at one reading, say what is on the card and where it comes from, and end on a fact. The studio remarks, the file names and the winks are gone. "Hence the snakes at his feet" (tiresias) is a short plain sentence, not a bow; I let it stand.

## Objections

### phoenician-sailor
Problem: The last sentence is still built as a paired antithesis with a colon doing a verb's work ("That is the drawing's suggestion, not Eliot's:") and ends on a turn ("and says nothing of the Sailor"). Frazer is a name with no word of what he wrote.
Corrected: 47–48, "Is your card, the drowned Phoenician Sailor, / (Those are pearls that were his eyes. Look!)", and Part IV, Death by Water (312–321). The pearls are from Ariel's song in <i>The Tempest</i> (I. ii). He is drawn head down, in the posture of the Tarot's Hanged Man, the card Madame Sosostris does not find (54–55). The likeness is the artist's. Eliot's note on 46 connects the Hanged Man with the Hanged God of Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i> and with the hooded figure of Part V (360–366), and does not mention the Sailor.

### belladonna (ANSWERS; SHOWS stands)
Problem: "Pater's page on the Mona Lisa" is an odd word for it, and in the last sentence "a poison" reads as a third thing between the plant and the drops, so the list has to be read twice.
Corrected: 49–50, "Here is Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks, / The lady of situations." "The Lady of the Rocks" recalls Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i> and Walter Pater's description of the Mona Lisa in <i>The Renaissance</i> (1873): "She is older than the rocks among which she sits." <i>Belladonna</i> is Italian for "beautiful lady". It is also the name of deadly nightshade, a poisonous plant, and of the eye-drops made from it, which women once used to widen their pupils.

### three-staves
Problem: "Eliot's note:" is a colon doing a verb's work, and the gloss on the Fisher King is hung on the end of a long quotation, so the sentence has to be unpicked. The Rider pack is named without the word "tarot", which is the one word that tells a reader what it is.
Corrected: 51, "Here is the man with three staves". Eliot's note says: "The Man with Three Staves (an authentic member of the Tarot pack) I associate, quite arbitrarily, with the Fisher King himself." The Fisher King is the wounded king of the Grail legend, whose land lies waste. The drawing gives him a crutch for the wound, a rod to fish with, and a staff set in the dry ground, which has begun to sprout. The composition follows the Three of Wands of the Rider tarot pack (1909), drawn by Pamela Colman Smith, where a man with his back turned stands on a height looking out over the sea at ships.

### one-eyed-merchant (ANSWERS; SHOWS stands)
Problem: Small. The words quoted are line 210 alone, but the citation is (209–210); a reader sent to 209 finds "Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant". Quote both lines and the citation is right.
Corrected: 52, "the one-eyed merchant". Eliot's note on 218 speaks of "the one-eyed merchant, seller of currants", who "melts into the Phoenician Sailor". The seller of currants is Mr. Eugenides, "the Smyrna merchant / Unshaven, with a pocket full of currants" (209–210), so the card draws him as Eugenides.

### hanged-man (ANSWERS; SHOWS stands)
Problem: The middle sentence runs to sixty words with two quotations and two glosses nested in it; it is not readable at one go. Pamela Colman Smith is a name with no word of who she is on this card (she is explained only on three-staves), and "The Golden Bough" is not said to be Frazer's book.
Corrected: 54–55, "I do not find / The Hanged Man." Eliot's note on 46 says the card "fits my purpose in two ways". It is "associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer", the sacrificed god of Frazer's book <i>The Golden Bough</i>, killed so that the land may live again. Eliot also associates him with "the hooded figure in the passage of the disciples to Emmaus in Part V" (360–366), where the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples (Luke 24). The walkers on the road and the shoot on the dead bough come from that note. The head-down pose, the crooked leg and the halo are from the Hanged Man of the Rider tarot pack (1909), drawn by Pamela Colman Smith.

### crowd-in-a-ring
Problem: "follows at 62–65" and "refer those lines to the crowds of the dead" are not how a person says it. Eliot's notes say "Cf.", which is "compare".
Corrected: 56, "I see crowds of people, walking round in a ring." A few lines later a crowd flows over London Bridge (62–65); Eliot's notes on 63 and 64 compare it with the crowds of the dead in Dante's <i>Inferno</i> (cantos iii and iv).

### sibyl
Problem: Trimalchio is a name with no word of who he is. "How she came to it" leaves "it" to be guessed. The note ends on "and she did not ask for youth", which reads as a twist rather than the plain fact it is.
Corrected: The epigraph, from Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i> (chapter 48). Trimalchio, the rich host of the dinner party in the book, says he saw the Sibyl at Cumae with his own eyes, hanging in a jar, and that when the boys asked her "Sibyl, what do you want?" she answered "I want to die." How she came to that state is told in Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> xiv: Apollo granted her as many years of life as there were grains in a handful of sand, and she had forgotten to ask for youth to go with them.

### marie
Problem: Valerie Eliot is a name with no word of who she is. The one sentence carries the identification, the dates, the aunt, the memoir and "whom Eliot had met", and then "the sledding, she says, was taken from his conversation with her" has two women in it and a reader has to work out which "she" is which.
Corrected: 13–18, "Marie, / Marie, hold on tight. And down we went. / In the mountains, there you feel free." Eliot's widow, Valerie Eliot, in her 1971 facsimile edition of the drafts, identifies Marie with the Countess Marie Larisch (1858–1940), a niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria and author of a memoir, <i>My Past</i> (1913). Eliot had met the Countess, and the sledding, Valerie Eliot says, was taken from his conversation with her.

### philomel (ANSWERS; SHOWS stands)
Problem: "and in the telling Eliot follows she is turned into a nightingale" is the scholar's hedge welded into the sentence where it cannot be parsed at one reading. The hedge is kept; it gets its own sentence.
Corrected: 97–103, "The change of Philomel, by the barbarous king / So rudely forced; yet there the nightingale / Filled all the desert with inviolable voice". Eliot's note refers to Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> vi, where Tereus, king of Thrace, rapes his wife's sister Philomela and cuts out her tongue. In the telling Eliot follows, she becomes the nightingale.

### fisher-king
Problem: "Headnote" is an editor's word a reader will not know. The last sentence strings three clauses on a semicolon and an "and", and "attaches the king to" is not a thing a person says.
Corrected: 187–195, "While I was fishing in the dull canal / On a winter evening round behind the gashouse". The Fisher King is the wounded king of the Grail legend, whose land lies waste until he is healed. The opening paragraph of Eliot's notes says the poem's title, plan and much of its symbolism came from Jessie L. Weston's book on the legend, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920). His note on line 425, "Fishing, with the arid plain behind me", sends the reader to her chapter on the Fisher King, and his note on 46 links the king to the Man with Three Staves, a card of Madame Sosostris's reading.

### thunder (ANSWERS; SHOWS stands)
Problem: Small. "The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad" is a name with no word of what it is; four words mend it. The note is the longest in the pack (108 words) but every clause explains an emblem on the card, so nothing is cut.
Corrected: 396–423, "Ganga was sunken, and the limp leaves / Waited for rain, while the black clouds / Gathered far distant, over Himavant". Ganga is the Ganges and Himavant the Himalaya. The thunder then speaks three times, "DA", and each time the speaker hears in it one of three Sanskrit words, <i>Datta</i>, <i>Dayadhvam</i>, <i>Damyata</i>, which Eliot's note translates "give, sympathise, control" and traces to a fable in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, one of the ancient Hindu scriptures. The key on the bank is for <i>Dayadhvam</i> ("I have heard the key / Turn in the door once", 412–413); the boat is for <i>Damyata</i> ("The boat responded / Gaily, to the hand expert with sail and oar", 419–420).

### london-bridge-falling
Problem: Small. "The bridge standing, with the crowd on it, is the card" makes a bridge a card; it is on the card.
Corrected: 427, "London Bridge is falling down falling down falling down", the nursery rhyme. The same bridge, still standing and with the crowd on it, is on the card The Crowd on London Bridge (60–68).

### swallow
Problem: Two things. The quotation prints a closed dash ("chelidon—O swallow") where the site's text at 429 has a spaced one ("chelidon — O swallow"); the card should match the text it points to. And "the nightingale's song of Tereus's crime" drops Tereus with no word of who he is; on this card a reader has not been told the Philomela story, so the note should say where it is told.
Corrected: 429, "Quando fiam ceu chelidon — O swallow swallow". The Latin is from the last stanza of the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i> ("The Vigil of Venus"), an anonymous Latin poem of late antiquity: "when shall I be as the swallow, that I may cease to be silent?" The question comes after the nightingale has sung of the wrong done her by Tereus, the story of the card Philomel (97–103); Eliot's note adds "Cf. Philomela in Parts II and III". In the usual telling of that story Philomela's sister Procne became the swallow. "O swallow swallow" echoes a song in Tennyson's <i>The Princess</i> (1847), "O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South".

### tower
Problem: Small. Nerval is named with no word of who he is (two words mend it), and "trump XVI" is exact but opaque to a reader who does not know that the picture cards of a tarot pack are called trumps.
Corrected: 430, "Le Prince d'Aquitaine à la tour abolie" (the Prince of Aquitaine of the ruined tower), the second line of <i>El Desdichado</i> ("The Disinherited"), a sonnet by the French poet Gérard de Nerval, collected in his <i>Les Chimères</i> (1854); Eliot's note cites it. The star, the lute and the black sun on the card are from the same sonnet: "ma seule étoile est morte" (my only star is dead) and "mon luth constellé / Porte le soleil noir de la Mélancolie" (my star-strewn lute bears the black sun of Melancholy). The Tower is also a card of the real Tarot pack, trump XVI, where it is shown struck by lightning.

## Still unsettled
- What the live page shows. I read the site's poem.js: the SHOWS and ANSWERS fields are rendered only for the twenty-seven cards of "the rest of the pack". For the nine cards of Madame Sosostris's reading (phoenician-sailor, belladonna, three-staves, wheel, one-eyed-merchant, blank, hanged-man, crowd-in-a-ring) and for the back, a turned card shows instead the "tarot-*-note" and "tarot-*-deck" records in commentary/site/ui.txt, and those -deck records still carry the sentences cut here ("the only kind of one-eyed man a printed card can have", "gone into the water instead", "Something is there and cannot be read. Hold the pointer over it", "Round the head that is not there"). Unless whoever owns ui.txt takes the same changes, this batch's work on those nine cards changes nothing a reader sees. Not mine to fix; it needs saying.
- Length. Eight ANSWERS fields now run to 89–108 words, two and a half to three times their old length, and each is one small-type paragraph. I have not cut them, because every sentence carries a fact or a source the scholar asked for, and I would sign off on them as they are; but they are at the limit, and nothing more should be added to thunder, hanged-man, three-staves or tower.
- swallow's dash. Matching the site's spaced dash at 429 is my call on wording, not a change of fact; noted so the scholar does not take it for one.
- philomel. "In the telling Eliot follows, she becomes the nightingale" keeps the scholar's hedge in the scholar's sense (Ovid does not say plainly which sister becomes which bird). If the scholar would rather the note said that outright, it is theirs to add.
