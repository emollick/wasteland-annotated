## Objections

### water
Problem: The sentence is framed "from the spring rain … to the rain at line 395" and then lists, after the colon, only things that come before 395, so the frame and the list pull against each other; and the walk does not end at 395: its last stops are the boat at 419 and the fisherman on the shore at 424.
Corrected: The passages where water appears, in order, from the spring rain of the opening lines to the shore at the end of the poem (line 424): the shower over the Starnbergersee, Madame Sosostris’s warning “Fear death by water,” the Thames of Part III, the drowning of Phlebas in Part IV, the rock without water at the start of Part V, and the rain that comes at line 395. Eliot’s headnote to his notes credits Jessie L. Weston’s book on the Grail legend, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, one chapter of which is called “The Freeing of the Waters.”

### dead
Problem: "In order:" followed by four items with no "and" reads as a stacked list, and as if those four were the whole of a fifteen-stop walk.
Corrected: The poem’s dead, in the order they appear, among them the crowd on London Bridge (lines 62–63), the corpse planted in the garden (71), Phlebas in Part IV and the “Dry bones” of line 391. Part I takes its title, The Burial of the Dead, from the burial service in the Book of Common Prayer.

### cities
Problem: The poem never names Munich (lines 8–10 name the Starnbergersee and the Hofgarten), so "the places the poem names … Munich" is wrong at its first item; "the City" on its own means nothing to a reader outside Britain; and a list that promises the poem's order puts the five cities of lines 375–376 after the Ganges of 396.
Corrected: The places the poem names, in the order it names them: the Starnbergersee and Munich’s Hofgarten at the start; London through the middle, with the bridge, the streets and churches of the City of London, the pubs and the river from Richmond to Greenwich; in Part V the five cities named in two lines, “Jerusalem Athens Alexandria / Vienna London” (375–376); and near the end the Ganges and Himavant.

### nothing
Problem: "At lines 120–126, between the woman with bad nerves and the man" reads as a place on the page, not an exchange.
Corrected: The word “nothing” wherever the poem uses it: at line 40, by the one who came back from the hyacinth garden and could not speak; at lines 120–126, in the exchange between the woman with bad nerves and the man who does not answer her; and at lines 302 and 305, by the third Thames-daughter on Margate Sands.

### eyes
Problem: The list runs in line order except that the one-eyed merchant (52) comes after London Bridge (65).
Corrected: Eyes in the poem: the eyes that fail in the hyacinth garden (line 39), the pearls that were the drowned man’s eyes (48 and 125), the one-eyed merchant (52), the eyes each man fixes before his feet on London Bridge (65), the “lidless eyes” of line 138, and the blind Tiresias (218). Eliot’s note on Part V names the journey to Emmaus, where, in Luke 24, the disciples’ eyes “were holden,” held, so that they did not know who walked beside them.

### eliot
Problem: "From Eliot’s life in 1921 and 1922" does not describe the walk a reader then takes: its first stop is Munich in August 1911, another is the bank he joined in 1917, and two are the summers of his boyhood off Cape Ann. The dates I have kept are the site's own.
Corrected: Places and dates from Eliot’s life that lie behind particular lines of the poem, among them Munich in August 1911 (line 8) and, in the last months of 1921, Margate Sands (300) and the waters of Leman (182), each set beside the lines it bears on.

### epigraph-draft
Problem: "Trimalchio" arrives with no word of who he is, in the one sentence a reader most needs to follow.
Corrected: The title page of the typescript carries, under “The Waste Land,” a passage from Conrad’s <i>Heart of Darkness</i> ending with the dying Kurtz’s words, “The horror! the horror!” Pound wrote, on 24 January 1922, that he doubted Conrad was “weighty enough to stand the citation”; Eliot answered that it was “much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative”; Pound then told him to do as he liked about Conrad. Eliot nevertheless replaced it with the sentence from Petronius’s <i>Satyricon</i> in which Trimalchio, the host of the dinner, says that he saw the Sibyl of Cumae hanging in a jar, and that when the boys asked her what she wanted she answered, “I want to die.” The Sibyl had been granted long life, but not youth.

### prophet
Problem: "His note on line 23" comes straight after "a voice speaking as the prophets do", so "his" reads as the voice's; and "the prophets" wants "Old Testament" for a reader who does not know which prophets are meant.
Corrected: Lines 19–30: a voice speaking as the Old Testament prophets do, addressing the reader as “Son of man” (Ezekiel’s phrase, as Eliot’s note says) and offering the shade of the red rock. Eliot’s note on line 23 cites Ecclesiastes; “the shadow of this red rock” has been linked to Isaiah 32:2.

### lover
Problem: Two colons in one line, each doing a verb's work.
Corrected: The one who came back from the hyacinth garden and could not speak. Lines 37–41: “I could not / Speak, and my eyes failed.”

### he
Problem: Webster and Middleton are surnames with no word of what they are, and "That Shakespearian Rag" is a title with none.
Corrected: Lines 115–116, 118, 120, 124–125, 127–130 and 135–138, printed without quotation marks, which is usually read as meaning that he does not speak aloud. Some of his replies are other people’s words: the playwrights Webster (118) and Middleton (137–138), both cited in Eliot’s notes; Ariel’s song from The Tempest (125); and the song “That Shakespearian Rag” (128–130).

### fisher
Problem: The first sentence carries a relative clause, a participle and a three-item list, and a reader has lost the subject by the time the list starts. Split it; nothing else changes.
Corrected: The speaker by the river in Part III, lines 173–198, who repeats Spenser’s refrain “Sweet Thames, run softly” and fishes in the dull canal behind the gashouse. He speaks partly in others’ lines: Spenser’s (176, 183–184), the Psalmist’s “I sat down and wept” (182), and Ferdinand’s in The Tempest (191–192, Eliot’s note). That he is the Fisher King of the Grail legend is a reading; Eliot’s notes name the Fisher King at lines 46 and 425.

### nightingale
Problem: "Philomela" here, "Philomel" in the poem (line 99), on the women and birds pathways and everywhere else on the site; a first-time reader will take them for two people.
Corrected: Lines 203–206: the nightingale’s song as the Elizabethan poets wrote it, “jug jug” and “Tereu,” calling on Tereus, the king who raped Philomel; her story is told at lines 99–103, and “So rudely forc’d” repeats line 100.

### thames1
Problem: "La Pia" is a name with no word of who she is.
Corrected: Highbury, Richmond and Kew: lines 292–295. Eliot’s note sends the reader to Dante’s Purgatorio, canto V, where one of the dead, La Pia, says: “Siena made me, Maremma unmade me.”

### ascetic
Problem: The brackets are hard to parse at one reading ("307, and 309–310"), and "collocation", outside the quotation marks, is a word a reader has to look up.
Corrected: Lines 307–311: Augustine’s Confessions (307 and 309–310, beginning “To Carthage then I came”) set beside the Buddha’s Fire Sermon (308 and 311, “Burning”). Eliot’s note calls them “two representatives of eastern and western asceticism” and says that setting them side by side “is not an accident.”

### seer
Problem: "Blick ins Chaos" is a title with no word of what it is.
Corrected: Lines 367–395, from “What is that sound high in the air” to “Bringing rain.” Eliot’s note on the hooded hordes cites Hermann Hesse’s book Blick ins Chaos.

### fisherking
Problem: "Weston" is a surname with no word of who she is, on the one card whose label the note itself calls a reading; the other records spell her out.
Corrected: Lines 424–426: “I sat upon the shore / Fishing, with the arid plain behind me.” Eliot’s note on line 425 refers to the chapter on the Fisher King in Jessie L. Weston’s book on the Grail legend, From Ritual to Romance; the poem does not name the speaker.

### fragments
Problem: "The Latin Pervigilium Veneris" and "Kyd’s Spanish Tragedy" say what neither is; one word each.
Corrected: Lines 427–432, a line each: the nursery rhyme “London Bridge is falling down” (427); Dante’s Purgatorio, canto XXVI (428); the Latin poem Pervigilium Veneris (429); Nerval’s sonnet “El Desdichado” (430); the poem’s own line, “These fragments I have shored against my ruins” (431); and Kyd’s play The Spanish Tragedy (432). Eliot’s notes give the sources of all but the nursery rhyme.

### 1917
Problem: "In the City" again; the walker's label was changed for the same reason.
Corrected: Joins Lloyds Bank’s Colonial and Foreign Department in the City of London in March. Prufrock and Other Observations published, dedicated to Verdenal.

### 1921, October
Problem: "Sydney Schiff" is a name with no word of who he was, and "in October" repeats the heading the entry sits under.
Corrected: Lloyds grants three months' sick leave. Eliot goes to Margate and stays until 12 November; on 4 November he tells his friend Sydney Schiff that he has written 'some fifty lines' of Part III, sitting in a shelter on the seafront.

### 1922, September
Problem: The subject, Smyrna, is parted from its verb by two appositives, and the point of the second clause, that the fire is not in the poem, is left for the reader to work out.
Corrected: Smyrna burns, 13–22 September, a month before the poem is published. The fire is not in the poem; Mr. Eugenides, 'the Smyrna merchant' of line 209, was already in the typescript Pound edited in January.

### 1933
Problem: Two websites and three people are named with no word of what or who they are, in an entry meant to be two sentences; and the colon after "discs" does the work of a comma.
Corrected: Eliot records the poem at Columbia University, on aluminium discs, the earliest surviving reading. The discs were long dated 1935, and the PennSound archive still labels them so; the Poetry Archive dates them 1933, following the scholar Lacy Rumsey and the work of Richard Swigg (2012) and Jason Camlot (2019).

### 1968
Problem: "John Quinn" is a name with no word of who he was.
Corrected: The New York Public Library announces that it holds the typescript, long thought lost, with Pound’s and Vivien Eliot’s comments written on it. Eliot had sent it in 1922 to the New York lawyer and collector John Quinn; after Quinn’s death in 1924 it went unseen until the library bought it in 1958.

## Still unsettled
- poem: the note gives the first reply to the thunder as 402–409, but the site's own colouring runs 402–410 ("In our empty rooms" is line 410). One of them is off by one; scholar to settle. The note also leaves out two passages the lens colours as the poem's voice, 322–330 (the opening of Part V) and 396–400 ("Ganga was sunken"); a reader who clicks there gets a card that does not mention the lines they are on.
- water: the shower over the Starnbergersee (lines 8–9) is named in the intro but is not a stop (the Part I stops are lines 4, 24, 42, 47 and 55). Add the stop or drop the phrase; editor.
- birds: three of the nine stops are not birds (the cricket at 23, the cicada at 354, the bats at 380) and the intro does not say so. A clause ("and three singers that are not birds") would stop the first stop from contradicting it; editor.
- nothing: the seventh stop (402, "what have we given?") is not a use of the word, and the intro says "wherever the poem uses it"; editor.
- eliot: I have dropped "1921 and 1922" as the pathway's scope because the stops run from boyhood to January 1922; the scholar should confirm the three dates I kept, which are the site's own (Munich, August 1911; Margate, October 1921; Lausanne, November–December 1921).
- stranger: the card's translation ("a real German") differs from the one the page shows on hover at line 12 ("real German"); make them the same.
- upanishad: the rewrite quotes "sympathise" and "a feeble translation", which is what the site's own text of the notes prints; the scholar's round-one text quoted "our equivalent" as the 1922 reading. Scholar to say which is the 1922 text, since the site says its text is the 1922 edition.
- he and song: the same song is "That Shakespearian Rag" (the song's title) on his card and "the Shakespeherian Rag" (the poem's spelling) on the song card; and the song card lists lines 128–130, which the lens colours as his. One spelling, and the scholar to decide whose card the rag belongs on.
- title-draft: a three-word gloss on "the Police" (the police news in the paper) would help a reader; editor to decide whether it is worth the words.
