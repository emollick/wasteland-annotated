# Round 1, reader, batch C2

## Passes
None as they stand. Every record has at least one thing a non-specialist trips on. In about a third of them (lyly-campaspe, augustine-confessions, hesse-blick-ins-chaos, kyd-spanish-tragedy, london-bridge, shakespearian-rag, chapman-birds, pervigilium-veneris, huxley-crome-yellow) the fix is a clause or a single sentence and the rest of the note is kept as it was.

## Changes

### spenser-prothalamion
Problem: "Eliot keeps the refrain and empties the river" is a metaphor standing in for what Eliot did, and I had to stop to work it out. "Notice that" is the tour guide, and "also unhappy, also walking" is rhythm for its own sake.
Proposed:
note: A "spousal verse" for the double wedding of the Earl of Worcester's daughters. The poet, out of favour at court, walks by the Thames and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans, who stand for the brides. Eliot keeps the refrain; in his lines the nymphs have departed and the river carries nothing, not even the summer's litter. Spenser's poet is himself unhappy, walking off his "sullein care" by the river before the wedding cheers him up.

### psalm137
Problem: The what quotes Eliot's line, not the psalm's, so it does not tell me what the source says. "a psalm about being asked to write a poem when you cannot" is a reading passed off as a fact, "in exile from the bank" is a wink, and "put his lake in it" is the flourish at the end.
Proposed:
what: "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept."
note: The psalm of the Jewish exiles in Babylon, whose captors ask them for a song and who ask in return how they can sing the Lord's song in a strange land. Eliot takes its first line and replaces Babylon with Leman, the French name of Lake Geneva, on whose shore he was staying, away from his work at the bank, in the winter of 1921 [fact?].

### marvell-coy-mistress
Problem: The what's dots are neither Marvell's line nor Eliot's, and it took me a moment to see why they were there. "The great English argument for going to bed now" is the X-is-a-Y opening with a verdict built in, and "quotes the turn of the argument twice and gives it a rattle of bones" makes me reconstruct what Eliot did instead of telling me.
Proposed:
what: "But at my back I always hear / Time's wingèd chariot hurrying near."
note: Marvell's speaker urges his mistress not to wait: time is short, and in the grave there is no embracing. Eliot twice begins a line with Marvell's "But at my back," and each time puts something else where the chariot was: at line 185 it is the rattle of bones he hears, at line 196 the horns and motors that bring Sweeney to Mrs Porter. Eliot's essay on Marvell, published in 1921, months before the poem, praised "a tough reasonableness beneath the slight lyric grace."

### day-parliament-of-bees
Problem: The what does not say what the source is, and "turned into" assumes I already know both stories. "A masque-like sequence of "characters" among the bees" needs a specialist to parse. "Ovid" is dropped without the book, and "in a motor car" ends the note on a twist.
Proposed:
what: "A noise of horns and hunting" that brings Actaeon to Diana.
note: A sequence of short verse scenes, each called a "character," in which bees stand for human types [fact?]. The lines Eliot's note quotes describe a spring hunt that brings Actaeon to the goddess Diana as she bathes. In Ovid's <i>Metamorphoses</i> the hunter who sees Diana naked is turned into a stag and torn apart by his own dogs. In Eliot's lines 196–197 the horns are motor horns, and the pair they bring together are Sweeney and Mrs Porter.

### verlaine-parsifal
Problem: The what is in French with no English, so a reader without French gets nothing from it. The <i>Revue wagnérienne</i> and "Weston" are names dropped without saying what they are. "so that the temptation and the temple stand one line apart" is a paired antithesis that tells me what to think, and "the thing the poem never manages" is the epigram at the end.
Proposed:
what: "And, oh, those children's voices singing in the dome!": the last line of a sonnet on Wagner's <i>Parsifal</i>.
note: Verlaine's sonnet on Wagner's last opera, written for the <i>Revue wagnérienne</i>, a Paris journal devoted to Wagner, compresses the whole plot into thirteen lines and then, in the fourteenth, simply reports the sound of the boys' choir in the Grail temple. Eliot puts that line straight after the soldiers' song about Mrs Porter, so that the bawdy song and the temple choir sit side by side. Parsifal is the knight who heals the wounded king in the Grail legend that Jessie Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, the book Eliot's headnote cites, puts behind the poem. In the poem the king is left fishing on the shore, unhealed.

### lyly-campaspe
Problem: "the vocative of Tereus" is a grammarian's word, and nothing says who Tereus is. "with the pretty convention turned back into what it describes" is the closing epigram; I read it twice before I saw it meant the rape.
Proposed:
note: The Elizabethan convention that the nightingale sings "jug jug" and cries "tereu," calling on Tereus, the king who raped Philomela, is at its plainest in this song from Lyly's court comedy. The songs in Lyly's plays were first printed in the collected edition of 1632, and whether he wrote them is disputed. Eliot's "Jug Jug to dirty ears" at line 103 and the four lines of birdsong at 203–206 use Lyly's bird-calls, and both times the words "So rudely forced" stand close by.

### sappho-104
Problem: The what mixes Eliot's words with a description and does not say what the fragment is. "a critic, Demetrius" gives no when or where; "Byron" is dropped without the poem. "relay stations" is a metaphor doing a plain sentence's work, and "the typist's teatime" is a wink.
Proposed:
what: Two lines to the evening star, which brings the sheep, the goat and the child home.
note: Two lines that survive because an ancient Greek critic, Demetrius, quoted them in his treatise <i>On Style</i>. Eliot's note says his lines "may not appear as exact as Sappho's" and that he had in mind the "longshore" fisherman who comes in at nightfall. His wording at lines 220–221 is closer to two English poems that had already echoed Sappho: Byron's lines to Hesperus in <i>Don Juan</i> [fact?] and Stevenson's "Requiem" ("Home is the sailor, home from sea").

### goldsmith-vicar
Problem: "Olivia Primrose" and "the squire" are names without who. "in the same meter, puts on a record" is so compressed that I had to build the parallel myself. The last sentence is a paired antithesis ending on a twist ("she is allowed not to die").
Proposed:
note: Olivia, the Vicar's daughter, seduced and abandoned by the local squire, sings this to her family. Goldsmith's song says the only thing left to a betrayed woman is to die. Eliot's typist, in lines that keep Goldsmith's metre, smooths her hair and puts a record on the gramophone. The scene has been read both as contempt for her and as sympathy for her.

### froude-elizabeth
Problem: One man is "Leicester" in the what, "Lord Robert" in the passage and "Dudley" in the note, and nothing joins the three. "in circumstances that made it impossible" is coy. "Eliot's Thames pageant of red and gold is this afternoon ... with nothing coming of it" is a compressed metaphor ending on a twist.
Proposed:
note: Froude quotes a letter of 1561 from the Spanish ambassador, Álvaro de la Quadra, to King Philip II. "Lord Robert" is Robert Dudley, later Earl of Leicester. The marriage never happened: Dudley's wife had been found dead at the foot of a staircase the year before, and the suspicion that she had been murdered made it impossible [fact?]. Eliot's note cites this passage for the lines beginning at 279, the gilded barge, "red and gold," with Elizabeth and Leicester in it.

### augustine-confessions
Problem: "six books later" made me count (Book III to Book X), when the passage already says X.34. "which is why the Fire Sermon ends with it" states a reading as a fact. The rest is plain.
Proposed:
note: In his <i>Confessions</i> Augustine describes his student years in Carthage, "a cauldron of unholy loves," and, in Book X, the pull that beautiful things still have on a man trying to keep his eyes on God. In the Latin of the first sentence Carthago puns on sartago, a frying pan, which may be one reason Eliot chose the sentence to close the Fire Sermon. Eliot uses Pusey's translation of 1838, the one in Everyman's Library, and in his note rewrites its first sentence as "to Carthage then I came, where a cauldron of unholy loves sang all about mine ears."

### buddha-fire-sermon
Problem: "the enlightenment" and "Gayā Head" assume I know whose and where. The last sentence, "everything, from the river to the typist, is seen as burning," is a reading passed off as a fact and shaped for effect.
Proposed:
note: Preached, the tradition says, to a thousand fire-worshipping ascetics on Gayā Head, a hill in northern India [fact?], soon after the Buddha's enlightenment. Eliot read it at Harvard in Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i>, and his note says it "corresponds in importance to the Sermon on the Mount." Part III takes its title from it, and the "Burning burning burning burning" of line 308.

### brihadaranyaka
Problem: "The fable is about hearing what you need to hear in a single syllable" is the aphorism that tells me what it means; "gives each a confession" is a literary shorthand I had to decode. The last clause does not say what English "a feeble translation" refers to, and the formal ending of an Upanishad is explained twice.
Proposed:
note: Eliot read the Upanishads in Sanskrit at Harvard with Charles Lanman in 1911–13, and his note cites Paul Deussen's German translation of 1897. In the fable each group hears in the one syllable the command it needs. Eliot puts the three commands in a different order and follows each with a passage in which the speaker answers it. "Shantih," peace, spoken three times, is the formal ending of an Upanishad, and the poem ends the same way. Eliot's last note says the English phrase "The Peace which passeth understanding" is "a feeble translation" of the word.

### bradley
Problem: "Oxford idealist" is a philosopher's term that a general reader takes the wrong way. "Ugolino's tower" is dropped without who or where. "the philosophical form of Ugolino's tower" and "that no key opens" are metaphors describing the poem, and "set against exactly this" is emphasis in place of explanation.
Proposed:
note: The Oxford philosopher on whom Eliot wrote his Harvard doctoral dissertation (finished 1916, never defended, published 1964). Eliot's note quotes page 346 straight after quoting Dante's Ugolino, who hears the door of the tower locked below him. The two have been read together: each of us shut inside our own experience as Ugolino was shut in the tower. In the poem the command "Dayadhvam," sympathise, is followed at once by the lines about the key and the prison to which the note is attached.

### hesse-blick-ins-chaos
Problem: The what quotes two phrases without saying where they come from (one is the poem, the other Eliot's note on Part V) or what the source is. "printed him in the first Criterion" means nothing unless you know Eliot founded a magazine. Otherwise plain.
Proposed:
what: The essay behind the "hooded hordes" and what Eliot's note on Part V calls "the present decay of eastern Europe."
note: Three essays written in the aftermath of the First World War, two of them on Dostoevsky, in which Hesse saw the "Russian man" of <i>The Brothers Karamazov</i> as the coming type of a Europe that had lost faith in its own order. Eliot's note quotes the essay "The Brothers Karamazov, or the Downfall of Europe," misprinting "in heiligem Wahn" as "im heiligem Wahn." He visited Hesse in Switzerland in 1922 and printed him in the first number of the <i>Criterion</i>, the magazine he founded that year.

### shackleton-south
Problem: The what quotes Eliot's note instead of saying what the source is. "the boat journey across the Southern Ocean survived" is inverted and I read it twice. "Worsley and Crean" are names without who, "the road to Emmaus" is dropped without the story, and "Frank Hurley's photographs ... are the images to hold beside it" is the tour guide telling me what to look at.
Proposed:
what: The crossing of South Georgia, when three exhausted men felt "that we were four, not three."
note: In May 1916, after the <i>Endurance</i> had been crushed in the ice and the party had come through the open-boat voyage across the Southern Ocean, Shackleton and two of his men, Worsley and Crean, crossed the mountains of South Georgia on foot to reach a whaling station. Eliot's note says he remembered the account imperfectly ("I think one of Shackleton's"). He set it beside the Gospel story of the road to Emmaus, in which two disciples walking from Jerusalem after the crucifixion are joined by a third whom they do not recognise as the risen Christ; the lines beginning at 360 ask who the third is.

### chapman-birds
Problem: "Listen to a recording:" is an instruction to the reader, and "really does sound like" and "is a fair transcription" are the chatty verdict. The description of the song is useful and should stay. Eliot's note also says where he heard the bird, which the note leaves out.
Proposed:
note: The field guide Eliot had as a boy, a gift from his mother; his own copy survives. His note quotes it, gives the bird the scientific name then in use, <i>Turdus aonalaschkae pallasii</i> (now <i>Catharus guttatus</i>), and says he had heard the bird in Quebec County. The song, a long clear note followed by a falling, liquid phrase, is what Eliot's note calls its "water-dripping song." The bird sings at dusk, and the "Drip drop drip drop drop drop drop" of the next line imitates it.

### pervigilium-veneris
Problem: The refrain is given in Latin only, though the translation is a line away. "late Latin" is a specialist's period. The last sentence is a flourish, and "the poem" in it means The Waste Land while "the poem" two sentences earlier meant the Pervigilium.
Proposed:
note: A Latin poem of ninety-three lines, written some time between the second and fourth centuries AD, for the eve of the spring festival of Venus; its refrain is "cras amet qui numquam amavit," tomorrow let him love who has never loved. After the flowers and nymphs, the last stanza turns to the poet's own silence and envies the swallow, who is Procne, sister of the raped Philomela. Eliot's 1922 text reads "ceu" where the poem has "uti," and so do his manuscripts; the line was changed to "uti" in <i>Collected Poems 1909–1935</i> (1936).

### nerval-desdichado
Problem: The what is French with no English. "Ivanhoe" is dropped without its author. "A poet who has twice crossed the river of the dead and come back, the sonnet says, and can still play" is built for rhythm, with the verb held back to the end. "takes the second line and leaves the consolation" is the paired antithesis closing the note.
Proposed:
what: "Le Prince d'Aquitaine à la tour abolie": the Prince of Aquitaine with the ruined tower.
note: The first sonnet of Nerval's <i>Chimères</i>, written between bouts of madness a year before he hanged himself in a Paris alley. The title is Spanish, "the disinherited one," taken from a knight's shield in Walter Scott's <i>Ivanhoe</i>; the "tour abolie" is often read as the Tower of the Tarot. In the last lines the poet says he has twice crossed Acheron, the river of the dead, and come back, and still plays Orpheus's lyre. Eliot quotes only the second line.

### kyd-spanish-tragedy
Problem: The last sentence, "A revenge carried out by means of a multilingual entertainment is not a bad description of the poem that quotes it," is the wink that tells me what to think of the poem. "means I'll supply what you want and I'll pay you back" should say that both senses are meant. The rest is plain and useful.
Proposed:
note: Hieronimo, Knight Marshal of Spain, whose son Horatio has been murdered and hanged in an arbour, is asked by the murderers to write a play for the court. "I'll fit you" means two things at once: I'll give you what you want, and I'll pay you back. The play he supplies is written "in sundry languages," and in performing it the murderers are stabbed for real. "Hieronimo is mad againe" is from the title page of the 1615 quarto and of the reprints that followed it.

### london-bridge
Problem: "Frazer" is a name without who; "the old custom of burying a victim" states a legend as history; the last clause, "the only one in English a child would know," is the epigram.
Proposed:
note: The rhyme runs through building materials that fail until a watchman is set. Folklorists of James Frazer's generation liked to connect it with the belief that a bridge was once made to stand by burying a victim in its foundations. Eliot's commuters crossed the real London Bridge at line 62. The rhyme is the first of the run of quotations at lines 427–430, the "fragments" of line 431, and the only one of the four in English.

### shakespearian-rag
Problem: "Ziegfeld Follies" without saying what it was. "well enough to misquote one on purpose" asserts Eliot's intention as a fact. Otherwise plain and useful.
Proposed:
note: Published in 1912 and advertised as a number from that year's Ziegfeld Follies, the Broadway revue, though it seems never to have been sung in the show and was first performed in vaudeville in January 1913. The song sets Shakespeare's characters dancing to ragtime. Eliot knew the popular songs of his Harvard years; his extra syllable, "Shakespeherian," has been read as deliberate, giving the word the rag's syncopation. No recording from the period is known.

### dans-le-restaurant
Problem: "then, out of nowhere, the drowned Phoenician" is a fragment for effect; "a childhood fumble" is flip; "He is the only poet the poem quotes in two languages" is the epigram named in the brief.
Proposed:
note: One of the four poems Eliot wrote in French in 1917–18. A waiter tells a customer about a childhood memory, sheltering with a little girl under a tree in the rain, and the customer, disgusted, tells him to go and wash; the last stanza, with no transition, is about the drowned Phoenician. Eliot translated that stanza into English for Part IV, dropping the tin cargo and the Cornish swell and adding "Gentile or Jew."

### death-of-st-narcissus
Problem: The first sentence buries its subject under two participial phrases and I read it twice. "John Quinn" is a name without who. "in the cadence the later poem keeps ... something other than his own shadow on the sand or against the fire" is dense, and "the poem's first prophecy" is a metaphor where a line reference would do.
Proposed:
note: An early poem of Eliot's about a saint who wanted to be a tree, a fish and a girl, and died pierced with arrows. It was set in type for <i>Poetry</i> (Chicago) in 1915 and withdrawn at proof stage, and survived among the manuscripts Eliot sent in 1922 to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector [fact?], with the <i>Waste Land</i> drafts. After these two lines the speaker promises to show the listener something other than his own shadow, on the sand or against the fire: the saint's bloodstained cloth and limbs. Eliot reworked the opening, with the rock now red, into lines 25–30. The poem is still in copyright, so only its first two lines are given here.

### dickens-omf
Problem: "Betty Higden" and "Sloppy" are names without who. "the plainest statement of what the poem does that anyone has made, including its author" is a verdict, and "the poem's river in prose" is the metaphor the brief names.
Proposed:
note: Betty Higden, a poor old woman in the novel, is praising Sloppy, the foundling she has brought up, who reads her the newspaper and does all the voices in the police-court reports. Eliot wrote the sentence across the top of the typescript of Parts I and II as their title; it did not survive into the published poem. Dickens's novel opens with a body being fished out of the Thames.

### conrad-hod
Problem: "Kurtz," "Marlow" and "Petronius" are names without who. "still answers to it" is a reading stated as fact, in a metaphor; "is worth rereading with Part III in mind" is the tour guide.
Proposed:
note: The last words of Kurtz, the trader whom the narrator, Marlow, has gone up the river to find, stood at the head of the typescript as the poem's epigraph. Pound doubted whether Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation"; Eliot defended it as "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," then replaced it with the passage from Petronius that stands there now. The phrase "the heart of light" at line 41 has been read as an echo of Conrad's title. The novel opens on a boat on the Thames at Gravesend at dusk, with London behind it.

### tennyson
Problem: "a Victorian ancestor of the man in rats' alley" is a metaphor stating a reading as fact; "the English half of a Latin line" is a puzzle until you go and look at line 429; "shows in what he took" is the closing bow.
Proposed:
note: The mad speaker of <i>Maud</i> imagines himself buried a yard under a city street with the traffic going over him; "a handful of dust" is his phrase, and the passage has been compared with the "rats' alley" of Part II. Line 429 is half Latin, from the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i>, and half English, and the English half, "O swallow swallow," is from the song in <i>The Princess</i>. In 1936 Eliot called Tennyson's "the finest ear of any English poet since Milton."

### huxley-crome-yellow
Problem: "in drag" is flip; "Garsington" is dropped without saying what it was; "the two knew each other's work" says nothing. "immediate source" leaves me wondering what the other source is.
Proposed:
note: Huxley's country-house satire came out in November 1921, while Eliot was at Margate and Lausanne. Its Mr. Scogan, a cynical guest who dresses up as an old gypsy woman to tell fortunes at the village fête under the name "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana," is where the name of Eliot's "famous clairvoyante" comes from. Huxley and Eliot knew each other from Garsington, Lady Ottoline Morrell's house near Oxford, where both were guests.

### eliot-frontiers
Problem: The first sentence runs to sixty words on a colon and three clauses. "spike the guns" looks like Eliot's own phrase and should be in quotation marks if it is; readers had "bought" the notes is not what he said, or not in those words. "Take the notes as part of the poem and as a joke at its expense; both are true" is the epigram named in the brief.
Proposed:
note: A lecture given at the University of Minnesota in 1956 to an audience of nearly fourteen thousand. Thirty-four years on, Eliot described the notes as padding that had got out of hand. He had meant only to list his references, to "spike the guns" [fact?] of critics who had accused him of plagiarism; then, when the poem proved too short to make a book on its own, he expanded the notes to fill pages. He also said they could not now be removed: a reader who bought his poems and found the notes missing would ask for his money back.

## The batch as a whole
The facts are good and the notes are short; most of them tell me in two sentences what the source is and what Eliot did with it, which is what I came for. The trouble is the last sentence. In nearly every record the note turns, at the end, from telling me what is in the source to telling me what to think of the poem, and does so in a metaphor ("empties the river", "the poem's river in prose"), a paired antithesis ("takes the second line and leaves the consolation") or a joke ("not a bad description of the poem that quotes it"). The second habit is names dropped as if I knew them: Weston, Ugolino, Frazer, Garsington, Quinn, Kurtz, Worsley and Crean, and one man called Leicester, Lord Robert and Dudley within a single record. Three "what" lines are in French or Latin with no English, and several quote Eliot or his note instead of saying what the source is. Nothing here is as bad as the two examples Ethan quoted; it is the same voice at a lower volume, and once the endings are cut most of the notes are fine as they stand.
