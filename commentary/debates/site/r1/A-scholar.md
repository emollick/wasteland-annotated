# Round 1, scholar, batch A

## Checked

- The site's line numbers against the 1922 text: Project Gutenberg #1321, counted by script. 434 lines; every number the batch cites lands on the line it names (20, 42, 62, 68, 111, 135, 161, 208, 209, 213, 255, 320, 339, 346, 357, 361, 398, 416, 424, 427–434). Line 56, "crowds of people, walking round in a ring", is six lines before the London Bridge crowd at 62, not four.
- The 1922 readings claimed on the About page: archive.org scan wasteland01elio, a first-printing copy (limitation leaf "Of the one thousand copies printed"). Confirmed, all of them: "Od' und leer das Meer", "tonight", "The hot w ter at ten", "dont", "wont", "its time", "alright", "smoothes", "mount in", "ceu chelidon", "aetherial", "a feeble translation"; the margin number 350 beside "A spring"; the flush-right "If there were water"; the note numbers 357, 360, 366–76, 401, 411, 424, 433. The third printing (August 1928) and fourth (April 1930) read "smooths" and "The hot water at ten" but still "mount in".
- "mount in": bookseller collations of the first printing (Second Story Books, Heritage Auctions). The "a" dropped out during the run, and sheets with both readings were bound and sold together on publication day. "Most copies" cannot be shown; "many copies" can.
- OpenStreetMap relation 2263653: the OSM API. name=River Thames, type=waterway, waterway=river. Correct.
- The date of Pound's "Complimenti, you bitch" letter: Lawrence Rainey, "What was left out", LRB, 3 December 2009, and Hugh Kenner (1973). "24 Saturnus, An 1" is 24 January 1922; D. D. Paige's 24 December 1921 was an error repeated in the 1988 Letters and corrected since. The letter answers the revised typescript Eliot sent from London.
- The "rhythmical grumbling" attribution: the facsimile's own epigraph note, as quoted in several places online. Eliot's remark was quoted by Theodore Spencer in a lecture at Harvard and written down by Henry Ware Eliot Jr. The site has Eliot lecturing and Spencer reporting; it was the other way about.
- The manuscript's history: NYRB (Wilson, 1971), NYPL, Wikipedia. Sent to Quinn in 1922 (arrived January 1923); sold by Quinn's niece Mary Conroy to the Berg Collection on 4 April 1958 for $18,000; made public in 1968, three years after Eliot's death. The site's "lost for forty years" is loose but not wrong.
- Vivien Eliot's lines: Wikipedia (Vivienne Haigh-Wood Eliot) and other secondary sources, agreeing with my memory of the facsimile's transcript of the Part II typescript. Two lines of the pub scene are in her hand in the margin, "If you don't like it you can get on with it" (153) and "What you get married for if you don't want children?" (164). The site says one.
- The draft of Part IV: secondary sources agree on 93 lines cut to the last ten, so "eighty-three lines of sea voyage" holds. Not checked against the facsimile itself.
- Eliot's daily route over London Bridge: searched the biographies and the walking-tour literature. Nothing places his commute on the bridge; his homes from 1916 to 1922 (Crawford Mansions, then Clarence Gate Gardens) were in Marylebone, north-west of the City, and the crowd on the bridge came in from the railway terminus on the south bank. Sources also disagree on the bank's address (17 Cornhill, 71 Lombard Street, Threadneedle Street). Not established; see [legend-places] and [map-lede].
- Lombard Street: not named anywhere in the poem (checked the text). The map lede lists it among the poem's places.
- Could not verify online: Pound's "Bad" on the sea voyage; whether all of Pound's marks are in pencil (my memory of the facsimile is pencil and ink, which is why I propose dropping "in pencil"). "Bogus scholarship" is from "The Frontiers of Criticism" (1956), from memory, not re-checked.

## The batch as a whole

The facts on the About page are in better order than the prose around them: every 1922 reading it claims is in the first printing, the account of the numbering is exact, and the sources are the right ones. The errors are elsewhere, and most are small but real: Ferdinand's father for Ferdinand, four lines for six, one line of Vivien's for two, a Vedic hymn for an Upanishad, Lombard Street among the poem's places, Ganga and Himavant as the last places named, a reversed attribution for "rhythmical grumbling", and a commute over London Bridge that no biography I know supports. The voice problem is concentrated in the lens legends, the tools' subtitles and the tarot notes, where readings are stated as facts and nearly every note ends on a turn; the page ledes and the About page mostly need a sentence removed rather than rewritten. Of the ninety-four records, thirty-two pass as they stand, fifty-nine are changed (in forty-nine entries, since the nine lenses and the seven tool names are each treated as one set, offered whole so that they read as one hand), and three are left to the reader as matters of wording.
