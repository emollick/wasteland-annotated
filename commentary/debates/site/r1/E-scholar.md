## Changes

### phoenician-sailor
Problem: "he is the Hanged Man the reading cannot find, gone into the water instead" is a reading stated as fact. Eliot's note on 46 links the Hanged Man to Frazer's Hanged God and to the hooded figure of Part V, not to the Sailor; the equation is the drawing's and should be said to be.
Proposed: ANSWERS: 47–48, and Part IV (312–321). "Those are pearls that were his eyes" is from Ariel's song in <i>The Tempest</i> (I. ii). He is drawn head down, in the posture of the Tarot's Hanged Man, whom Madame Sosostris does not find (54–55): the drawing's suggestion is that the Sailor has taken his place under the water. Eliot's note (46) connects the Hanged Man with Frazer's Hanged God and with the hooded figure of Part V, not with the Sailor.
Source: Eliot's note on 46 (Boni & Liveright 1922).

### belladonna
Problem: "is Leonardo's Virgin of the Rocks" states as fact what is a reading; Eliot's notes say nothing of Leonardo. Pater's sentence is better quoted than paraphrased, and the paraphrase of "belladonna" should say what the cosmetic did.
Proposed: ANSWERS: 49–50, "Here is Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks, / The lady of situations." "The Lady of the Rocks" recalls Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i> and, behind it, Pater's page on the Mona Lisa in <i>The Renaissance</i> (1873): "She is older than the rocks among which she sits." Belladonna is the plant (deadly nightshade), its poison, the drops that widen the pupils, and Italian for "beautiful lady".
Source: Pater, The Renaissance, "Leonardo da Vinci" (Gutenberg #2398).

### three-staves
Problem: the parenthesis "(so: a wound to lean on, a line to fish with, a rod of office set in the waste ground)" performs, and the note leaves out that Eliot calls the association "quite arbitrary", which a reader should be told. SHOWS stands.
Proposed: ANSWERS: 51. Eliot's note: "The Man with Three Staves (an authentic member of the Tarot pack) I associate, quite arbitrarily, with the Fisher King himself." The drawing gives him a crutch for the king's wound, a rod to fish with and a staff planted in the waste ground. The composition follows Pamela Colman Smith's Three of Wands (1909): a man with his back turned, standing on a height, looking out over the sea at ships, three staves planted beside him.
Source: Eliot's note on 46; Waite, The Pictorial Key to the Tarot (1911), Three of Wands.

### wheel
Problem: minor. Four Latin words with no translation and no source, and "looks ahead to" is the tour guide's verb.
Proposed: ANSWERS: 51. The ship's wheel is from Part IV: "O you who turn the wheel and look to windward" (320). The four figures are those of the medieval Wheel of Fortune, as in the thirteenth-century Carmina Burana manuscript: <i>regno</i> (I reign) enthroned at the top, <i>regnavi</i> (I have reigned) falling, <i>sum sine regno</i> (I have no kingdom) beneath, <i>regnabo</i> (I shall reign) climbing.
Source: Codex Buranus, the Wheel of Fortune miniature (Wikipedia, "Rota Fortunae").

### hanged-man
Problem: "the card shows the place he is missing from" is the epigram; the ANSWERS paraphrases the note where it could quote it, and "which is why the three walkers are there, and why the bough sprouts" explains the drawing in the artist's voice.
Proposed: SHOWS: A dead tree with one long bough for a beam and a rope tied to it, its noose hanging open. The air is dense cross-hatching, and where the Hanged Man should hang, inverted, one leg crooked, the paper is left bare in his shape, with a gold halo round the head that is not there. Far off at the bottom, on a road, two walkers and a hooded third. A green shoot on the dead bough.
ANSWERS: 54–55, "I do not find / The Hanged Man." Eliot's note (46): the Hanged Man "is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer", and "with the hooded figure in the passage of the disciples to Emmaus in Part V" (360–366). The walkers on the road and the shoot on the dead bough come from that note; the inverted pose, the crooked leg and the halo are Pamela Colman Smith's.
Source: Eliot's note on 46; Waite, The Pictorial Key to the Tarot, XII.

### sibyl
Problem: the long life asked for without youth is not in Petronius; it is Ovid's story (Metamorphoses xiv). The note credits it to the epigraph.
Proposed: ANSWERS: The epigraph, from Petronius, <i>Satyricon</i> 48. Trimalchio says he saw the Sibyl at Cumae with his own eyes, hanging in a jar, and that when the boys asked her "Sibyl, what do you want?" she answered "I want to die." How she came to it is in Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> xiv: Apollo granted her as many years as the grains in a handful of sand, and she did not ask for youth.
Source: Petronius, Satyricon 48; Ovid, Metamorphoses xiv 130–153.

### swallow

Proposed: ANSWERS: 429, "Quando fiam ceu chelidon—O swallow swallow". Source: Boni & Liveright 1922 and the site's text at 429 (Rainey, The Annotated Waste Land, on the "ceu"/"uti" variant); Pervigilium Veneris, final stanzas; Tennyson, The Princess iv.

## Unsettled or for the reader
- Vignette titles: wording, the reader's call. Six carry a poetic tail or an inversion ("Elizabeth and Leicester, below the white towers"; "London Bridge, the crowd flowing over"; "A barge with red sails, down Greenwich reach"; "By Richmond, a narrow canoe"; "Behind the gashouse, the dull canal"; "Trams and dusty trees: Highbury"). Plainer forms, if wanted: "Elizabeth and Leicester on the river"; "The crowd on London Bridge"; "A barge in Greenwich reach"; "A narrow canoe by Richmond"; "The dull canal behind the gashouse"; "Highbury: trams and dusty trees". The other seven are plain and can stay.
- "The Metropole, Brighton": the poem says only "the Metropole"; Brighton is the usual gloss (Southam), and there was also a Hotel Metropole on Northumberland Avenue in London. As a picture's name it can stand; if the map's caption says more, it should say "usually taken as".
- "St Mary Woolnoth, Lombard Street": right (the church stands at the corner of Lombard Street and King William Street); the poem names King William Street (66). Either street will do.
- Two drawings disagree with each other: the merchant wears a bowler on one-eyed-merchant and a homburg on eugenides; London Bridge has "three open arches" on crowd-on-london-bridge and "five arches" on london-bridge-falling (Rennie's bridge of 1831 had five). Not a matter of the text; the artist may want to know.
- SHOWS wording the reader may want plainer, which I have left: "opens into a bare oval: the heart of light" (hyacinth-girl); "three forks of bare lightning out of it: the thunder's three words" (thunder); "a trump of the real pack" (tower; "trump XVI of the Tarot" would be exact).
- lady-in-the-chair closes its quotation "Glowed into words." with a full stop, where the line runs on ("Glowed into words, then would be savagely still"). Convention allows it; Ricks and McCue would not.
- tiresias's range 215–248 stops short of 256, where the typist's scene ends; the typist card takes 222–256, so nothing is lost.
- I could not consult Ricks and McCue or Southam directly for the Larisch and Metropole notes; what I give is from the facsimile's page 126 as quoted online and from the hotels' own records.

## The batch as a whole
The cards' line numbers are right throughout, with one slip (swallow), and the drawings are described concretely; most SHOWS fields can stand as they are. What has leaked is the studio: an ink percentage, a group id, "No accent", "back.svg", "not to be confused with", and three winks (the one-eyed profile, "the plainest card after the blank", "the place he is missing from"). Two quotations do not match the site's own text (112 and 429), and three source claims overstate: Petronius is given Ovid's story, the Pervigilium a transformation it does not tell, and two readings (the Sailor as the Hanged Man; the Lady of the Rocks as Leonardo's) are stated as facts. Where Eliot's note is the source it should be quoted, and it usually can be in a phrase: "quite arbitrarily", "I think one of Shackleton's", "seller of currants". Everything else is wording, and the reader's.
