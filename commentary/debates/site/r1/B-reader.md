## Changes

### first
Problem: "Each stop is a place to stand, not an explanation" is the paired antithesis, and it is the same tour-guide figure Ethan quoted ("where the poem is standing"). "Read the lines, read the note, move on" is three fragments in a row. I am not told what a stop actually contains.
Proposed: Twelve stops through the poem for a reader meeting it for the first time. Each stop gives a passage and a short note on it, in the order of the poem. Read the lines first, then the note, then go on to the next stop.

### water
Problem: "The poem's hydrology" is named in the brief as a tell. "Follow the water and you follow the argument:" is the colon-and-list opening, closed with a twist ("the two are never told apart"). It tells me what to conclude instead of what I will see.
Proposed: The passages where water appears, in order, from the spring rain of the opening lines to the rain at the end of Part V. In the poem water is what the dry land lacks and also what drowns.

### dead
Problem: The first sentence is a list of fragments for effect; the second ends on a wink ("the dead never quite stay in it").
Proposed: The poem's dead, in order: the buried, the drowned, the bones, and the crowd on London Bridge. Part I takes its title, The Burial of the Dead, from the burial service in the Book of Common Prayer, and the dead go on appearing in every part after it.

### women
Problem: The last sentence counts to sound clever ("two are queens; one is a bird") and ends on the twist. The first two sentences are fine.
Proposed: Eliot's note on Tiresias says that in the poem "all the women are one woman." This walk visits each of them in the order she appears, so that you can judge the claim for yourself. Some speak in their own words; some are figures from older books; Philomel, from Ovid, appears as a nightingale.

### cities
Problem: "five capitals falling in two lines" is the counting flourish, and "The poem's geography" is built like "the poem's hydrology". Title passes.
Proposed: The places the poem names, in the order it names them: Munich at the start, London through the middle, the Ganges near the end, and in Part V the five cities named in two lines.

### birds
Problem: "Every bird in the poem is a musician, and every song is a complaint" is an aphorism that tells me how to hear the birds and not which birds or where. The second sentence is useful and stays.
Proposed: The poem's birds, in the order they appear, with the lines where each is heard. Several are given their calls in the text: Jug Jug, Drip drop, Co co rico. Recordings of the birds themselves, where they exist, are in the listening room.

### nothing
Problem: The list reads as five speakers, but the last two are one: the Thames-daughter at Margate says "Nothing with nothing" and, three lines on, that her people "expect / Nothing", and the site's own Voices lens gives lines 300–306 to her. "A lover" is a guess about lines 37–41 that the Voices lens itself avoids.
Proposed: One word, followed through the poem. It is said by the one who came back from the hyacinth garden and could not speak, by the woman with bad nerves and the man who does not answer her, and, twice, by the Thames-daughter on Margate Sands.

### eyes
Problem: Seven fragments stacked for effect, then an antithesis for a close ("cannot see, or see too much"). "Eyes that were holden" is a King James word no one will know unglossed.
Proposed: Eyes, and what they can and cannot see: the speaker whose eyes fail in the hyacinth garden, the drowned man's eyes turned to pearls, the crowd on London Bridge with eyes fixed on their feet, the lidless eyes at the end of the game of chess, the one-eyed merchant, the blind Tiresias, and the two on the road to Emmaus whose eyes were "holden", held, so that they did not know who walked beside them.

### eliot
Problem: "Not a key to the poem, but a map of where he was standing" is the sentence the brief quotes as a tell, and it is the "standing" figure Ethan objected to. "Surface in the text" is a metaphor doing a plain word's job. Title passes.
Proposed: Places and dates from Eliot's life in 1921 and 1922 that lie behind particular lines of the poem, each set beside the lines it bears on.

### title-draft
Problem: The last sentence is a metaphor about the poem that ties a bow ("reads the world's news aloud in every voice it can do"), and "survives in the notes" is wrong or unclear: the notes never mention Dickens. Nothing tells me what the Dickens phrase means or who says it. "Weston's Grail country and Malory's 'waste land' of the Dolorous Stroke" are three names a non-specialist cannot place. The title asserts a visible deletion ("crossed out") that the body does not describe; if the typescript simply carries the heading, call it the earlier title.
Proposed title: The earlier title
Proposed body: Across the top of the typescript of Parts I and II Eliot typed "He Do the Police in Different Voices". The phrase is Betty Higden's in Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i>, praising the boy Sloppy for reading the newspaper aloud: "He do the Police in different voices." The published title comes from the Grail legend as Eliot read it in Jessie Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>; his headnote to the notes says that the title, the plan and much of the symbolism of the poem were suggested by her book. [Malory and the Dolorous Stroke: keep only if the scholar confirms that "waste land" is Malory's phrase, and then say in one clause what the Dolorous Stroke is.]

### epigraph-draft
Problem: The last sentence is the bow, an antithesis ("a dying man's verdict... an immortal's wish"). "Kurtz" and "the Sibyl from Petronius" are not explained, so the sentence about the change means nothing to me. Title passes.
Proposed body: The typescript carried a different epigraph: a passage from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i> ending with the dying Kurtz's words, "The horror! the horror!" Pound doubted that Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation". Eliot answered that it was "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative", but gave way, and put in its place the sentence from Petronius that the poem now opens with, in which the Sibyl of Cumae, asked what she wants, answers that she wants to die.

### poem
Problem: "It is never quite a person" is the epigram at the end. "Elegises" and "the thunder's glosses" are words I had to translate. Label passes.
Proposed note: The voice that belongs to no named character. It opens the poem, describes the room at the start of Part II, speaks the lines on the drowned Phlebas in Part IV, and answers each DA of the thunder in Part V.

### marie
Problem: "A displaced Austrian aristocrat" states a reading as a fact. The poem gives coffee in the Hofgarten, a sled ride with her cousin the archduke, and going south in winter; it never says Austrian or displaced.
Proposed note: A woman remembering a summer: coffee and talk in the Hofgarten in Munich, and a sled ride in the mountains as a child with her cousin the archduke. Lines 8–18, except the sentence of German at line 12, which this edition gives to another speaker.

### prophet
Problem: "From under the red rock" reads as though the prophet is under it; he is inviting the listener in. "Ezekiel's and Isaiah's voice" states a pairing of sources as fact (see the scholar section).
Proposed note: A voice in the manner of the Old Testament prophets, addressing the reader as "Son of man" (Ezekiel's phrase, as Eliot's note says) and offering the shade of the red rock. Lines 19–30.

### lover
Problem: "The poem's one admission of feeling" is a verdict on the whole poem dropped as an aside; it tells me how to feel, and it is arguable. Label passes.
Proposed note: The one who came back from the hyacinth garden with the girl and could not speak. Lines 37–41.

### sosostris
Problem: Line 46 appears in both ranges; I read it twice before seeing that she begins halfway through the line.
Proposed note: The fortune-teller (the poem's word is clairvoyante), reading the cards. Lines 43–46 introduce her in the poem's own voice; she speaks from the middle of line 46 ("Here, said she") to line 59.

### walker
Problem: Label only. Capital-C "City" for the financial district will read as plain "the city" to anyone outside Britain. The note is clear and passes.
Proposed label: The walker in the City of London

### he
Problem: "He thinks, in other people's lines" is a riddle. It means that his replies are unspoken and that some of them are quotations; say so.
Proposed note: His replies are the lines without quotation marks between 115 and 138. They are thoughts, not speech, and some of them are quotations.

### pub
Problem: "The Cockney monologue" is a name for the passage, not a description of it: it does not say who is talking or about what. The span also contains the publican's call, which the next voice covers.
Proposed note: A woman in a London pub, telling what she said to her friend Lil about Lil's husband Albert, home from the army. Lines 139–171, interrupted by the publican's call.

### publican
Problem: No line numbers, unlike every other voice, and "publican" is not a word every reader has. Label can stay if the note explains it.
Proposed note: The landlord calling closing time: HURRY UP PLEASE ITS TIME, five times between lines 141 and 169.

### ophelia
Problem: "On her way to the water" is the wink: she is not on her way anywhere in the scene, and you have to know she drowns to get it.
Proposed note: Line 172: Ophelia's goodnight from <i>Hamlet</i> (Act IV, scene 5), her last words on stage.

### fisher
Problem: Three names in a row ("Spenser's poet, Ferdinand and the Fisher King") and none explained; a reader cannot place any of them, and "in one voice" is the flourish. The Fisher King link is a reading, not Eliot's note at this point (see the scholar section).
Proposed note: The speaker by the river in Part III, who repeats Spenser's refrain "Sweet Thames, run softly" and then fishes in the canal behind the gashouse. Eliot's note at line 192 links him to Ferdinand in <i>The Tempest</i>, mourning his father; he has also been read as the Fisher King of the Grail legend. Lines 173–198.

### song
Problem: The three items are out of line order (128–130 comes last). "A song, overheard" is the comma-fragment label. "The soldiers' song" states as fact what Eliot's note leaves open (see the scholar section).
Proposed label: Songs quoted
Proposed note: Snatches of song: the Shakespeherian Rag (128–130); the ballad about Mrs Porter, which Eliot's note says was reported to him from Sydney (199–201); and Verlaine's line on children singing in the dome (202).

### nightingale
Problem: "In bird-language" is a coinage where a plain statement would do, and the note does not say who Philomel is.
Proposed note: Lines 203–206: the nightingale's cry, "Jug jug" and "Tereu", for Philomel, who was raped by Tereus and changed into a nightingale. Her story is told at lines 99–103.

### typist
Problem: "One half-formed thought aloud" quotes the poem ("Her brain allows one half-formed thought to pass") without marking it, and "aloud" contradicts it: the poem calls it a thought (see the scholar section).
Proposed note: Line 252, her only words, in quotation marks: what the poem calls her "one half-formed thought".

### thames
Problem: A reader does not know what a Thames-daughter is, or that the refrain is Wagner's; "The river's song" is a figure in place of that information.
Proposed note: The three Thames-daughters, as Eliot's note calls them, singing together: lines 266–291. The refrain "Weialala leia" is the Rhine-daughters' from Wagner's <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, which the note cites.

### ascetic
Problem: "Two ascetics, one word between them" is the counting epigram, and the word is not even named.
Proposed note: Lines 307–311: Augustine's <i>Confessions</i> ("To Carthage then I came") set beside the Buddha's Fire Sermon ("Burning"). Eliot's note says the pairing of "these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism" is "not an accident".

### thunder

Proposed note: DA, three times: lines 401, 411 and 418. [Scholar to confirm the numbers in the site's count.]

### fisherking
Problem: "And the fragments that follow" overlaps the next voice, The fragments (427–432), so I cannot tell whom the site thinks speaks them. The quotation after a colon is a caption, not a sentence.
Proposed note: Lines 424–426, "I sat upon the shore / Fishing, with the arid plain behind me". Eliot's note on "Shall I at least set my lands in order?" (426) refers to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King.

### fragments
Problem: "A Latin poet, Nerval, the poet, Kyd": "the poet" after "a Latin poet" made me read twice (it means Eliot's own line), and Nerval and Kyd are bare names.
Proposed note: Lines 427–432, a line each: the nursery rhyme London Bridge is falling down (427); Dante's <i>Purgatorio</i> (428); the Latin <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i> (429); Nerval's sonnet <i>El Desdichado</i> (430); the poem's own line, "These fragments I have shored against my ruins" (431); and Kyd's <i>Spanish Tragedy</i> (432). ### upanishad

Proposed note: The poem's last two lines, 433–434. Eliot's note translates the three words as "Give, sympathise, control" and gives their source as the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, one of the Hindu scriptures; Shantih, repeated three times, is "a formal ending to an Upanishad".

## The batch as a whole
The pathway introductions are the weakest thing here: every one of the nine either opens on an aphorism, stacks fragments for effect, counts something to sound clever, or closes on a turn, and the one Ethan would quote next is "Not a key to the poem, but a map of where he was standing". The Voices lens is good wherever it is plain (a name, a place, a line range: the Thames-daughters, the sailor, Tiresias) and goes wrong wherever a note tries to be memorable in five words ("one word between them", "in bird-language", "on her way to the water", "It is never quite a person"). The timeline is the best of the batch; most entries are a date and a fact, and what I changed was dropped quotations without a speaker, untranslated Italian, a colon standing in for a verb, and three fragments at the end. The pattern I saw most often, across all four files, is the site withholding the plain fact a reader needs (what a Thames-daughter is, who Kurtz is, why Smyrna or Narcissus is in the timeline, who Verdenal was) while spending its words on an effect. When the effect is removed, the fact usually fits in the space it leaves.
