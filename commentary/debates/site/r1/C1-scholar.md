## Changes

### weston
Problem: Two of the brief's named epigrams ("It does not matter.", "a very good bad book") and a metaphor ("the poem's skeleton"). The Syrian merchants are compressed past what Weston says (see Checked). "Thirty-four years later" counts for effect.
Proposed:
note: Weston (1850–1928) was a scholar of Arthurian romance who argued, following Frazer, that the Grail stories preserved the remains of an ancient fertility cult: a king whose wound or sickness lays his land waste, a cup and a lance that were sexual symbols before they were Christian relics, and a rite of initiation descended from the mystery cults of Attis and Mithra, which she says were carried across the Roman Empire by Syrian merchants, slaves and soldiers (chapter XII). Medievalists have not accepted the argument. Eliot's headnote says the book suggested "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem", and recommends it "to any who think such elucidation of the poem worth the trouble"; in 1956 he regretted "having sent so many enquirers off on a wild goose chase after Tarot cards and the Holy Grail." The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel).
Source: Weston, From Ritual to Romance, ch. XII (Gutenberg #4090); Eliot's headnote, 1922; "The Frontiers of Criticism", 1956.

### frazer
Problem: Readings stated as facts ("Stetson's planted corpse, Phlebas in the current ... are Frazer's"; Eliot's note links only the Hanged Man to Frazer). "The whole question of whether April can bring anything back" is a flourish. The Turner sentence ends on a twist and leaves its point, that this is the Sibyl of the epigraph, unsaid.
Proposed:
note: Frazer's twelve-volume comparative study of magic and religion, which Eliot's headnote says "has influenced our generation profoundly," argued that behind the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris, and by implication of Christ, lay one agricultural drama: the god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by ritual, a king sacrificed, an effigy buried or drowned. The two volumes Eliot names describe the "gardens of Adonis," pots of quick-sprouting seed carried out with images of the dead god and "flung with them into the sea," and the effigies of Osiris "made of earth and corn" that the priests buried so that the grain would sprout from the god's body. Eliot's note on the Tarot says the Hanged Man "is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer"; the corpse planted in Stetson's garden (line 71) and Phlebas in the current (line 312) have been read against the same rites. Frazer's title and his opening page come from Turner's painting <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1834), which he took for a view of Lake Nemi; it in fact shows Lake Avernus and the Sibyl of Cumae holding the bough that admits Aeneas to the underworld, the Sibyl of the poem's epigraph.
Source: Frazer, abridged edition, chs. XXXIII and XL (wording checked); Eliot's note on line 46; Wikipedia, The Golden Bough (painting).

### petronius
Problem: The closing epigram ("A prophetess kept as a curiosity; a poem introduced by a fat man's boast") is one the brief names. In Ovid the Sibyl foretells her shrinking; it has not yet happened. The one piece of history a reader wants here, the Conrad epigraph Pound talked Eliot out of, is missing.
Proposed:
note: The speaker is Trimalchio, the freed slave grown enormously rich whose dinner party fills most of what survives of the <i>Satyricon</i>; he is boasting to his guests of the wonders he has seen. The Sibyl of Cumae, in Ovid's telling (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV), asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand, forgot to ask for youth to go with them, and foresees that she will shrink until only her voice is left. The typescript of the poem carried an epigraph from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, ending "The horror! the horror!"; Pound doubted that Conrad was weighty enough for the place, and Eliot substituted Petronius before publication.
Source: Ovid, Met. XIV.130–153; the 1971 facsimile, p. 3; Eliot–Pound letters of January 1922 (Letters, vol. 1).

Proposed:

## The batch as a whole

The facts in this batch are mostly right, and the passages and translations are accurate throughout; I found one outright error of fact (Ophelia's exit), one misquotation of Eliot (the Middleton phrase), one miscount (four words for five), and a handful of loose claims (Weston's Syrian merchants, "Harvard senior", "dry stone" from Ezekiel, the site's dedication printed as if it were in the 1922 text). The recurring fault is the one Ethan named: readings presented as facts ("are Frazer's", "becomes", "never allows the change") and a closing sentence that performs, in about half the records. The cure in nearly every case was the same: say what Eliot's note cites, word the critics' links as links, and end on the last fact rather than on a bow. Two records pass as they stand and several more needed only a sentence changed.
