# Round 1, reader, batch F (the 113 pathway stop notes)

Read as a first-time reader with the stop's line open beside the box. Line numbers below are the site's count. Where a correction cites "Eliot's note" I have checked the wording against the 1922 notes; from about line 367 Eliot's own note numbers run one behind the site's count, so the corrections say "Eliot's note on this line" rather than quote his number.

## Accepted (ids of notes that read well as they stand)

first-8, first-12, water-13, women-8, women-11, cities-5, cities-8, cities-9, birds-3

These say who or what is there, in a sentence or a plain gloss, and do not perform. first-8 quotes Eliot's note correctly and its last sentence is an instruction, not a wink. first-12 tells the reader what to do with eight lines they cannot yet parse. water-13's "The longest drought" is a label of the kind the brief allows. cities-5, -8 and -9 are glossary entries. birds-3 identifies "Tereu", which is what a reader needs there. women-11 is plain; whether "soldiers'" goes beyond Eliot's note is a question for the scholar (see unsettled).

## Objections, one per record

### first-1
Problem: The last sentence, "The poem is about things that will not finish", is the bow-tying epigram, a reading of the whole poem passed off as fact at line 1. The instruction and the count before it are useful and true.
Corrected: Start with the sound. Read the first seven lines aloud. Five of them end on a participle (breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding) and run on into the line below, so that no sentence comes to rest where a line does.

### first-2
Problem: Three fragments and a colon, and "the poem's method for four hundred lines" is a flourish. The reader is not told who was speaking before or what kind of voice this is.
Corrected: A new speaker begins here, and nothing marks the change but the tone. Marie's memories of Munich and the sled (8–18) give way to a voice that sounds like a prophet's; "Son of man" is how God addresses Ezekiel, in the verse Eliot's note cites. Expect this throughout the poem. Speakers come and go without being introduced.

### first-3
Problem: "The one moment of feeling in Part I" is a superlative stated as fact; "two lines of Wagner" is loose (the first quotation is four lines); "Hold on to" tells the reader to remember a phrase without saying why. Nothing says what the German is.
Corrected: The German on either side (31–34 and 42) is from Wagner's opera <i>Tristan und Isolde</i>, which Eliot's notes name: first a sailor's song, then the shepherd's report of an empty sea [to check the two descriptions]. Between the two quotations, someone remembers coming back from a garden with the hyacinth girl and being unable to speak or see. Read lines 37 to 41 slowly, and notice "I knew nothing"; the poem will say the word again in other voices.

### first-4
Problem: "who is also the poem's prophet" and "Everything she deals comes true" are readings stated as fact (not every card returns), and "Note the card she cannot find" points without saying why. Eliot's own note says what the reader needs.
Corrected: Madame Sosostris reads tarot cards, and Eliot's note on line 46 says he used the pack "to suit my own convenience" and that the Phoenician Sailor, the Merchant and the "crowds of people" all "appear later". Watch for them: the drowned sailor at 312, the crowd at 62, the one-eyed merchant at 209. The card she cannot find, the Hanged Man, Eliot links in the same note to the hooded figure of Part V (360–366).

### first-5
Problem: "The real London: a bridge, a street, a clock" is the aphorism-and-list shape the brief names, and "The poem's trick, learned from Baudelaire" is a knowing aside. Dante and Baudelaire are dropped without a word of what each contributes.
Corrected: London Bridge, King William Street, which runs up from it into the City, and St Mary Woolnoth at the top of the street are all real [to check the geography]. Line 63 is from Dante's <i>Inferno</i> (canto iii), where the crowd of the dead is so large, and Eliot's note on "Unreal City" quotes Baudelaire on a Paris where ghosts accost passers-by in daylight. Eliot's notes ask you to see the morning crowd and Dante's dead as one crowd.

### first-6
Problem: "A conversation in which only one person speaks" is a paradox for effect (his lines are on the page), and the last sentence states a reading of the title as fact, without its source.
Corrected: Her lines are in quotation marks and his are not. Try reading hers aloud and his silently, as replies he does not say; that is one way the punctuation has been read. The section's title comes from a game of chess in Middleton's play <i>Women Beware Women</i>, which Eliot's note on line 138 cites; in the play the game is played downstairs while a seduction goes on upstairs [to check].

### first-7
Problem: Three fragments; "most ordinary speech, and its most overheard" is a paired flourish, and "the poem's clock running out" describes the poem instead of the line. Nothing says what "demobbed" or the capitals mean.
Corrected: A woman in a pub retells what she said to her friend Lil about Lil's husband Albert, who is coming home from the army ("demobbed" is demobilised). The capitals, HURRY UP PLEASE IT'S TIME, are the landlord calling time at closing. Read it aloud; it is the plainest talk in the poem, and the landlord's call has been read as the poem's own warning that time is short.

### first-9
Problem: "The river sings" and "This is the poem as music:" are the fragment and the aphorism with a colon. The stop should say what begins here and where the refrain comes from.
Corrected: Eliot's note says the song of the three Thames-daughters begins here; it is modelled on the Rhine-daughters of Wagner's <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, whose cry the refrain "Weialala leia" copies. The lines shorten to two or three words. Read them aloud for their rhythm first; the sense (barges on the tide, then Queen Elizabeth on the river) can wait for a second reading.

### first-10
Problem: Four stacked fragments, and "gently" is a flourish. The tarot link is right but not explained.
Corrected: Part IV is ten lines long. Phlebas is the drowned Phoenician Sailor of Madame Sosostris's card (47), and her warning, "Fear death by water" (55), is carried out here, as Eliot's note on the cards says. Read it twice, slowly.

### first-11
Problem: This is the "twenty-nine lines of rock before two words of rain" count that the owner singled out, and "Let it be as dry as it is" is a wink.
Corrected: From here to line 359 the lines ask for water and there is none. Read them at their own pace, without hurrying toward relief. The rain does not come until line 395, and then only as "a damp gust / Bringing rain".

### water-1
Problem: Quotes the visible line, then states a reading ("unwelcome") as fact.
Corrected: The first water in the poem is spring rain, in the sentence that calls April "the cruellest month". The pathway follows water through the poem, and the places where it is missing.

### water-2
Problem: "the first of the poem's many sounds of water that are not there" is a turn for effect. The useful fact is the echo in Part V.
Corrected: This is the first dry place in the poem, and the first time water is missing from it. The phrase "sound of water" returns in Part V (353 and 356), where again the sound is wished for and not there.

### water-3
Problem: Fragments; "Wagner's shepherd" is dropped with no word of the opera or the scene, and the German is not translated.
Corrected: "Desolate and empty the sea" [to check translation]. Eliot's note gives the source, the third act of Wagner's <i>Tristan und Isolde</i>, where Tristan lies dying and waits for Isolde's ship, and the shepherd set to watch for it reports an empty sea. It is the poem's first sea, and there is nothing on it.

### water-4
Problem: "Ariel's pearls" assumes the reader knows <i>The Tempest</i>, and "The first death by water is a card" is a twist ending.
Corrected: Madame Sosostris's first card is the drowned Phoenician Sailor. The line in brackets is from Ariel's song in Shakespeare's <i>The Tempest</i>, about a drowned man whose eyes have turned to pearl [to check]. The sailor himself appears in Part IV, at line 312.

### water-5
Problem: "The reading's one instruction" is ambiguous (the tarot reading, not the reader's), and "the reverse of what the Grail legend advises" is a reading stated as fact.
Corrected: This is the only advice Madame Sosostris gives, and Eliot's note on the cards says it is "executed in Part IV", Death by Water (312–321). In the Grail legend that Eliot's headnote sends readers to (Jessie Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>), water is what the dry land waits for; the card's warning has been read as running the other way.

### water-6
Problem: "a room with no water in it but the hot water at ten" is a wink.
Corrected: The line from Ariel's song comes back (it was line 48, on the drowned sailor's card), in the reply of the man in the room; Eliot's note here points back to the garden and the card. The only water in the scene is "The hot water at ten" (135), one item in the day's routine.

### water-7
Problem: "Ophelia leaves the pub. Her next scene is the brook." is a compressed joke; nothing says that the line is Ophelia's or where it comes from. Eliot's notes do not cite it, so a rewrite must not say they do.
Corrected: The line that ends the pub scene is Ophelia's, from <i>Hamlet</i>: she says it in her madness, and two scenes later the Queen reports that she has drowned in a brook [to check]. The pub's goodnights turn into hers.

### water-8
Problem: "A wedding river with the wedding removed" is an epigram, and Spenser is not named.
Corrected: The refrain is from Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596), a song for a double wedding in which nymphs gather flowers on the bank of the Thames; Eliot's note names it. Here the nymphs "are departed", and the river is described by the rubbish it no longer carries.

### water-9
Problem: Two fragments after the quotation; "Babylon's river" needs the psalm, and "where the poem was written" overstates (the site's other stops say "finished", or Parts IV and V).
Corrected: The line rewrites Psalm 137, "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept", the song of the exiles [to check]. Leman is the French name of Lake Geneva; Eliot spent November and December 1921 at Lausanne, on its shore, and wrote the last parts of the poem there [to check against cities-6 and eliot-6].

### water-10
Problem: "The Fisher King by the gasworks" is an epigram that states a reading as fact and does not say who the Fisher King is.
Corrected: Someone fishes in a canal behind the gasworks, thinking of "the king my brother's wreck" and "the king my father's death", words Eliot's note traces to <i>The Tempest</i>. The fisherman has been read as the Fisher King of the Grail legend, the wounded king whose land lies waste, named in Eliot's headnote.

### water-11
Problem: "as it was in 1921, then as it was in 1561" is a clever pairing, and the reader is not told that a song begins here or whose barge comes next.
Corrected: Eliot's note says the song of the Thames-daughters begins here. Its first verse is the working river of the 1920s, with oil, tar and barges on the tide; the second (279–291) is the same river under Elizabeth I, in a barge with the Earl of Leicester, a scene Eliot's note takes from Froude's history of her reign [to check the date 1561].

### water-12
Problem: Three fragments.
Corrected: Phlebas is the drowned Phoenician Sailor of Madame Sosostris's card (47), and this is the death by water she warned of (55); Eliot's note on the cards says Death by Water "is executed in Part IV". The water here is a current under the sea, picking his bones.

### water-14
Problem: "A hymn to a sound" is a metaphor for the passage, and "a bird that imitates water" leaves the bird unnamed.
Corrected: The wish for water narrows to a wish for its sound. The bird at 357 is the hermit-thrush; Eliot's note says its "water-dripping song" is "justly celebrated". The "Drip drop" of 358 is the bird, not water, and line 359 says so.

### water-15
Problem: "Two words." is the counting flourish.
Corrected: This is the only rain that falls in the poem, in a damp gust after the cock crows and the lightning flashes. The next line moves to India, where the rain is still waited for.

### water-16
Problem: A fragment; "the monsoon" is a reading, and the two Sanskrit names are not explained.
Corrected: Ganga is the Ganges and Himavant the Himalaya. The river is low, and the rain that would fill it waits in black clouds over the mountains; the thunder that speaks at line 400 comes from there.

### water-17
Problem: "Water at last as something that answers a hand" is a poetic summary where a plain one would do. The observation is right.
Corrected: For the first time in the poem the water is calm, and something is done on it well; the boat answers "the hand expert with sail and oar". The line follows the thunder's third word, Damyata, which Eliot's note glosses as "control".

### water-18
Problem: "The last water and the last drought, in one sentence" is a paired flourish. The line needs its link to 189 and to Eliot's note.
Corrected: The fisherman recalls the one at line 189, now on a shore with the dry land behind him. This is the poem's last water and its last dry ground, and Eliot's note on the line sends the reader to the chapter on the Fisher King in Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>.

### dead-1
Problem: A fragment, and the epigraph is in Latin and Greek; the reader needs to be told what it says and where it is from.
Corrected: The epigraph is from Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i>. The speaker says he saw with his own eyes the Sibyl of Cumae hanging in a jar, and that when boys asked her, "Sibyl, what do you want?", she answered, "I want to die." In the legend she had been granted a long life without lasting youth [to check].

### dead-2
Problem: "the first thing named after April" is wrong as it stands (the lilacs are named first) and is a count for effect.
Corrected: The first thing the poem calls dead is the land, in its second line, and lilacs are what grow out of it.

### dead-3
Problem: "Ezekiel's valley, Ecclesiastes' long home" are two allusions dropped without a word of what they are; and Eliot's note cites Ezekiel 2:1 ("Son of man"), not the valley of dry bones, so the valley is a reading.
Corrected: Eliot's notes send this passage to two books of the Bible. "Son of man" (20) is how God addresses the prophet Ezekiel, and line 23 echoes Ecclesiastes 12:5, where the grasshopper is a burden and man goes "to his long home", the grave. The dead tree and the dry bones of Ezekiel's valley (chapter 37) have also been heard here, though Eliot's note does not cite them [to check].

### dead-4
Problem: The quotation is the line the reader is looking at, and "The hyacinth garden" adds only a label.
Corrected: Coming back from the hyacinth garden, the speaker was "neither / Living nor dead". The phrase has a match in Part V (328–329), "He who was living is now dead / We who were living are now dying".

### dead-5
Problem: A colon standing in for a sentence; Dante is named but not the poem or the scene.
Corrected: Line 63 is from Dante's <i>Inferno</i> (canto iii), as Eliot's note says. Just inside the gate of Hell, Dante sees so long a train of the dead that he "had not thought death had undone so many"; they are the souls who lived without praise or blame [to check].

### dead-6
Problem: "Frazer's buried god, in a suburb" states a reading as fact, does not say who Frazer is, and "suburb" is not in the poem.
Corrected: Stetson is asked whether the corpse buried in his garden has begun to sprout. Eliot's headnote names Frazer's <i>The Golden Bough</i>, a study of ancient rites in which a god or his effigy is buried so that the crops will rise; the question has been read as that rite carried out in a back garden.

### dead-7
Problem: "Webster's dirge" is dropped without the play, and "for unburied men" is not what the song is (it is sung over a murdered son's body). The change from wolf to dog is the fact worth keeping.
Corrected: Lines 74–75 rework a song from John Webster's play <i>The White Devil</i> (1612), which Eliot's note cites. A mother sings it over her murdered son, "But keep the wolf far thence, that's foe to men, / For with his nails he'll dig them up again" [to check]. Eliot makes the wolf a dog and the foe a friend.

### dead-8
Problem: "The trenches." is the reading stated as fact that the task names; the poem does not say it.
Corrected: The reply, in lines without quotation marks, is a place of rats and dead men's bones. "Rats' alley" has often been read as the trenches of the war that had ended three years before, though the poem does not name them.

### dead-9
Problem: The note quotes the two lines the reader is looking at and adds nothing.
Corrected: Bones and a rat again, as at 115–116; here the bones lie in a garret and are heard by the fisherman at the canal. Eliot's note on line 115 points forward to this passage [to check: the 1922 note reads "Cf. Part III, l. 195"].

### dead-10
Problem: The note quotes the visible line and adds only the name. Nothing says who Tiresias is or where he walked among the dead.
Corrected: Tiresias is the blind prophet of Thebes. In Homer's <i>Odyssey</i> (book xi) Odysseus goes down to the dead to consult him, which is what "walked among the lowest of the dead" remembers [to check]; the story of his years as a woman is in the passage from Ovid that Eliot's note quotes.

### dead-11
Problem: A label that repeats the visible line.
Corrected: Phlebas is the drowned Phoenician Sailor of Madame Sosostris's card (47), and this is the death by water she warned of (55). The lines follow him after his death, from what he forgets to the current that picks his bones.

### dead-12
Problem: The note quotes the two visible lines and adds nothing.
Corrected: The lines before this (322–327) have been read as the arrest and death of Christ, from the torchlight in the garden to the agony [to check]. Eliot's note on Part V names "the journey to Emmaus" as its first theme, the walk on which the risen Christ went unrecognised. The "he" who is dead is not named.

### dead-13
Problem: "The Chapel Perilous" is dropped without a word of what it is.
Corrected: Eliot's note on Part V names "the approach to the Chapel Perilous" as one of its themes and sends readers to Jessie Weston's book, where the knight who seeks the Grail must pass a night of terrors in a chapel with a corpse in it [to check]. Here the chapel is empty, the door swings, and the bones are only bones.

### dead-14
Problem: A colon and a list of three restating lines 408–410.
Corrected: Lines 408 to 410 list what is left after a death, the memories, the wills and the empty rooms, and say that the "moment's surrender" of line 404 is to be found in none of them.

### dead-15
Problem: "Ugolino" is dropped with no word of who he is.
Corrected: Eliot's note sends the key to Dante's <i>Inferno</i> (canto xxxiii), where Count Ugolino, locked in a tower at Pisa with his sons and left to starve, remembers hearing the door below being nailed shut [to check]. The same note quotes the philosopher F. H. Bradley on each mind being a circle "closed on the outside".

### women-1
Problem: A fragment that stops before the point; the reader is not told what she answers or where the epigraph is from. And "bottle" here against "jar" in dead-1.
Corrected: The epigraph, from Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i>, describes the Sibyl of Cumae hanging in a jar, and boys asking her, "Sibyl, what do you want?" She answers that she wants to die. She is the first woman in the poem, and the first to be quoted.

### women-2
Problem: "frightened and free" is a paired flourish, and the caption has no verb.
Corrected: Marie, the first speaker in the poem to be given a name, remembers a sled ride as a child at her cousin the archduke's, when she was frightened and he told her to hold on.

### women-3
Problem: Three fragments and a count.
Corrected: The hyacinth girl speaks two lines, in quotation marks, and is answered by someone who remembers the garden and could not speak. She is not named and does not speak again.

### women-4
Problem: A rhythmic list of three ending in "right about everything", a reading stated as fact. "Afraid of the police" is a gloss presented as plain description.
Corrected: Madame Sosostris, a fortune-teller, has a cold and is careful about who sees her horoscopes; "One must be so careful these days" has been read as a nod to the law against fortune-telling [to check]. Several of the cards she deals come true later in the poem, as Eliot's note on line 46 says.

### women-5
Problem: A colon and a list of three, with none of the three explained.
Corrected: Belladonna is Italian for "beautiful lady" and the name of deadly nightshade, a poison; "the Lady of the Rocks" recalls Leonardo's painting <i>The Virgin of the Rocks</i>. She is a card of Madame Sosostris's own, not one of the tarot pack [to check].

### women-6
Problem: A colon, a list of three, and a twist ("made into furniture"); the sources are named without their works.
Corrected: The lines describing her room borrow from three earlier poems, which Eliot's notes name. Line 77 rewrites Shakespeare's description of Cleopatra's barge in <i>Antony and Cleopatra</i>, the "laquearia" of line 92 are the panelled ceiling of Dido's palace in Virgil's <i>Aeneid</i>, and the "sylvan scene" of line 98 is Eden in Milton's <i>Paradise Lost</i>. The woman herself is not described until her hair at line 108, and she is never named.

### women-7
Problem: Nearly plain, but "Philomel" is dropped without her story's source, and "Jug Jug" is not explained.
Corrected: Philomel, in Ovid's <i>Metamorphoses</i> (Eliot's note gives the book), was raped by her sister's husband King Tereus, who cut out her tongue; the gods changed her into a nightingale. The picture over the mantel shows the change, and "Jug Jug" is how Elizabethan poets wrote the nightingale's song [to check].

### women-9
Problem: A stacked list ("thirty-one, five children, no teeth"), and "who tells her the truth" is a judgement. "No teeth" is inferred; the poem says Albert gave her money for a new set.
Corrected: Lil is thirty-one, has had five children and nearly died of one of them, and took pills "to bring it off", that is, to end another pregnancy; her husband Albert, back from four years in the army, had given her money for new teeth. We hear all this from her friend, who is telling the pub what she told Lil.

### women-10
Problem: "Ophelia, leaving." is a wink; nothing says whose line it is or where it comes from. Eliot's notes do not cite it.
Corrected: The last line is Ophelia's, from <i>Hamlet</i>. She says it in her madness, as she leaves the stage for the last time; two scenes later her drowning is reported [to check]. Her goodnight takes over from the goodnights of the pub.

### women-12
Problem: The quotation is from line 255, not the line the reader is looking at, and it is not placed.
Corrected: The typist comes home, and is visited by a clerk; when he has gone she "smooths her hair with automatic hand" (255) and puts on a record. Tiresias, who watches, says he has "foresuffered all".

### women-13
Problem: "talking nonsense" reads as a wink unless the reader knows it is quoted from Eliot's note; the note itself is the thing to give.
Corrected: Elizabeth I and her favourite, Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, on the Thames in a barge. Eliot's note quotes the Spanish ambassador's report of the afternoon: alone with Dudley on the poop of the barge, the queen "began to talk nonsense", and Dudley said there was no reason they should not be married if she pleased.

### women-14
Problem: A colon and a list, and "La Pia of Siena" is dropped with nothing to say who she is or why she is behind the first daughter.
Corrected: Eliot's note says the three Thames-daughters speak in turn from here to 306, and each names her places: Highbury, Richmond and Kew; Moorgate; Margate. The first one's "Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me" copies a line Eliot's note quotes from Dante's <i>Purgatorio</i>, spoken by La Pia, a woman of Siena murdered by her husband in the marshes of the Maremma: "Siena made me, Maremma unmade me" [to check].

### women-15
Problem: Two readings stated as fact, neither sourced.
Corrected: The line has been read as the weeping of the women at the crucifixion, and as the grief of a Europe in ruin after the war; Eliot's note on this passage quotes Hermann Hesse on "half of Europe" on its way to chaos [to check the first reading].

### women-16
Problem: A caption that paraphrases the visible line and adds nothing.
Corrected: A woman fiddles on her own hair as if it were the strings of an instrument. She is the last woman in the poem, and her hair has been linked to the hair "spread out in fiery points" of the lady in the chair (108–110) [to check].

### cities-1
Problem: A colon standing in for a sentence, and the two names are not glossed (a lake and a garden).
Corrected: The Starnberger See is a lake south of Munich, and the Hofgarten is a public garden in the city. Eliot spent August 1911 in Munich [to check the month].

### cities-2
Problem: "by way of a sentence in German" is a knowing aside, and the sentence is not translated.
Corrected: The German means "I am not Russian at all, I come from Lithuania, a true German" [to check translation]. The speaker is not named.

### cities-3
Problem: A colon doing a verb's work; Baudelaire is named without the poem or the words Eliot's note quotes.
Corrected: The city is London, named by its bridge and its streets in the lines that follow. Eliot's note on this line quotes Baudelaire on Paris, "swarming city, city full of dreams, where the ghost in broad daylight accosts the passer-by" [to check translation].

### cities-4
Problem: Two fragments, and "Eliot's route to work" is a biographical claim stated as fact without a source.
Corrected: King William Street runs from London Bridge up into the City, and St Mary Woolnoth is the church at the top of it, at the corner of Lombard Street, where Eliot worked at Lloyds Bank from 1917 [to check]. Eliot's note on line 68 says the church's "dead sound" on the stroke of nine is "a phenomenon which I have often noticed".

### cities-6
Problem: Leman is the lake, not the town, so the gloss is wrong; and "finished" here against "written" in water-9.
Corrected: Leman is Lake Geneva, by its French name. Eliot spent November and December 1921 at Lausanne, on its shore, and finished the poem there [to check against water-9 and eliot-6].

### cities-7
Problem: True, but the fire came after the poem was written and just before it was published, and a first-time reader will take the note to mean the poem alludes to it.
Corrected: Smyrna, now Izmir, was a port on the coast of Asia Minor and a centre of the dried-fruit trade [to check]. The city burned in September 1922, a year after the poem was written and a month before it was first printed [to check the dates].

### cities-10
Problem: A bare list of names, with nothing said about any of them.
Corrected: Four real places, running east from the West End into the City: the Strand, Queen Victoria Street, Lower Thames Street, and the church of St Magnus the Martyr beside London Bridge [to check]. Eliot's note calls its interior "one of the finest among Wren's interiors" and cites a pamphlet against the demolition of City churches.

### cities-11
Problem: Two names, both already on the page.
Corrected: Two places on the Thames below London Bridge. Greenwich Reach is the stretch of river at Greenwich, and the Isle of Dogs is the loop of land the river bends round opposite it, in 1921 covered by docks [to check].

### cities-12
Problem: A bare list, already on the page.
Corrected: Five places in the three Thames-daughters' stories, which Eliot's note says are told in turn. Highbury is a suburb in north London; Richmond and Kew are on the river upstream of the city; Moorgate is in the City; Margate is a seaside town where the Thames meets the sea, and where Eliot wrote part of Part III in October 1921 [to check].

### cities-13
Problem: "Augustine's cauldron" is dropped with no word of who Augustine is or what the cauldron was.
Corrected: Carthage, on the coast of North Africa, where St Augustine went as a young man; Eliot's note quotes his <i>Confessions</i>, "to Carthage then I came, where a cauldron of unholy loves sang all about mine ears".

### cities-14
Problem: The note quotes the visible lines and adds nothing.
Corrected: Five cities, from Jerusalem to London, named without commas and then called "Unreal", the word used for London at lines 60 and 207. Eliot's note on this passage quotes Hermann Hesse on half of Europe on its way to chaos [to check].

### cities-15
Problem: "The poem's last places are a river and a mountain" is a flourish, and the names are not glossed.
Corrected: Ganga is the Ganges and Himavant the Himalaya, in which the river rises [to check]. After London they are the last places the poem names.

### birds-1
Problem: "Not a bird, but the first singer" is a paired antithesis, and Ecclesiastes is dropped without the verse.
Corrected: A cricket, not a bird, is the first singing creature in the poem. Eliot's note sends the line to Ecclesiastes 12:5, "the grasshopper shall be a burden".

### birds-2
Problem: Three fragments; nothing says who Philomel is or what "Jug Jug" means.
Corrected: Philomel, in the story Eliot's note sends to Ovid's <i>Metamorphoses</i>, was raped by King Tereus, who cut out her tongue, and was changed into a nightingale. "Jug Jug" is how Elizabethan poets wrote the nightingale's song [to check].

### birds-4
Problem: Quotes the visible line and adds nothing the line does not say.
Corrected: Gulls, the birds a sailor hears; Phlebas, a fortnight dead, has forgotten them along with the swell of the sea and "the profit and loss".

### birds-5
Problem: "not a bird and not water" is a twist on the previous stop.
Corrected: The cicada, like the cricket of line 23, is a singer that is not a bird. The lines want the sound of water and get instead the dry sound of an insect and of grass.

### birds-6
Problem: "Listen to it" is a bare instruction; if the site has a recording the note should say where, and if not it is a wink. Eliot's note says more than "Quebec", and the next line needs explaining.
Corrected: Eliot's note names the bird, the hermit-thrush, says he heard it "in Quebec County", and calls its "water-dripping song" "justly celebrated". The "Drip drop drip drop" of the next line is the bird's song, and line 359 says there is still no water.

### birds-7
Problem: "Not birds. Included for the wings." is two fragments and a knowing aside about the pathway's own rules.
Corrected: Bats are not birds, but they whistle and "beat their wings", and they are the only winged creatures in this part of the poem, so the pathway stops for them.

### birds-8
Problem: "Peter's cock, Hamlet's, and the French farmyard" is a list of three with nothing explained.
Corrected: "Co co rico" is a cock's crow as French spells it. A cock crowing at first light has been read as the cock of the Gospels, whose crow marked Peter's denial of Christ, and as the one in <i>Hamlet</i> whose crow sends the ghost away [to check]; here it is followed by lightning and the poem's only rain.

### birds-9
Problem: "Procne" is dropped with no word of the myth or of the two sources behind the line.
Corrected: The Latin before it, from the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i> (Eliot's note names it), asks when the speaker will be like the swallow, and "O swallow swallow" is from Tennyson's <i>The Princess</i> [to check]. In the myth of Philomel, her sister Procne was changed into a swallow, so the nightingale of Parts II and III is answered here by her sister; Eliot's note points back to Philomela.

### nothing-1
Problem: Quotes the two lines the reader is looking at and adds nothing.
Corrected: The first "nothing" in the poem is the speaker's, back from the hyacinth garden, when he could not speak, his eyes failed, and he "knew nothing".

### nothing-2
Problem: Quotes the visible line and adds nothing.
Corrected: The man's reply to "What is the wind doing?" In this scene his lines have no quotation marks, and have been read as thoughts he does not say aloud.

### nothing-3
Problem: Quotes the visible lines and adds nothing.
Corrected: She asks three questions in a row, and "Nothing" is set on a line of its own.

### nothing-4
Problem: Quotes the visible line and adds nothing.
Corrected: The last "nothing" in this scene. His answer, unspoken, is a line from a popular song of 1912 [to check], "that Shakespeherian Rag".

### nothing-5
Problem: Quotes the visible lines and adds nothing.
Corrected: The third Thames-daughter, on Margate Sands, in the last of the three speeches that Eliot's note says they make in turn.

### nothing-6
Problem: Quotes the visible lines and adds nothing.
Corrected: The same speaker. "Nothing" ends her speech and the Thames-daughters' song, on a line of its own.

### nothing-7
Problem: "The question that answers the word" is a riddle-epigram.
Corrected: The pathway ends at the thunder's first question. Eliot's note glosses "Datta" as "give"; after the poem's nothings, what is asked is what has been given, and the answer (403–406) is a moment's surrender.

### eyes-1
Problem: A colon in place of a sentence, and "the Sibyl" is not placed.
Corrected: The speaker of the epigraph, from Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i>, says he saw the Sibyl of Cumae in her jar "with my own eyes" (<i>oculis meis vidi</i>). The pathway follows the poem's eyes from here to Part V.

### eyes-2
Problem: Quotes the visible words and adds nothing.
Corrected: Back from the hyacinth garden, the speaker could not speak and his eyes failed; the sentence runs on to "the heart of light, the silence".

### eyes-3
Problem: Quotes the visible line and adds nothing.
Corrected: The line in brackets is from Ariel's song in Shakespeare's <i>The Tempest</i>, where a drowned man's eyes have become pearls [to check]. It is said again at line 125, in the room in Part II, and Eliot's note there points back to this card.

### eyes-4
Problem: Quotes the visible words and adds nothing.
Corrected: A card that is not in the real tarot pack [to check]. Eliot's note on Tiresias calls the one-eyed merchant the "seller of currants", which makes him Mr Eugenides of line 209, and says he "melts into the Phoenician Sailor".

### eyes-5
Problem: Quotes the visible line and adds nothing.
Corrected: The commuters on London Bridge look down as they walk. Eliot's note on the sighs of line 64 cites Limbo in Dante's <i>Inferno</i> (canto iv), where the dead sigh but do not weep [to check].

### eyes-6
Problem: Quotes the visible line and adds nothing.
Corrected: One of two golden Cupids carved on the frame of the glass; the other "peeped out" (80). Eliot's note on line 77 sends the passage to Shakespeare's Cleopatra, whose barge had boys "like smiling Cupids" beside her [to check].

### eyes-7
Problem: Quotes the visible words and adds nothing.
Corrected: The last line of the scene in the room. The couple will play chess and wait for a knock, with eyes that cannot close; Eliot's note sends the game of chess to Middleton's play <i>Women Beware Women</i>.

### eyes-8
Problem: Quotes the visible lines and adds nothing.
Corrected: Office workers at the end of the day look up from their desks. This is "the violet hour", and the lines lead to Tiresias, who is blind and yet "can see".

### eyes-9
Problem: Quotes the visible words and adds nothing.
Corrected: Tiresias was blinded and given the gift of prophecy in return, in the story Eliot's note quotes from Ovid [to check]. His seeing, the note says, "is the substance of the poem".

### eyes-10
Problem: Quotes the visible words and adds nothing.
Corrected: The clerk's stare is the only thing said about his eyes. When he has gone, the typist "looks a moment in the glass" (249).

### eyes-11
Problem: "Eyes that do not know him" is a reading in the form of an epigram, and its source (the Emmaus story, "their eyes were holden") is not given; nor is the Antarctic account in Eliot's note.
Corrected: Eliot's note says these lines were suggested by an account of an Antarctic expedition, on which the exhausted party "had the constant delusion that there was one more member than could actually be counted". His note on Part V also names the journey to Emmaus, in Luke's Gospel, where the risen Christ walks beside two disciples whose eyes "were holden that they should not know him" [to check].

### eliot-1
Problem: A colon and a list; "a king drowned" without the king.
Corrected: Eliot spent August 1911 in Munich [to check]. The Hofgarten is a public garden there with a colonnade along one side, and the Starnberger See is the lake south of the city in which King Ludwig II of Bavaria drowned in 1886 [to check].

### eliot-2
Problem: Two names dropped with no word of who they were, offered as if the reader could choose between them.
Corrected: Two people from Eliot's life have been proposed as the figure behind the hyacinth garden. One is Emily Hale, the Boston woman he loved before he left America in 1914 and wrote to for decades afterwards [to check]. The other is Jean Verdenal, his friend in Paris in 1910–11, whom he later remembered crossing the Luxembourg Gardens waving a branch of lilac, and who was killed in the war in 1915 [to check]; the poem names neither.

### eliot-3
Problem: "The clock struck nine as he arrived" is a biographical scene presented as fact, with no source; "across the road" needs checking. Eliot's own note is the better evidence.
Corrected: From 1917 Eliot worked at Lloyds Bank in Lombard Street, close to St Mary Woolnoth [to check "across the road"]. Eliot's note on line 68 says the church's dead sound on the stroke of nine is "a phenomenon which I have often noticed".

### eliot-4
Problem: Two fragments; "Vivien" is not identified.
Corrected: In 1921 the Eliots lived in a flat at 9 Clarence Gate Gardens, near Regent's Park [to check]. Eliot's wife Vivien had bad nerves and poor health, and Eliot himself broke down in the autumn of 1921; the scene has often been read with their marriage in mind, though the poem does not name them.

### eliot-5
Problem: "who told them about Lil" presents a character as a real acquaintance, and the source of the fact is not given.
Corrected: Ellen Kellond was the Eliots' maid. The pub monologue was based on a story she told them, according to the notes to the facsimile edition of the drafts (1971) [to check].

### eliot-6
Problem: A colon and a list; "Dr Vittoz" is not identified.
Corrected: Eliot spent November and December 1921 at Lausanne, on Lake Geneva, being treated for nervous exhaustion by Dr Roger Vittoz, a specialist in nervous disorders [to check]. Parts IV and V were written there, and this line, with the lake under its French name, is the poem's one mention of the place.

### eliot-7
Problem: A colon and a list; "the Nayland Rock shelter" and "fifty lines" are given with no context.
Corrected: Eliot spent three weeks at Margate in October and November 1921, on leave from the bank after his breakdown, and wrote there; he told a friend he had drafted about fifty lines of Part III [to check]. The Nayland Rock shelter, a seafront shelter where he sat to write, still stands [to check].

### eliot-8
Problem: A colon; "the drowned sailor's original voyage, cut by Pound" is compressed past understanding for a first-time reader.
Corrected: In the draft, Part IV began with a long account of a fishing voyage out of Gloucester, Massachusetts, near where the Eliots spent their summers, ending in shipwreck; Pound cut it, and the ten lines on Phlebas are what remained [to check]. The Phlebas lines themselves are Eliot's English version of the end of a French poem of his published in 1918 [to check].

### eliot-9
Problem: Two fragments.
Corrected: The Eliots spent their summers at Gloucester, on Cape Ann in Massachusetts, where Eliot learned to sail as a boy; the boat that answers "the hand expert with sail and oar" has been read as a memory of those summers [to check].

### eliot-10
Problem: A colon and a list of three, and "il miglior fabbro" is dropped untranslated; the dedication was not part of the January 1922 meeting (it was first printed in 1925 [to check]).
Corrected: In January 1922, on his way home from Lausanne, Eliot stopped in Paris and went through the draft with Ezra Pound, who cut it heavily in pencil [to check]. "Il miglior fabbro", "the better craftsman", is the dedication Eliot later gave the poem, first printed in 1925 [to check]; the phrase is Dante's, for the Provençal poet Arnaut Daniel.

## Still unsettled (questions for the scholar or the editor)

1. Shape of the word-trail pathways (Nothing, Eyes). Ten of the eleven Eyes stops and six of the seven Nothing stops are the visible line quoted back with nothing added. If the box is meant to act as a highlight, the corrections above are optional; if it is meant to say something, every stop needs the one sentence I have given. Editor to decide, and to say whether the quotation should stay in the box at all when the line is beside it.
2. One voice per pathway. At present each pathway mixes bare quotations, captions without verbs, colon lists and epigrams (Water is quote-plus-tag, Cities is names only, Eliot is "place, date: three items"). I suggest one shape per pathway: First = an instruction in a sentence or two; Water, Dead, Birds = a sentence saying what is here and, where there is one, what Eliot's note says; Women, Cities = a gloss sentence saying who or where; Eliot = place and date, then one sentence of what is known and its source. Editor's call.
3. Lausanne. water-9 says the poem was "written" there, cities-6 "finished", eliot-6 "Parts IV and V". Scholar to fix one statement and the editor to use it at all three stops.
4. The Sibyl's container: "jar" (dead-1) against "bottle" (women-1) for <i>ampulla</i>. Choose one.
5. Eliot's notes do not annotate line 172 (Ophelia). The originals did not claim they do, but any rewrite of water-7 and women-10 must avoid "Eliot's note cites".
6. cities-4 "Eliot's route to work" and eliot-3 "The clock struck nine as he arrived": source? My corrections fall back on Eliot's note to line 68. "Across the road" for Lloyds and St Mary Woolnoth also needs checking.
7. eliot-10: "il miglior fabbro" was not in the December 1922 edition (first printed 1925, I believe). If the site's text is the Boni & Liveright 1922, does the dedication appear at all, and should this stop sit at the end of the poem?
8. cities-7: the fire of Smyrna postdates the poem. Keep the stop, with the dates made plain, or drop it?
9. women-11: "a soldiers' song" goes beyond Eliot's note, which says only that the ballad "was reported to me from Sydney, Australia". Scholar to confirm the gloss or cut "soldiers'".
10. birds-6 "Listen to it": does the site have a recording of the hermit-thrush? If so the corrected note should end "There is a recording on the Listen page" (or wherever it is); if not, nothing.
11. water-11: the date 1561 for De Quadra's letter needs checking; I have kept it marked.
12. dead-3: "Ezekiel's valley" (the dry bones, chapter 37) is not in Eliot's note, which cites 2:1. Keep as a reading, or cut?
13. dead-6 and dead-8: I have worded "Frazer's buried god" and "The trenches" as readings ("has been read as"). Scholar to confirm that wording is the right strength.
14. Eliot's note numbers. From about the site's line 367 his numbers run one behind (his 401 is the site's 402, his 424 the site's 425), and his note "126" is on what the site numbers 125. Corrections say "Eliot's note on this line" or "on this passage"; the editor may prefer the site's convention.
15. eyes-5: Eliot's Dante note is on the sighs of line 64, not on line 65; the correction says so.
16. Several corrections add a fact with a source the site may not yet cite (Ludwig II, Vittoz, the Gloucester draft, the 1918 French poem, Tennyson for "O swallow swallow", "That Shakespearian Rag" of 1912). All are marked [to check]; if the scholar cannot source one, cut the clause rather than the note.
