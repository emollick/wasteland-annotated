# Batch F, round 1: the scholar's report on the 113 pathway stop notes

## Checked (what you verified and where)

- Every quotation and every line reference in the 113 notes against the site's numbered text (r1/wl_numbered.txt). Exact, except where noted below: first-3 ("two lines of Wagner": the Tristan quotations are four lines, 31-34, and one line, 42), first-9 ("the lines shorten to two words": line 271 is one word, "Wide"), women-7 (the poem's quotation marks enclose "Jug Jug" only), nothing-3 (the quotation runs across the break between 121 "Do" and 122 "You know nothing?"), water-15 (a shower of rain falls at line 9), cities-15 (London Bridge at 427 and Aquitaine at 430 come after Ganga and Himavant), dead-2 (lilacs are named before the dead land), women-9 (Albert tells Lil to "have them all out", so she still has teeth).
- Counts: five participial line-ends in lines 1-7 (first-1), right; lines 331-359 are twenty-nine (first-11), right; Part IV is ten lines, 312-321 (first-10), right; the last eight lines are in English, Italian, Latin, French and Sanskrit (first-12), right, but line 431 is Eliot's own, not a quotation.
- Every appeal to Eliot's notes against the 1922 notes (r1/wl_gutenberg.txt, lines 562-1176): the headnote (Weston, Frazer, "vegetation ceremonies"); notes at 20 (Ezekiel 2:1), 23 (Ecclesiastes 12:5), 31 and 42 (Tristan), 46 (the Tarot: Hanged Man, Phoenician Sailor, Merchant, crowds, "Death by Water is executed in Part IV", Man with Three Staves as Fisher King), 60 (Baudelaire), 63-64 (Inferno iii and iv), 68 ("A phenomenon which I have often noticed"), 74 (Webster's dirge), 77 (Antony and Cleopatra), 92 (laquearia, Aeneid i), 98 (Paradise Lost iv), 99 (Ovid, Philomela), 100 and 115 (cross-references to 204 and 195), 138 (Middleton), 176 (Prothalamion), 192 (The Tempest), 199 ("reported to me from Sydney, Australia"), 218 ("What Tiresias sees, in fact, is the substance of the poem"), 264 (St Magnus Martyr), 266 (the three Thames-daughters, 292-306, the Rhine-daughters), 279 (Froude, De Quadra, "talk nonsense"), 293 (La Pia), 307 and 309 (Augustine's cauldron), the Part V headnote (Emmaus, the Chapel Perilous, eastern Europe), 357 (the hermit-thrush "which I have heard in Quebec County", its "water-dripping song"), 360 (the Antarctic expedition), 366-76 (Hesse), 401 ("Give, sympathize, control"), 407 (Webster's spider), 411 (Ugolino, Bradley), 424 (Weston's Fisher King chapter), 427-431 (Purgatorio xxvi, Pervigilium Veneris "Cf. Philomela", Nerval, Kyd), 433 (Shantih). Note that Eliot's numbers run one behind the site's from 346; the rewrites below avoid quoting his numbers for lines beyond 345.
- Epigraph and dedication in the Gutenberg text (lines 54-59): the Petronius epigraph reads "oculis meis vidi in ampulla pendere"; the Gutenberg text carries the dedication "For Ezra Pound / il miglior fabbro", which the Boni & Liveright 1922 edition did not.
- Composition: Lawrence Rainey, Revisiting The Waste Land (2005), text at epdf.pub: Parts IV and V "were composed between 21-22 November, when Eliot left for Lausanne, and 2 January, when he returned to Paris"; their typescripts were made on Pound's typewriter in Paris "between 2 January 1922, when Eliot arrived from Lausanne, and 16 January, when he left Paris to return to London." The draft of Part IV was 93 lines (Poetry Archive; NYRB, Auchincloss 1984), of which Pound left the ten on Phlebas.
- The dedication: added in print in Poems 1909-1925 (1925); handwritten by Eliot in Pound's copy of the 1922 Boni & Liveright edition (Wikipedia, The Waste Land; Poetry Foundation exhibit "Better Craftsmen, Not Gods", as reported in search). The phrase is Purgatorio xxvi. 117, of Arnaut Daniel.
- Elizabeth and Leicester: De Quadra's letter to Philip II of 30 June 1561, in Froude's History of England vol. 7 (Wikiquote, Robert Dudley), so "1561" in water-11 is right.
- Lloyds Bank: Eliot began as a clerk in the Colonial and Foreign Department on 19 March 1917 and left in the autumn of 1925 for Faber & Gwyer (tseliot.com, "T. S. Eliot at Lloyds Bank").
- 9 Clarence Gate Gardens: the Eliots moved there in October 1920 (clarencegategardens.org.uk, History); other accounts say November, so the rewrite says "the autumn of 1920".
- Munich: Eliot's own statement that most of Prufrock "was written in the summer of 1911 when I was in Munich"; Peter Cash's English Association bookmark: "In July he left for a holiday in Munich and Northern Italy". So "August 1911" (cities-1, eliot-1) is too narrow; "the summer of 1911" is what the sources support.
- Ludwig II of Bavaria was found dead in the shallows of Lake Starnberg on 13 June 1886; the official finding was drowning, which is still contested (Wikipedia, Ludwig II of Bavaria). Hence "found drowned", not "drowned", in eliot-1.
- Smyrna burned 13-22 September 1922 (Wikipedia, Burning of Smyrna). The Criterion printing was October 1922.
- Mylae, 260 BC, First Punic War, Rome's first victory at sea (Wikipedia, Battle of Mylae).
- Verdenal: Eliot's Criterion commentary of April 1934, "a friend coming across the Luxembourg Gardens in the late afternoon, waving a branch of lilac" (NYRB, Miller 1978; Jonathan Bate's substack). Quoted below as a phrase only, as the brief allows.
- Emily Hale: Eliot's declaration to her before leaving for Europe in 1914, at Chestnut Hill (tseliot.com, Eliot-Hale letters chronology).
- Gloucester: the family's summer house on Eastern Point, built 1896; Eliot's boyhood sailing and fishermen (tseliot.com, "T. S. Eliot's Gloucester"; Gloucester HarborWalk).
- Pervigilium Veneris: in the poem's own version of the myth Philomela is the nightingale and Procne the swallow (Loeb, Pervigilium Veneris), so birds-9 stands; Eliot's note adds "Cf. Philomela".
- Mrs Porter: the ballad was sung by Australian troops in the war, on the tune of "Red Wing" (Great War Fiction, "Mrs Porter"; Southam). Eliot's note gives only "Sydney, Australia".
- The Metropole: Southam glosses it as Brighton ("a weekend in Brighton" carrying sexual implications, Student's Guide, 1971, p. 83, quoted at literarylondon.org); there was also a Hotel Metropole in London. So Brighton is an identification, not a fact of the poem.
- Fortune-tellers were liable to prosecution under the Vagrancy Act 1824 (National Archives, "Fortune Telling and Palmistry: prosecutions under the Vagrancy Act, 1824"), which supports "afraid of the police" as a reading, not a fact.
- Hamlet: Ophelia's "Good night, ladies" is her exit in IV. v; Gertrude reports the drowning in IV. vii. Webster: Cornelia's dirge, The White Devil V. iv ("keep the wolf far thence, that's foe to men"). Middleton, Women Beware Women II. ii: Livia keeps the Mother at chess while Bianca is seduced above.
- Rulings from the other batches applied without reopening: no "route to work" (cities-4, first-5); no "Lombard Street" for the bank and no "clock struck nine as he arrived" (eliot-3); Margate from mid-October 1921, the Nayland Rock shelter, "some fifty lines" to Schiff on 4 November (eliot-7); Lausanne under Dr Vittoz, November and December 1921 (eliot-6, water-9, cities-6); Vivien's lines 153 and 164 (women-9, eliot-5); Pound's letter of 24 January 1922 (eliot-10); readings worded as readings (dead-6, dead-8, birds-8 and the like).

## Accepted (ids of notes that are right as they stand, on substance)

first-2, first-8, first-10; water-2, water-3, water-4, water-6, water-11, water-13; dead-1, dead-4, dead-5, dead-7, dead-9, dead-10, dead-11, dead-12, dead-13, dead-15; women-1, women-2, women-3, women-8, women-10, women-12, women-13, women-14, women-16; cities-2, cities-3, cities-5, cities-9, cities-10, cities-11, cities-12, cities-13, cities-14; birds-2, birds-3, birds-4, birds-5, birds-6, birds-9; nothing-1, nothing-2, nothing-4, nothing-5, nothing-6; eyes-1, eyes-2, eyes-3, eyes-4, eyes-5, eyes-6, eyes-7, eyes-8, eyes-9, eyes-10.

That is 58 of 113. Most are bare quotations or a name and a line, and they are right. Among them, wording the reader may still want to look at, on which I have no objection of substance: first-2 ("the poem's method for four hundred lines"), first-10 (four fragments), water-4 and water-6 (the closing aside), women-14 ("And La Pia of Siena behind the first"), dead-15 ("nailed shut" is Singleton's and Mandelbaum's rendering of Dante's chiavar; Longfellow and Eliot's own "key" have it locked; either is defensible). birds-6 ends "Listen to it.", which is fine if the stop plays a recording and an empty instruction if it does not.

## Objections, one per record

### first-1
Problem: The count is right, but the last sentence states a reading as a fact and ties the bow the brief names ("The poem is about things that will not finish").
Corrected: Read the first seven lines aloud. Five of them end on a participle, breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding, so that each line runs on into the next.
Source: Lines 1-7 (wl_numbered.txt).

### first-3
Problem: "Between two lines of Wagner" is wrong: the first Tristan quotation is four lines (31-34), the second one line (42). "The one moment of feeling in Part I" is a reading stated as fact (Marie on the sled is feeling too).
Corrected: Between two quotations from Wagner's <i>Tristan und Isolde</i> (lines 31-34 and 42), someone remembers the hyacinth garden and being unable to speak. The passage ends on "the heart of light, the silence."
Source: Lines 31-42; Eliot's notes at 31 and 42.

### first-4
Problem: "Everything she deals comes true" overstates the note. Eliot's note at 46 says the Phoenician Sailor, the Merchant, the "crowds of people" and Death by Water return; Belladonna and the Wheel are not accounted for, and the Man with Three Staves only "quite arbitrarily".
Corrected: Madame Sosostris, a fortune-teller with a cold. Eliot's note at line 46 says that several of her cards return later in the poem: the Phoenician Sailor, the Merchant, the crowds of people, death by water. The Hanged Man is the card she cannot find.
Source: Eliot's note at 46.

### first-5
Problem: "walking over it to work" is an inference (the poem gives a winter dawn and the stroke of nine, not an office); the ruling on "route to work" should keep the commute out of this note too. "A clock" is loose: St Mary Woolnoth "kept the hours" with a bell. "The poem's trick, learned from Baudelaire" is the metaphor-for-explanation the brief names.
Corrected: London itself: London Bridge, King William Street, and the bell of St Mary Woolnoth at nine on a winter morning. Eliot's notes send the crowd to Dante's <i>Inferno</i> and "Unreal City" to Baudelaire's Paris, so that a London crowd is also the dead.
Source: Lines 60-68; Eliot's notes at 60, 63, 64, 68.

### first-6
Problem: "The section's title, A Game of Chess, is about moves made without speaking" is a reading stated as fact, and not the title's source. Eliot's note at 138 gives the game of chess in Middleton's <i>Women Beware Women</i>, where the game keeps a mother occupied while a seduction goes on upstairs. Whether the man's unquoted lines are spoken or thought is itself a reading, and should be worded as one.
Corrected: A conversation in which only one voice is heard: her lines are in quotation marks, his are not, and his are usually read as unspoken. The title comes from Middleton's <i>Women Beware Women</i>, cited in Eliot's note at 138, where a game of chess keeps a mother occupied while her daughter-in-law is seduced upstairs.
Source: Eliot's note at 138; Middleton, Women Beware Women, II. ii.

### first-7
Problem: "The publican's shout is also the poem's clock running out" is a metaphor describing the poem rather than explaining it. "Its most overheard" is a flourish.
Corrected: A pub at closing time, in the plainest speech the poem has. The line in capitals is the landlord's call at closing time.
Source: Lines 139-172.

### first-9
Problem: "The lines shorten to two words" is wrong; line 271 is one word. "The river sings" passes over what Eliot's note says: the song is the Thames-daughters', after Wagner's Rhine-daughters.
Corrected: The Song of the Thames-daughters begins here, Eliot's note says, on the model of Wagner's Rhine-daughters. The lines shorten to a word or two. Read them for their rhythm first.
Source: Eliot's note at 266; lines 266-291.

### first-11
Problem: This note is the pattern Ethan quoted from the Water lens ("twenty-nine lines of rock before three words of rain"), and "Let it be as dry as it is" is a flourish. The counts are right; the manner has to go. "The rain, when it comes" also decides a question the poem leaves open: after "Bringing rain" the Ganges is still "waiting for rain" (396-397).
Corrected: From here to line 359 there is rock and no water. Rain is not named again until line 395, "Bringing rain", and even then the Ganges in the next line is still waiting for it.
Source: Lines 331-359, 394-397.

### first-12
Problem: "A stack of quotations" is not quite right: line 431, "These fragments I have shored against my ruins", is Eliot's own. "Read them as a noise" is a flourish.
Corrected: Of the last eight lines, all but line 431 quote or adapt another text, in English, Italian, Latin, French and Sanskrit. Read them through once before looking anything up; Eliot's notes give the sources, and the Fragments view sets the lines out one by one.
Source: Lines 427-434; Eliot's notes at 427-433.

### water-1
Problem: "The first water is unwelcome" is a reading stated as fact.
Corrected: "Dull roots with spring rain." The poem's first water, in the month it calls the cruellest.
Source: Lines 1-4.

### water-5
Problem: "The reverse of what the Grail legend advises" is a reading stated as fact, and unclear as it stands (the Grail legend advises nothing about drowning). The point, that in Weston and Frazer death by water belongs to rites of renewal, should be attributed and worded as a reading. "The reading's one instruction" is ambiguous on a reading pathway.
Corrected: "Fear death by water." The one instruction in Madame Sosostris's reading. In Weston and Frazer, the books Eliot's headnote names, drowning belongs to rites of renewal, and the warning has been read against that.
Source: Eliot's headnote and note at 46; Weston, From Ritual to Romance; Frazer, Adonis, Attis, Osiris.

### water-7
Problem: "Her next scene is the brook" is inaccurate: Ophelia has no next scene; her drowning is reported by Gertrude two scenes later (IV. vii). "Ophelia leaves the pub" is a knowing conflation.
Corrected: Ophelia's last words on stage, from <i>Hamlet</i> IV. v, given to the pub at closing time. Two scenes later the Queen reports her drowning in the brook.
Source: Hamlet IV. v and IV. vii.

### water-8
Problem: "A wedding river with the wedding removed" is a reading in the aphoristic form the brief names, and the note's source is not given.
Corrected: "Sweet Thames, run softly." The refrain of Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596), a poem for a double betrothal on the Thames, which Eliot's note cites. Here the river bears no "testimony of summer nights."
Source: Eliot's note at 176; Spenser, Prothalamion.

### water-9
Problem: "Where the poem was written" overstates: Parts I and II were typed in London before Eliot went to Lausanne, Part III was worked on in London and at Margate; Parts IV and V were drafted at Lausanne. Leman is the lake, and the psalm is not named.
Corrected: "By the waters of Leman I sat down and wept." Psalm 137, "By the rivers of Babylon", with Lac Léman, Lake Geneva, in place of Babylon. Eliot was at Lausanne, on the lake, in November and December 1921, and drafted Parts IV and V there.
Source: Psalm 137:1; the ruling on Lausanne; Rainey, Revisiting The Waste Land (Parts IV and V composed between 21-22 November 1921 and 2 January 1922).

### water-10
Problem: The poem says "gashouse", not "gasworks". "The Fisher King by the gasworks" states a reading as fact; Eliot's notes name the Fisher King at 46 and 424, not here.
Corrected: "fishing in the dull canal ... round behind the gashouse." The fisherman has been read as the Fisher King of Weston's book, whom Eliot's notes name at lines 46 and 424. The two lines that follow are Ferdinand's, from <i>The Tempest</i> (note at 192).
Source: Lines 189-192; Eliot's notes at 46, 192, 424.

### water-12
Problem: Three fragments stacked ("Phlebas. The current under sea. The tarot fulfilled."), which the brief names; the substance is right.
Corrected: Phlebas the Phoenician, drowned: "A current under sea / Picked his bones in whispers." Eliot's note at line 46 says that the "Death by Water" of Madame Sosostris's warning "is executed in Part IV."
Source: Lines 312-316; Eliot's note at 46.

### water-14
Problem: "A hymn to a sound that is not there" is a metaphor in place of explanation, and "a bird that imitates water" is a reading; what Eliot's note says is that the hermit-thrush has a "water-dripping song".
Corrected: "If there were the sound of water only." The lines imagine the sound they lack and end with the hermit-thrush, whose "water-dripping song" Eliot's note on line 357 describes, and with "Drip drop drip drop" (358). Then: "But there is no water."
Source: Lines 353-359; Eliot's note at 357.

### water-15
Problem: "The only rain that falls" is false: "Summer surprised us ... With a shower of rain" (8-9). "Two words" is the counting flourish. And whether rain falls at 395 at all is open, since the next lines have the leaves still waiting for it.
Corrected: "Bringing rain." The first rain since the shower over the Starnbergersee at line 9, and the only rain in Part V. The next lines leave the Ganges still waiting for it.
Source: Lines 8-9, 394-397.

### water-16
Problem: "The monsoon" is not in the poem; the lines give a sunken river, limp leaves waiting for rain, and black clouds over Himavant. Minor.
Corrected: "Ganga was sunken." The Ganges, low, and the black clouds gathering over Himavant, the Himalaya. The thunder that speaks next is from the Upanishad named in Eliot's note.
Source: Lines 396-401; Eliot's note on the thunder (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, 5, 1).

### water-17
Problem: "Water at last as something that answers a hand" is a metaphor describing the poem; the note does not say what the passage is.
Corrected: "The boat responded / Gaily, to the hand expert with sail and oar." The third of the thunder's words, <i>Damyata</i>, "control" in Eliot's note, and the poem's one calm sea.
Source: Lines 419-423; Eliot's note on the thunder's words.

### water-18
Problem: The paired antithesis ("The last water and the last drought, in one sentence") does the brief's forbidden work, and the note omits the one source a reader would want here: Eliot's note sends this line to Weston on the Fisher King. Minor.
Corrected: "I sat upon the shore / Fishing, with the arid plain behind me." Eliot's note sends the reader to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King. The shore and the arid plain are the poem's last water and its last drought.
Source: Eliot's note at 424 (his numbering 424); lines 424-426.

### dead-2
Problem: "The dead land is the first thing named after April" is wrong: "month", "breeding" and "Lilacs" come first.
Corrected: "Lilacs out of the dead land." The land is dead in the poem's second line. The title of Part I is the Prayer Book's: The Order for the Burial of the Dead.
Source: Lines 1-2; Book of Common Prayer.

### dead-3
Problem: "Ezekiel's valley" (the dry bones of Ezekiel 37) is not what Eliot's note cites; the note at 20 gives Ezekiel 2:1, "Son of man". "Ecclesiastes' long home" is right but unexplained.
Corrected: "the dead tree gives no shelter." Eliot's notes send line 20 to Ezekiel 2:1, "Son of man, stand upon thy feet", and line 23 to Ecclesiastes 12:5, where "the grasshopper shall be a burden" and "man goeth to his long home."
Source: Eliot's notes at 20 and 23; Ezekiel 2:1; Ecclesiastes 12:5 (AV).

### dead-6
Problem: "Frazer's buried god, in a suburb" is a reading stated as fact (ruled on in the other batches). Frazer is in Eliot's headnote; the suburb is not in the poem.
Corrected: "That corpse you planted last year in your garden." Eliot's headnote names Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i> and its "vegetation ceremonies", and the corpse that may sprout has been read as one of Frazer's buried gods, set in a back garden.
Source: Eliot's headnote; lines 71-73.

### dead-8
Problem: "The trenches" is a reading stated as fact (ruled on). Eliot's own note sends the line elsewhere, to the rat at 195.
Corrected: "rats' alley / Where the dead men lost their bones." Eliot's note points to the rat on the river bank at line 195. The lines have also been read as the trenches of the war that had ended three years before.
Source: Eliot's note at 115; line 195.

### dead-14
Problem: "What the dead leave: memories, wills, empty rooms" is the colon-and-list the brief names, and "wills" is a gloss on "seals broken by the lean solicitor". The Webster source of the spider is not given.
Corrected: "our obituaries." Lines 407-410 list what a life leaves behind, obituaries, memories, seals for a solicitor to break, empty rooms, and set them against the "moment's surrender" of line 404. The spider of line 408 comes from Webster's <i>White Devil</i>, cited in Eliot's note.
Source: Lines 404-410; Eliot's note on the spider (his 407).

### women-4
Problem: "Afraid of the police" is a reading (of "One must be so careful these days") stated as fact, and "right about everything" overstates Eliot's note, as in first-4.
Corrected: Madame Sosostris, "famous clairvoyante", with a bad cold. Her "One must be so careful these days" has been read as a fortune-teller's wariness of prosecution, and Eliot's note at line 46 says that several of her cards return later in the poem.
Source: Lines 43-59; Eliot's note at 46; prosecutions of fortune-tellers under the Vagrancy Act 1824 (National Archives).

### women-5
Problem: "A card, a poison, a painting" is the triad-for-rhythm; "a painting" (Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>) is a reading, not in Eliot's note, and the note says the card is Eliot's own.
Corrected: Belladonna, "the Lady of the Rocks", a card of Eliot's own making; his note says he departed from the Tarot pack. The name is Italian for "beautiful lady" and the name of a poison, and her title has been read as an echo of Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>.
Source: Eliot's note at 46; lines 49-50.

### women-6
Problem: "Eve's garden" is not what the note gives (Paradise Lost iv. 140 is Satan's first sight of Eden), and "made into furniture" is a flourish. The three sources are Eliot's own notes and should be attributed to them.
Corrected: The woman in the chair. Eliot's notes trace the room to Cleopatra's barge in <i>Antony and Cleopatra</i>, to the "laquearia" of Dido's hall in the <i>Aeneid</i>, and to the "sylvan scene" of Eden in <i>Paradise Lost</i>.
Source: Eliot's notes at 77, 92, 98.

### women-7
Problem: The quotation marks are misplaced: the poem has "Jug Jug" in quotation marks and "to dirty ears" outside them. Ovid is not named.
Corrected: Philomel, raped by her sister's husband Tereus and turned into a nightingale (Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> VI, in Eliot's note), whose song comes as "Jug Jug" to dirty ears.
Source: Lines 99-103; Eliot's note at 99.

### women-9
Problem: "No teeth" is not established: Albert tells her to "have them all out, Lil, and get a nice set", so she has teeth, bad ones. "Who tells her the truth" is a reading. The ruling on Vivien's lines belongs here.
Corrected: Lil, "only thirty-one", with five children and the teeth Albert wants her to have out, and the friend who tells the story. Two of the friend's lines, 153 and 164, were written by Vivien Eliot.
Source: Lines 139-172; the ruling on Vivien Eliot's lines.

### women-11
Problem: "A soldiers' song" is right but goes beyond Eliot's note, which says only that the ballad "was reported to me from Sydney, Australia"; the note should be quoted and the identification marked as one.
Corrected: Mrs Porter and her daughter, from a ballad which, Eliot's note says, "was reported to me from Sydney, Australia"; it has been identified as a song of the Australian troops in the First World War.
Source: Eliot's note at 199; Southam, Student's Guide; Great War Fiction, "Mrs Porter".

### women-15
Problem: Two readings stated as facts. "The mothers of Europe" comes from Eliot's note quoting Hesse on eastern Europe; "the women at the cross" is Luke 23:27 and is not in the notes.
Corrected: "Murmur of maternal lamentation." Eliot's note on this passage quotes Hesse's <i>Blick ins Chaos</i> on half of Europe on the road to chaos. The lamenting mothers have also been read with the women who "bewailed and lamented" Christ on the way to the cross (Luke 23:27).
Source: Eliot's note on lines 366-76 (his numbering); Luke 23:27 (AV).

### cities-1
Problem: "August 1911" is too narrow: Eliot's own account is "the summer of 1911", and he went to Munich in July. The Starnbergersee is not in Munich; the poem's spelling is "Starnbergersee".
Corrected: The Starnbergersee, a lake south-west of Munich, and the Hofgarten, in the city, where Eliot spent part of the summer of 1911.
Source: Eliot on Prufrock ("most of it was written in the summer of 1911 when I was in Munich"); Peter Cash, English Association bookmark on Prufrock ("In July he left for a holiday in Munich and Northern Italy").

### cities-4
Problem: "Eliot's route to work" has been dropped everywhere by ruling.
Corrected: King William Street runs north from London Bridge; St Mary Woolnoth stands at its far end, on the corner of Lombard Street. Eliot's note on the "dead sound on the final stroke of nine" reads, in full, "A phenomenon which I have often noticed."
Source: Eliot's note at 68; the ruling.

### cities-6
Problem: Leman is the lake, not Lausanne, and "where the poem was finished" is loose (Pound's cuts came in Paris and London in January 1922).
Corrected: Leman is Lac Léman, Lake Geneva. Eliot was at Lausanne, on its shore, in November and December 1921, and drafted Parts IV and V there.
Source: The ruling on Lausanne; Rainey, Revisiting The Waste Land.

### cities-7
Problem: True but misleading on its own: the poem was written before the fire. A reader takes "Smyrna, which burned in September 1922" as something the poem knows.
Corrected: Smyrna, now Izmir, the Aegean port from which Mr Eugenides brings his currants. The city burned in September 1922, after the poem was written and shortly before it was first printed, in October.
Source: Wikipedia, Burning of Smyrna (13-22 September 1922); the Criterion, October 1922.

### cities-8
Problem: "Brighton" is not in the poem. The Metropole has generally been taken as the Brighton hotel (Southam), but London had a Metropole too, so it is an identification and should be worded as one.
Corrected: The Cannon Street Hotel, beside the City station. The Metropole has generally been taken as the Hotel Metropole at Brighton.
Source: Southam, Student's Guide (1971), p. 83, on "a weekend in Brighton".

### cities-15
Problem: "The poem's last places are a river and a mountain" is false: London Bridge (427) and Aquitaine (430) come after.
Corrected: Ganga is the Ganges and Himavant the Himalaya, the poem's only Indian landscape. London Bridge and Aquitaine still follow, at lines 427 and 430.
Source: Lines 396-398, 427, 430.

### birds-1
Problem: "The first singer" is a reading (a cricket chirps), and Ecclesiastes is cited without saying what it says.
Corrected: "the cricket no relief." Not a bird. Eliot's note sends the line to Ecclesiastes 12:5, where "the grasshopper shall be a burden."
Source: Eliot's note at 23; Ecclesiastes 12:5 (AV).

### birds-7
Problem: "Included for the wings" is the wink the brief names.
Corrected: "bats with baby faces." Not birds, though they whistle and "beat their wings" (381).
Source: Lines 380-381.

### birds-8
Problem: "Peter's cock, Hamlet's" are readings stated as facts (ruled on); neither is in Eliot's notes. "The French farmyard" should say what it means: the crow is spelt as the French spell it.
Corrected: "Only a cock stood on the rooftree / Co co rico co co rico." The crow is spelt as the French spell it. The cock has been read with the one that crowed at Peter's denial, and with the one whose crowing sends the Ghost away in <i>Hamlet</i>.
Source: Lines 392-393; Matthew 26:74-75; Hamlet I. i.

### nothing-3
Problem: The quotation runs the break between lines 121 and 122 together: the poem has "Do" at the end of 121 and "You know nothing?" on 122.
Corrected: "Do / You know nothing? Do you see nothing? Do you remember / Nothing?"
Source: Lines 121-123.

### nothing-7
Problem: "The question that answers the word" is a riddle, not a note.
Corrected: "<i>Datta:</i> what have we given?" The thunder's first word, "give" in Eliot's note, and the question the poem puts to it.
Source: Lines 401-402; Eliot's note on the thunder's words.

### eyes-11
Problem: "Eyes that do not know him" is an unexplained allusion to Luke 24:16, stated as fact; the note gives neither Eliot's Antarctic source nor the Emmaus headnote.
Corrected: "Who is the third who walks always beside you?" Eliot's note traces the lines to an Antarctic expedition whose exhausted party counted one member more than there was. His headnote to Part V names the journey to Emmaus, where the disciples' "eyes were holden that they should not know him" (Luke 24:16).
Source: Eliot's Part V headnote and note at 360 (his numbering); Luke 24:16 (AV).

### eliot-1
Problem: "August 1911" (see cities-1); "the lake where a king drowned" should be exact, since Ludwig II's drowning is the official finding and still contested; and the colon-list form.
Corrected: Munich in the summer of 1911: the Hofgarten and its arcades, and the Starnbergersee, where King Ludwig II of Bavaria was found drowned in 1886.
Source: Eliot on the summer of 1911 in Munich; Wikipedia, Ludwig II of Bavaria (found dead in Lake Starnberg, 13 June 1886).

### eliot-2
Problem: Two biographical identifications presented as the only choice ("Emily Hale ... or Jean Verdenal"), stated as fact. Both are readings, and the lilac memory is Eliot's 1934 Criterion text, to be quoted in a phrase.
Corrected: The hyacinth garden has been read biographically, as Emily Hale, whom Eliot knew in Boston before he left for Europe in 1914, or as Jean Verdenal, his friend of the Paris year 1910-11, whom he remembered in 1934 crossing the Luxembourg Gardens "waving a branch of lilac."
Source: Eliot, Criterion commentary, April 1934; tseliot.com, Eliot-Hale letters chronology (the 1914 declaration); Lyndall Gordon for the Hale reading.

### eliot-3
Problem: "Lombard Street" for the bank is not stated by ruling; "The clock struck nine as he arrived" is not established by ruling. What is established is his employment and his own note.
Corrected: Eliot worked at Lloyds Bank in the City from March 1917 to the autumn of 1925, a short walk from St Mary Woolnoth, which stands where King William Street meets Lombard Street. His note on the "dead sound on the final stroke of nine" is "A phenomenon which I have often noticed."
Source: tseliot.com, "T. S. Eliot at Lloyds Bank" (19 March 1917; autumn 1925); Eliot's note at 68; the rulings.

### eliot-4
Problem: "Vivien's nerves and his" applies the scene to the marriage as a fact; it is a reading. The flat's date is missing.
Corrected: The Eliots' flat from the autumn of 1920 was 9 Clarence Gate Gardens, near Regent's Park. The scene has often been read against their marriage and the nervous illness of both.
Source: clarencegategardens.org.uk, History (moved October 1920).

### eliot-5
Problem: A fact without its source, and the ruling on Vivien's lines belongs here.
Corrected: The story was told to the Eliots by their maid, Ellen Kellond, according to Valerie Eliot's note in the 1971 facsimile of the drafts. Vivien Eliot wrote two of its lines, 153 and 164.
Source: Valerie Eliot (ed.), The Waste Land: A Facsimile and Transcript (1971), note to the pub scene; the ruling on Vivien Eliot's lines. See Still unsettled.

### eliot-6
Problem: Substance right (Rainey confirms Parts IV and V were drafted at Lausanne); the form is the stacked list ("Dr Vittoz, the lake, Parts IV and V").
Corrected: Lausanne, November and December 1921. Eliot was there under Dr Roger Vittoz, on the lake the poem calls Leman, and drafted Parts IV and V.
Source: The ruling on Lausanne; Rainey, Revisiting The Waste Land.

### eliot-7
Problem: "October 1921" is too short (he arrived in the middle of October and was still there on 4 November); the ruling's "some fifty lines" and the letter to Schiff are the facts to give.
Corrected: Margate, from the middle of October 1921, where Eliot wrote in a shelter on the seafront, the Nayland Rock shelter. On 4 November he told Sydney Schiff he had written "some fifty lines" of Part III.
Source: The ruling on Margate.

### eliot-8
Problem: "The drowned sailor's original voyage, cut by Pound" is a colon-fragment that does not say what happened; the draft's shape and the Gloucester summers are the facts.
Corrected: Gloucester, Massachusetts, where the Eliots had their summer house. The draft of Part IV opened with some eighty lines on a fishing voyage out of Gloucester that ends in shipwreck; Pound cut them, and the ten lines on Phlebas remain.
Source: The 93-line draft of Part IV (Poetry Archive; Auchincloss, NYRB 1984); tseliot.com, "T. S. Eliot's Gloucester" (the house of 1896).

### eliot-9
Problem: A reading stated as fact.
Corrected: The boat has been read against Eliot's boyhood summers at Gloucester, on Cape Ann, where he learned to sail.
Source: tseliot.com, "T. S. Eliot's Gloucester".

### eliot-10
Problem: "il miglior fabbro" was not in the 1922 text the site prints: Eliot wrote it by hand in Pound's copy and it was first printed in 1925. "The pencil" is a flourish (Pound wrote in pencil and ink). The Paris dates and the letter of 24 January are the facts.
Corrected: Paris, 2 to 16 January 1922, on the way home from Lausanne. At Pound's flat the drafts were cut to nearly their final shape; on 24 January Pound wrote "Complimenti, you bitch." The dedication "For Ezra Pound, il miglior fabbro", Dante's words for Arnaut Daniel in <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, was written by Eliot in Pound's copy of the 1922 edition and first printed in 1925.
Source: Rainey, Revisiting The Waste Land (2-16 January 1922); Wikipedia, The Waste Land, and the Poetry Foundation exhibit (dedication added 1925, handwritten in the 1922 copy); the ruling on Pound's letter; Purgatorio xxvi. 117.
