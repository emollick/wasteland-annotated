## Changes

### first
Problem: "Each stop is a place to stand, not an explanation" is the paired antithesis and the tour-guide framing the brief names; "Read the lines, read the note, move on" is fragments stacked for effect.
Proposed:
title: A first reading
intro: Twelve passages for a reader meeting the poem for the first time, in the order the poem gives them. At each one, read the lines first, then the note, then go on to the next.

### water
Problem: "The poem's hydrology" is on the brief's list of tells; "Follow the water and you follow the argument" and the three-part colon list are the voice; "the two are never told apart" is a reading passed off as fact.
Proposed:
title: Water
intro: Water in the poem, from the "spring rain" of line 4 to the rain of line 395: the shower over the Starnbergersee, the drowned sailor in Madame Sosostris's pack and her warning "Fear death by water" (line 55), the Thames of Part III, the drowning of Phlebas in Part IV, and the rock without water that opens Part V. Eliot's headnote credits Jessie L. Weston's From Ritual to Romance, whose third chapter is "The Freeing of the Waters".
Source: Eliot's headnote to the notes (1922); Weston, From Ritual to Romance (1920), ch. III.

### dead
Problem: "the crowd that death has undone" is a rhythmic list item, and "the dead never quite stay in it" is the closing flourish. The Prayer Book fact is right.
Proposed:
title: The dead
intro: Burials, drownings and bones, from the corpse planted in the garden (line 71) and the crowd on London Bridge (63) to Phlebas in Part IV and the "Dry bones" of line 391. "The Burial of the Dead", the title of Part I, is the name of the funeral service in the Book of Common Prayer.

### women
Problem: "so that you can test the claim" is fine in substance but the tally that follows ("Most of them speak; several are quotations; two are queens; one is a bird") is the counting flourish, and "two are queens" is wrong in any case: Cleopatra, Dido and Elizabeth are all in the poem.
Proposed:
title: The women
intro: Eliot's note to line 218 says that "all the women are one woman". This pathway gives the poem's women in the order they appear, those who speak and those who are only named or alluded to, so that the reader can weigh the claim.

### cities
Problem: "five capitals falling in two lines" counts to sound clever and is not true (Alexandria is not a capital); "ends on the Ganges" is not true either, since London Bridge returns at line 427.
Proposed:
title: Cities
intro: The places the poem names, in the poem's order, from the Starnbergersee and the Hofgarten in Munich to the Ganges and Himavant in Part V. London has the most stops: the bridge, the streets and churches of the City, the pubs, and the river from Richmond to Greenwich. Lines 375–376 name five cities in two lines: Jerusalem, Athens, Alexandria, Vienna, London.

### birds
Problem: "Every bird in the poem is a musician, and every song is a complaint" is the aphoristic opening, and it is false: the cock's "Co co rico" and the hermit-thrush's water-dripping song are not complaints.
Proposed:
title: Birds
intro: The birds in the poem: the nightingale into which Philomel was changed (lines 99–103 and 203–206), the gulls Phlebas forgot (313), the hermit-thrush of line 357, whose species Eliot's note gives and which he says he heard in Quebec County, the cock on the rooftree (392), and the swallow of line 429. Recordings, where they exist, are in the listening room.

### nothing
Problem: The list of speakers is rhythmic, and its last item is wrong: the "humble people" on Margate Sands do not say the word; the third Thames-daughter says they "expect / Nothing".
Proposed:
title: Nothing
intro: The word "nothing" wherever the poem uses it: at line 40, in the hyacinth garden; at lines 120–126, in the exchange between the woman and the man in Part II; and at lines 302 and 305, in the third Thames-daughter's speech on Margate Sands.

### eyes
Problem: Stacked fragments, then the epigram "The poem's people cannot see, or see too much." The Emmaus item is right but unexplained.
Proposed:
title: Eyes
intro: Eyes in the poem: the eyes that fail in the hyacinth garden (line 39), the pearls that were the drowned man's eyes (48, 125), the eyes each man fixes before his feet on London Bridge (65), the one-eyed merchant (52), the "lidless eyes" of line 138, and the blind Tiresias (218). Eliot's note on Part V names the journey to Emmaus, where, in Luke 24, the disciples' eyes "were holden" so that they did not know who walked with them.

### eliot
Problem: "Not a key to the poem, but a map of where he was standing" is quoted in the brief as the thing to remove.
Proposed:
title: The poem in Eliot's life
intro: Places and dates from Eliot's life in 1921 and 1922 that the text touches, such as Margate Sands (line 300), the waters of Leman (182), and the City streets between the station and the bank. Each stop gives the line, what is known, and where it is known from: a letter, the drafts, or one of Eliot's notes.

### title-draft
Problem: "The Dickens title survives in the notes only as a habit of mind: the poem reads the world's news aloud in every voice it can do" is the closing flourish, and it is wrong: Dickens is not in the notes at all. Malory's Dolorous Stroke is a critics' link, not Eliot's, and is stated as if it were behind the title. I could not confirm that the heading was crossed out (see Unsettled), so the title should not depend on it.
Proposed:
title: The earlier title
body:
The typescripts of Parts I and II carry the heading "He Do the Police in Different Voices", with "Part I" and "Part II" beneath it. The words are Betty Higden's in Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (Book I, chapter 16), praising the foundling Sloppy: "You mightn't think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices." The title page Eliot typed for the finished poem reads "The Waste Land", and his headnote to the notes says that the title, the plan and much of the incidental symbolism came from Jessie L. Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>. Dickens is not mentioned in the notes.
Source: The Waste Land: A Facsimile and Transcript (1971), the typescripts of Parts I and II and the title page; Our Mutual Friend, I.xvi; Eliot's headnote (1922).

### epigraph-draft
Problem: The last sentence ties the bow, and it is inaccurate: the Sibyl is not an immortal. She was granted as many years as grains of sand and forgot to ask for youth (Ovid, Metamorphoses XIV). The record also leaves out that Pound withdrew his objection, which is the point of the exchange: Eliot changed the epigraph anyway.
Proposed:
title: The epigraph that was replaced
body:
The title page of the typescript carries, under "The Waste Land", a passage from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i> ending with Kurtz's "The horror! the horror!" Pound wrote, on 24 January 1922, that he doubted Conrad was "weighty enough to stand the citation"; Eliot answered that it was "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative"; Pound then told him to do as he liked about Conrad. Eliot nevertheless replaced it with the sentence from Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i> (chapter 48) in which Trimalchio says that he saw the Sibyl at Cumae hanging in a jar, and that when the boys asked her what she wanted she answered, "I want to die." The Sibyl had been granted long life, not youth.
Source: Facsimile (1971), title page; Pound to Eliot, 24 January 1922, and Eliot's reply, Letters vol. 1; Petronius, Satyricon 48; Ovid, Metamorphoses XIV.130–153.

### poem
Problem: "It is never quite a person" is the epigram at the end. The rest is right but leaves out where the voice is.
Proposed:
label: The poem's own voice
note: The narrating voice: the opening lines, the description of the room at the start of Part II, the introduction of Madame Sosostris (43–46), Part IV, and the replies to the thunder in Part V (402–409, 412–417, 419–423).

### marie
Problem: "A displaced Austrian aristocrat, talking over coffee" is a reading stated as fact and gets the known fact sideways. What is known is that Eliot had met Countess Marie Larisch and that the sledding story came from her (Valerie Eliot's note in the facsimile).
Proposed:
label: Marie
note: Lines 8–18, read here as one speaker, named at lines 15–16. Valerie Eliot's edition of the drafts records that Eliot had met Countess Marie Larisch, niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria, and that the sledding story was hers. Line 12, in German, is given a voice of its own below.
Source: The Waste Land: A Facsimile and Transcript (1971), note to line 8.

### stranger
Problem: There is no café in the poem (they drink coffee in the Hofgarten), and that line 12 belongs to someone other than Marie is a reading; the note should say so.
Proposed:
label: A voice in German
note: Line 12, one sentence: "I am not Russian at all, I come from Lithuania, a real German." The poem does not say who speaks it; giving it to a second speaker is a reading.

### prophet
Problem: Eliot's notes cite Ezekiel (line 20) and Ecclesiastes (line 23), not Isaiah; Isaiah 32:2 is a critics' link for the rock. And the voice is not "under the red rock"; it invites the son of man to come in under it.
Proposed:
label: The prophet
note: Lines 19–30: a voice speaking as the prophets do, to the "son of man" (Eliot's note: Ezekiel 2:1) and in the words of Ecclesiastes (his note to line 23). "The shadow of this red rock" has been linked to Isaiah 32:2.
Source: Eliot's notes to lines 20 and 23.

### lover
Problem: "the poem's one admission of feeling" is a reading stated as fact and a flourish; lines 403–404 ("blood shaking my heart") are an admission of feeling too.
Proposed:
label: The one who could not speak

### shepherd
Problem: "Oed' und leer das Meer" is five words, not four. The count adds nothing; what the reader needs is who says it and what it means.
Proposed:
label: Wagner's shepherd
note: Line 42: the shepherd's report in Tristan, Act III (Eliot's note: "III, verse 24"), looking out for Isolde's ship: the sea is desolate and empty.

### walker
Problem: That the "I" of lines 60–76, 207–211 and 257–265 is one person is a reading; the note should say so. "Walks from the Strand to Lower Thames Street" is also loose: it is the music that creeps along the Strand (257–258).
Proposed:
label: The walker in the City
note: The "I" of three passages, read here as one voice: the crowd on London Bridge and the meeting with Stetson (60–76), Mr. Eugenides (207–211), and the music along the Strand and the bar in Lower Thames Street (257–265).

### she

Proposed:
label: The woman with bad nerves

### he

His replies are in other people's words: Webster (118, Eliot's note), Ariel's song from The Tempest (125), "That Shakespearian Rag" (128–130), Middleton (137, Eliot's note).

## Unsettled or for the reader

- Margate dates. The site's "15 October to 12 November" against "three weeks" in every secondary account I reached. My recollection of Hollis and the Letters is that the Eliots went down on Saturday 22 October and that Eliot came back on 12 November, which is three weeks; but I could not verify it online, so I have taken the arrival date out rather than change it. Check Letters vol. 1 (the first letter headed Albemarle Hotel) before restoring either date.
- Lloyds Bank's address. Eliot's own bank letters are headed 17 Cornhill, which is where the Colonial and Foreign Department was; the head office was 71 Lombard Street, the other side of the same block. "Lombard Street" is not wrong but "17 Cornhill" is what Eliot wrote. I have passed the record; the editor may prefer "at 17 Cornhill" with Letters vol. 1 as the source.
- "Cutting it roughly in half" (1922, January). The surviving drafts of the five parts run to something under 700 lines against 434; "half" is true only if the poems Eliot thought of adding (The Death of the Duchess, Exequy, Dirge, Elegy, Song, Saint Narcissus) are counted. Count from the facsimile transcript before keeping "half".
- "The title that was crossed out". I could not see the facsimile page. If the heading on the Part I or Part II typescript is in fact struck through, the old title may stand; if what is struck through is only the first page of Part I (Eliot's light pencil line through the night-town opening), it may not.
- Malory. Ricks and McCue and Southam link "waste land" to Malory's Dolorous Stroke. If the editor wants it back in title-draft, it should read "has been linked to Malory", not "behind it".
- Woolf's diary. "He sang it & chanted it rhythmed it" with or without a comma after "chanted it": check the Diary, vol. 2, 23 June 1922.
- Pound's inscription. My memory is that the inscription in Pound's copy reads "miglior fabbro" without "il"; the proposed 1923 wording is true either way, but if the editor quotes the inscription, check Ricks and McCue.
- song (passes). If the editor wants a source for "soldiers' song": Eliot's note says only that the ballad "was reported to me from Sydney, Australia"; that it was sung by Australian troops in 1915 is Southam's and Ricks and McCue's, and would be "said to have been sung by".
- sosostris (passes). "Lines 46–59, framed by the poem's voice at 43–46" overlaps at 46; the reader may prefer "Lines 43–59; hers from 'Here, said she' in line 46."
- pilgrim (passes). The label "The pilgrim on the road" rests on Eliot's note ("the journey to Emmaus"); the note could say so in four words if the reader wants it.
- Wording only: "Cockney" in the pub note is the commentators' word, not Eliot's; "London pub talk" would do as well.

## The batch as a whole

The facts are in good order: every line number in the Voices lens is right on the site's count, the timeline's dates are right where I could check them, and the two quotations from the Pound–Eliot exchange are exact. The trouble is almost all of the kind Ethan named, and it is concentrated in the nine pathway introductions and the two drafts records, where every piece ends on a flourish or opens with an aphorism, and where three of the flourishes turn out to be false ("five capitals", "two are queens", "every song is a complaint"). In the Voices lens the problem is different and smaller: a dozen notes present a reading as a fact (who speaks line 12, whether the man in Part II speaks aloud, whether the fisher is the Fisher King), and the fix is a clause, not a rewrite. The timeline needs only the Dial award moved to December, the Margate arrival date checked, and attribution for two quotations. I have proposed nothing I could not source.
