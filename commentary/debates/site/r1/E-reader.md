## Changes

### phoenician-sailor
Problem: The last sentence is a twist ("gone into the water instead") that passes the artist's reading off as fact, and "the reading" is not explained (it means Madame Sosostris's reading). "He is drawn inverted on purpose" is the maker explaining a choice rather than telling me what I am looking at. SHOWS passes.
Proposed:
ANSWERS: 47–48, "Is your card, the drowned Phoenician Sailor, / (Those are pearls that were his eyes. Look!)", and Part IV, Death by Water (312–321). The pearls are from Ariel's song in <i>The Tempest</i> (I. ii), which Eliot's note cites. He is drawn head down, in the posture of the tarot's Hanged Man, the card Madame Sosostris cannot find in the pack (54–55).

### belladonna
Problem: "Pater's Mona Lisa, older than the rocks among which she sits" drops a name and half a quotation with nothing to say who Pater is or that it is a quotation; "is Leonardo's" states a reading as fact; and "the plant, the poison, the cosmetic and 'beautiful lady'" is the rhythmic list of four. In SHOWS, "(belladonna drops)" assumes I already know what the drops do.
Proposed:
SHOWS: A woman seated in a grotto of stone, in a black mantle and gown that leave only her face and hands bare; the pupils of her eyes are wide, as belladonna drops make them; her lips are violet; she holds up a sprig of deadly nightshade, leaves, bells and berries. Sea visible through the mouth of the cave.
ANSWERS: 49–50, "Here is Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks, / The lady of situations." "The Lady of the Rocks" recalls Leonardo's painting <i>The Virgin of the Rocks</i>, and Walter Pater's description of the Mona Lisa in <i>The Renaissance</i> (1873): "She is older than the rocks among which she sits." <i>Belladonna</i> is Italian for "beautiful lady"; it is also the name of deadly nightshade, a poison whose extract women once dropped into their eyes to widen the pupils.

### three-staves
Problem: "(so: a wound to lean on, a line to fish with, a rod of office set in the waste ground)" is a colon and a list of three doing the work of a sentence. Pamela Colman Smith is a name dropped with nothing to say who she is. And the note leaves out the one thing Eliot's note actually says, that the link to the Fisher King is "quite arbitrarily" his own. SHOWS passes.
Proposed:
ANSWERS: 51, "Here is the man with three staves". Eliot's note says he associates this card, "quite arbitrarily, with the Fisher King himself", the wounded king of the Grail legend whose land lies waste. The drawing takes its three staves from that: a crutch for the wound, a rod to fish with, and a staff set in the dry ground, which has begun to sprout. The composition follows the Three of Wands as Pamela Colman Smith drew it for the Rider-Waite tarot pack (1909).

### wheel
Problem: Four Latin words with no translation, and "the medieval regno, regnavi…" assumes I know the picture tradition they label. SHOWS passes.
Proposed:
ANSWERS: 51, "and here the Wheel". It is drawn as a ship's wheel because of line 320, "O you who turn the wheel and look to windward". The four figures follow the medieval pictures of Fortune's wheel, where a king is shown at four points of the turn, labelled, going round from the top, <i>regno</i> (I reign), <i>regnavi</i> (I have reigned), <i>sum sine regno</i> (I have no kingdom), <i>regnabo</i> (I shall reign).

### one-eyed-merchant
Problem: "a profile shows one eye, which is the only kind of one-eyed man a printed card can have" is the wink the brief names. "A City wall" reads as a fortified city wall. In ANSWERS, "Eliot's note: the merchant 'melts into'…" is a colon doing a verb's work, and the note never tells me why the card of the one-eyed merchant shows Mr. Eugenides.
Proposed:
SHOWS: Mr. Eugenides in profile, so that only one eye shows. Bowler hat, astrakhan collar, long overcoat, unshaven; a pocket full of currants and a handful held out in invitation. On his back a large roped bundle, its contents hidden. Behind him a wall and a doorway in the City.
ANSWERS: 52, "the one-eyed merchant". Eliot's note on Tiresias (218) speaks of "the one-eyed merchant, seller of currants", who "melts into the Phoenician Sailor". The seller of currants is Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant, "Unshaven, with a pocket full of currants" (209–210), so the card draws him as Eugenides.

### blank

### hanged-man
Problem: "the card shows the place he is missing from" and "Round the head that is not there" are turns for effect. In ANSWERS, Frazer and Emmaus are dropped without a plain phrase, and "which is why" makes the drawing's choices sound like Eliot's.
Proposed:
SHOWS: A dead tree with one long bough for a beam and a rope tied to it, the noose hanging open. The air is dense cross-hatching; where the Hanged Man would hang, head down with one leg crooked, the paper is left bare in his outline, with a gold halo where the head would be. Far off at the bottom, on a road, two walkers and a hooded third. A green shoot on the dead bough.
ANSWERS: 54–55, "I do not find / The Hanged Man." Eliot's note says the card "fits my purpose in two ways": it is "associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer", the sacrificed god of <i>The Golden Bough</i> whose death brings the land back to life, and with "the hooded figure in the passage of the disciples to Emmaus in Part V" (360–366), where in Luke's Gospel the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples. That is why the drawing puts three walkers on the road below and a green shoot on the dead bough.

### crowd-in-a-ring
Problem: "Dante's dead" is dropped with no word of which Dante or why; "and ahead to" is a fragment hung off the line number. SHOWS passes.
Proposed:
ANSWERS: 56, "I see crowds of people, walking round in a ring." The ring looks ahead to the crowd that flows over London Bridge (62–65), which Eliot's notes on 63 and 64 compare to the crowds of the dead in Dante's <i>Inferno</i> (cantos iii and iv).

### back

### sibyl
Problem: Minor. "who asked for life without asking for youth" is a balanced antithesis, the whole note runs on from one colon, and Petronius's book is not named. SHOWS passes.
Proposed:
ANSWERS: The epigraph, from Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i>. The Sibyl of Cumae was granted long life but not lasting youth, and has withered until she hangs in a jar. When the boys ask her, "Sibyl, what do you want?", she answers, "I want to die."

### marie
Problem: "(The Countess Marie Larisch, the archduke's cousin.)" drops a name and a title with nothing to say who she was or which archduke, and puts the identification as a fact rather than a reading. SHOWS passes.
Proposed:
ANSWERS: 13–18, "Marie, / Marie, hold on tight. And down we went. / In the mountains, there you feel free." Marie has been identified with the Countess Marie Larisch (1858–1940), a niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria, whose memoir <i>My Past</i> appeared in 1913; the arch-duke of line 15 was her cousin.

### philomel
Problem: "The fire-opening dark below the shelf" is a stacked fragment I read twice. "Ovid, Metamorphoses vi." is a bare citation with no word of what the story is, which is what a reader turning this card wants.
Proposed:
SHOWS: A picture over an antique mantel, framed "as though a window gave upon the sylvan scene" (98). In it Philomel runs to the left, her arms already turning to wings, her head turned back; behind her the barbarous king, a black figure, crowned, with his sword raised; in the tree above, the nightingale sings. Below the mantelshelf, the dark of the fireplace.
ANSWERS: 97–103, "The change of Philomel, by the barbarous king / So rudely forced; yet there the nightingale / Filled all the desert with inviolable voice". Eliot's note refers to Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> vi: Tereus, king of Thrace, rapes his wife's sister Philomela and cuts out her tongue, and she is turned into a nightingale.

### fisher-king

Proposed:

### tiresias
Problem: "The snakes are from Ovid, in Eliot's note" does not say what the snakes did, which is the only reason they are on the card. SHOWS passes.
Proposed:
ANSWERS: 215–248, "I Tiresias, though blind, throbbing between two lives, / Old man with wrinkled female breasts". ### the-third
Problem: "No accent: the plainest card after the blank." is a builder's note with a flourish on it. "Eliot's note on Shackleton's party" names Shackleton with no word of what the note says. SHOWS passes.
Proposed:
ANSWERS: 360–366, "Who is the third who walks always beside you? / When I count, there are only you and I together". Eliot's note says the lines were prompted by an account of one of the Antarctic expeditions ("I forget which, but I think one of Shackleton's"), in which the exhausted party "had the constant delusion that there was one more member than could actually be counted". ### thunder
Problem: In SHOWS, "three forks of bare lightning out of it: the thunder's three words" is a colon doing a verb's work. In ANSWERS, "DA, DA, DA: the boat is Damyata, the key Dayadhvam" hands me three Sanskrit words with no translation, and Ganga and Himavant are not explained.
Proposed:
SHOWS: One great black cloud filling the top of the card, its underside lobed, and three forks of lightning out of it, one for each of the thunder's three words. Below, the white peaks of Himavant, the jungle crouched in humps, and Ganga sunken to a thread between wide cracked banks with limp leaves hanging at the edge. On the water a small boat responding to its oar and sail; on the near bank a gold key.
ANSWERS: 396–423, "Ganga was sunken, and the limp leaves / Waited for rain, while the black clouds / Gathered far distant, over Himavant". Ganga is the Ganges and Himavant the Himalaya. The thunder then speaks three times, "DA", and each time the speaker hears in it one of three Sanskrit words, <i>Datta</i>, <i>Dayadhvam</i>, <i>Damyata</i>, which Eliot's note translates "give, sympathise, control" and traces to a fable in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad. The key on the bank is for <i>Dayadhvam</i> ("I have heard the key / Turn in the door once", 412–413); the boat is for <i>Damyata</i> ("The boat responded / Gaily, to the hand expert with sail and oar", 419–420).

### swallow
Problem: The Latin is untranslated, the Pervigilium Veneris is a title with nothing to say what it is, "Philomela's sister becomes the swallow" is unexplained (why her sister?), and Tennyson's song is not placed. The range 428–429 seems to take in 428, which by the site's count is the Dante line; see below. SHOWS passes.
Proposed:
ANSWERS: 429, "Quando fiam uti chelidon — O swallow swallow". The Latin means "when shall I be as the swallow?" and is from the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i> ("The Vigil of Venus"), an anonymous Latin poem of late antiquity, whose speaker hears the nightingale sing and asks when he too will find a voice. Eliot's note adds "Cf. Philomela in Parts II and III"; in the old story Philomela's sister Procne became the swallow. "O swallow swallow" echoes a song in Tennyson's <i>The Princess</i>, "O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South".

### tower

Proposed:
ANSWERS: 430, "Le Prince d'Aquitaine à la tour abolie" (the Prince of Aquitaine of the ruined tower), the second line of Gérard de Nerval's sonnet <i>El Desdichado</i> ("The Disinherited", 1854), which Eliot's note cites. The star, the lute and the black sun on the card are from the same sonnet: "ma seule étoile est morte" (my only star is dead) and "mon luth constellé / Porte le soleil noir de la Mélancolie" (my star-strewn lute bears the black sun of Melancholy). The Tower is also a trump card of the real tarot pack, where it is shown struck by lightning.

## Unsettled or for the scholar
- swallow: by the site's count (one ahead of Eliot's from 346), 428 is "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina" and the swallow line is 429 alone, since "Quando fiam uti chelidon — O swallow swallow" is one line in 1922. I have written 429; the scholar should confirm, and likewise the numbers I have added in thunder (Dayadhvam 412–413, Damyata 419–420) and fisher-king (425, "Fishing, with the arid plain behind me"), all converted from Eliot's numbering by adding one.
- marie: the identification with the Countess Marie Larisch, her dates (1858–1940), her aunt the Empress Elisabeth, and My Past (1913) are as I have them; whether to add that Eliot met her (Valerie Eliot's account in the 1971 facsimile) is for the scholar, who has the source. I left that sentence out rather than mark a gap.
- belladonna: the Pater sentence ("She is older than the rocks among which she sits", The Renaissance, 1873) and the cosmetic use of belladonna drops are stated as fact; the scholar should confirm both, and whether "recalls Leonardo's painting" should rather be "has been read as".
- hanged-man: "the sacrificed god of The Golden Bough whose death brings the land back to life" is my gloss of Frazer, and "in Luke's Gospel" (Luke 24) my gloss of Emmaus. Both are what a reader needs; the scholar should check the wording.
- philomel and swallow: Ovid does not say plainly which sister becomes which bird; my "she is turned into a nightingale" follows the poem, and "Philomela's sister Procne became the swallow" follows the usual telling. If the scholar wants it hedged, "in the usual telling" will do.
- swallow: my one-sentence account of the Pervigilium Veneris (the speaker hears the nightingale and asks when he will find a voice) and "late antiquity" for its date want checking, as does the Tennyson placing (The Princess, 1847).
- tower: 1854 is the date of Les Chimères; the sonnet first appeared in 1853. The scholar may prefer one or the other.
- three-staves: "Rider-Waite tarot pack (1909)" and Pamela Colman Smith as its artist; and my paraphrase of the Fisher King ("whose land lies waste") in this note and in fisher-king.
- elizabeth-and-leicester: "Lord Robert Dudley, later Earl of Leicester" and "they talked of marrying" paraphrase the De Quadra letter Eliot quotes from Froude; and the editor may judge this addition, like the one in thames-daughters, more than the card needs.
- one-eyed-merchant: I have not seen the drawing; "a wall and a doorway in the City" keeps the batch's fact and only unpacks the capital C. If the wall is not in the City, "a wall and a doorway behind" is enough.

## The batch as a whole
The SHOWS fields are mostly in good order: they are plain inventories of what is on the card, concrete and in sentences, and most pass without a word changed. The trouble is in ANSWERS, and it is nearly always the same trouble: the note stops at a name or a title (Pater, Frazer, Weston, Shackleton, Nerval, Pamela Colman Smith, the Countess Marie Larisch, the Pervigilium Veneris, El Desdichado) or at four words of Latin, as though the reader had the same shelf as the writer, when one plain phrase would tell them who or what it is. Five cards still carry notes written for whoever built the page (blank, madame-sosostris, bad-nerves, the-third, london-bridge-falling), with file names and a percentage of ink in them. Three end on the wink or the twist the brief names (phoenician-sailor, one-eyed-merchant, hanged-man). Six of the thirteen vignette titles are captions inverted by a comma or a colon into lines of verse; the other seven are the plain form, and show what the six should be.
