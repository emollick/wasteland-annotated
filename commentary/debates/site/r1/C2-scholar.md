## Changes

### spenser-prothalamion
Problem: The first sentence is right. "Eliot keeps the refrain and empties the river" is a metaphor standing in for a fact that can be stated, and "Notice that … before the wedding cheers him up" is tour-guide address with a joke at the end.
Proposed:
note: A "spousal verse," as its title page calls it, for the double wedding of the Earl of Worcester's daughters, Elizabeth and Katherine Somerset. The poet, out of favour at court, walks by the Thames and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans who stand for the brides. Eliot's note to line 176 cites the poem. He keeps the refrain; his nymphs are twice said to have departed (lines 175 and 179). Spenser's poet is also unhappy, walking by the river to ease the "sullein care" of a fruitless wait at court.

### psalm137
Problem: "a psalm about being asked to write a poem when you cannot" is a reading given as fact; "in exile from the bank" and "put his lake in it" are jokes. The facts a reader wants (Lausanne, Lac Léman, the leave from Lloyds, the second sense of "leman") are missing.
Proposed:
note: The psalm of the Jewish exiles in Babylon, asked by their captors for a song. Eliot finished the poem at Lausanne, on Lake Geneva (in French, Lac Léman), in November and December 1921, on three months' leave from Lloyds Bank; he takes the psalm's first line and puts the lake in place of the rivers of Babylon. "Leman" is also an old English word for a lover, and the double sense has often been noticed.

### marvell-coy-mistress
Problem: "The great English argument for going to bed now" is a joke; "gives it a rattle of bones the first time and motor horns the second" is arch where the lines can simply be cited. Eliot has one Marvell note, not two, and the essay can be dated exactly.
Proposed:
note: Marvell's argument to his mistress: there is no time, because the grave is coming. Eliot's note cites the poem. Both times the turn of Marvell's sentence, "But at my back … I hear," is followed by another sound: at line 186 "The rattle of the bones," at 197 "The sound of horns and motors." Eliot's essay "Andrew Marvell," in the Times Literary Supplement of 31 March 1921, while he was at work on the poem, praised in Marvell "a tough reasonableness beneath the slight lyric grace."
Source: TLS, 31 March 1921 (tercentenary essay), reprinted in Selected Essays. Eliot's 1922 notes have a single Marvell note (Wikisource transcription of the 1922 text).

### day-parliament-of-bees
Problem: The lines are spoken by Polypragmus, a "vainglorious reveller," describing shows he means to stage; the note's "spring hunt" reads Day's "in the spring" as the season, which in Day is at least as likely the pool where Diana bathes. Day's own lines go on to the hounds tearing Actaeon, so Ovid need not be brought in as if Day lacked the ending. "In a motor car" is not in the poem; the sound of motors is.
Proposed:
note: Twelve "characters" of good and bad men, told as bees. The lines Eliot's note quotes are spoken by Polypragmus, "a mere vainglorious reveller," describing the shows he means to stage in his hall: a noise of horns and hunting is to bring Actaeon to Diana "in the spring," where all shall see her naked, and Actaeon's hounds are then to tear their master, as in Ovid. Eliot keeps the shape of Day's sentence and puts "horns and motors" for the hunt, Sweeney and Mrs Porter for Actaeon and Diana.
Source: Day, The Parliament of Bees (1641), character of Polypragmus (EEBO text).

### verlaine-parsifal
Problem: The translation of lines 2–4 drops "vers la Chair," and "gorge" here is bosom, not throat. In the note, "a barrack-room song about a brothel" states a reading of the Mrs Porter ballad as fact (Eliot's note says only that it was reported to him from Sydney), "so that the temptation and the temple stand one line apart" is a flourish, and "the thing the poem never manages" is a reading passed off as fact.
Proposed:
trans: Parsifal has overcome the Maidens, their pretty chatter and amusing lust, and his own leaning toward the Flesh, a virgin boy's, which all that tempts to love light breasts and that pretty chatter; he has overcome the beautiful Woman with the subtle heart, spreading her cool arms and her exciting bosom; he has overcome Hell and returns to his tent with a heavy trophy on his boyish arm, with the lance that pierced the supreme Side! He has healed the king, and here he is king himself, and priest of the most holy essential Treasure. In a robe of gold he adores, glory and symbol, the pure vessel where the real Blood shines. And, oh, those children's voices singing in the dome!
note: Verlaine's sonnet on Wagner's last opera, written for the Revue wagnérienne (January 1886), gives the plot in thirteen lines: the flower-maidens, Kundry, the recovered spear, the healed king. The fourteenth line is the boys' choir in the dome of the Grail temple. Eliot's note cites the sonnet. The line follows the Mrs Porter ballad, which his note says was "reported to me from Sydney, Australia." In Wagner, as in the romances Weston discusses, the knight heals the wounded king; the poem's Fisher King is still fishing at line 425, "with the arid plain behind me."

### lyly-campaspe
Problem: "are Lyly's bird" claims a source Eliot did not cite (his notes never mention Lyly; the song is the clearest instance of a convention), and "with the pretty convention turned back into what it describes" is the epigram ending.
Proposed:
note: The Elizabethan convention that the nightingale sings "jug jug" and calls "tereu," the vocative of Tereus, is at its plainest in this song from Lyly's court comedy. The songs in Lyly's plays were first printed in the collected edition of 1632, and whether he wrote them is disputed. Eliot does not cite Lyly; "Jug Jug" at line 103 and the birdsong at lines 203–206 use the convention, with "So rudely forc'd" set between the notes.

### sappho-104
Problem: "relay stations" and "the typist's teatime" are performance. Eliot's note gives both "longshore" and "dory," and Demetrius's reason for quoting is worth a word.
Proposed:
note: Two lines that survive because Demetrius quoted them in his treatise On Style (section 141), for the charm of their repeated "you bring." Eliot's note says his lines "may not appear as exact as Sappho's," and that he had in mind the "longshore" or "dory" fisherman "who returns at nightfall." Byron imitated the lines in Don Juan (Canto III, stanza 107), and Stevenson's "Requiem" has "Home is the sailor, home from sea"; both have been cited as the English between Sappho and Eliot.

### goldsmith-vicar
Problem: "in the same meter" is wrong. Goldsmith's line is a four-beat line; Eliot adds "and" to make his pentameter. "she is allowed not to die" is an epigram.
Proposed:
note: Olivia Primrose, seduced and abandoned by Squire Thornhill, sings this at her mother's request, the family sitting on the bank where she first met him. Eliot's note cites the song. He takes its first line and adds "and" to fill out his pentameter (Goldsmith's is a four-beat line); the typist then "smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone." The scene has been read as contempt for her, and also as the reverse: she does not die, as the song says a fallen woman must.
Source: The Vicar of Wakefield, ch. XXIV (Gutenberg #2667); the poem, lines 253–256.

### froude-elizabeth
Problem: "in circumstances that made it impossible" and "with nothing coming of it" are judgments given as facts and the second is a bow at the end. The letter can be dated; Dudley was not yet Leicester.
Proposed:
note: Froude quotes a dispatch of 30 June 1561 from the Spanish ambassador, Álvaro de la Quadra, Bishop of Aquila, to Philip II, describing an afternoon on the Thames that June. Dudley, not yet Earl of Leicester, had lost his wife, Amy Robsart, the previous September in a fall at Cumnor Place; the talk of marriage came to nothing. Eliot's note cites Froude for lines 279–289, the barge with its "gilded shell / Red and gold."
Source: De Quadra to Philip II, 30 June 1561, in Froude, History of England, vol. VII (Reign of Elizabeth, vol. I), ch. iv; Amy Robsart died 8 September 1560; Dudley created Earl of Leicester 1564.

### augustine-confessions
Problem: "six books later" is wrong (Book III to Book X is seven; say Book X). "which is why the Fire Sermon ends with it" passes a reading off as fact. "the one in Everyman's Library" is unsourced as a fact about Eliot's copy. Eliot's second Augustine note, which says the pairing with the Buddha is "not an accident," is the fact the reader wants here.
Proposed:
note: Augustine's account, in Book III, of his student years at Carthage, "a cauldron of unholy loves," and, in Book X, of the pull of beautiful things on a man trying to look at God. ### buddha-fire-sermon
Problem: "as important as the Sermon on the Mount" paraphrases where Eliot's words are short enough to quote; "It is the text behind the whole third section, in which everything, from the river to the typist, is seen as burning" is a reading stated as fact and a closing flourish.
Proposed:
note: Preached, in the account Warren translates from the Mahāvagga, to a thousand former fire-worshipping ascetics at Gayā Head, soon after the enlightenment. Eliot studied Sanskrit and Indian philosophy at Harvard in 1911–13 and knew Warren's Buddhism in Translations (Harvard Oriental Series, 1896). His note says the sermon "corresponds in importance to the Sermon on the Mount," sends the reader to Warren for the complete text, and calls Warren "one of the great pioneers of Buddhist studies in the Occident." Part III takes its title from the sermon, and line 308 its word.

### brihadaranyaka
Problem: "The fable is about hearing what you need to hear in a single syllable" is an epigram; "gives each a confession" is a reading; "the poem ends the way a Vedic text ends" repeats the sentence before it. Eliot's note gives the reference as "5, 1," and later editions softened "a feeble translation"; both are facts a reader wants.
Proposed:
note: Eliot read the Upanishads in Sanskrit at Harvard with Charles Lanman in 1911–13. His note gives the reference as Brihadaranyaka 5, 1 (the passage is 5.2 in the standard numbering) and sends the reader to Paul Deussen's German translation, Sechzig Upanishads des Veda (1897). In the fable each group hears in the one syllable the command it needs. Eliot reorders the three commands, glosses them in his note as "Give, sympathise, control," and follows each with a response. "Shantih," his last note says, is "a formal ending to an Upanishad," and "The Peace which passeth understanding" is "a feeble translation of the content of this word"; later editions soften this to "our equivalent to this word."
Source: Eliot's notes (1922 text, Wikisource transcription); Faber Collected Poems for the later wording.

### bradley
Problem: "the philosophical form of Ugolino's tower: each of us shut in a circle of experience that no key opens" is a metaphor doing the work of a fact, and "set against exactly this" is a flourish. What is factual, and better, is that Eliot's own note puts Bradley next to Dante's Ugolino.
Proposed:
note: The Oxford idealist on whom Eliot wrote his Harvard doctoral dissertation (finished 1916, never defended, published 1964 as Knowledge and Experience in the Philosophy of F. H. Bradley). Eliot's note on "I have heard the key" first quotes Dante's Ugolino hearing the tower door locked below him (Inferno XXXIII) and then adds this passage from page 346 of Appearance and Reality. The two together gloss "each in his prison," the lines that follow "Dayadhvam."

### shackleton-south
Problem: "made it the modern half of the road to Emmaus" is a metaphor, and "Frank Hurley's photographs … are the images to hold beside it" is an instruction to the reader with no bearing on the poem. The contrast between Eliot's memory of the passage and Shackleton's words is worth stating plainly.
Proposed:
note: In May 1916, after the Endurance had been crushed in the ice and the boat journey to South Georgia survived, Shackleton, Worsley and Crean crossed the island's mountains on foot to reach the whaling station at Stromness. Eliot's note says he had forgotten which expedition it was, "but I think one of Shackleton's," and remembers the party's "constant delusion that there was one more member than could actually be counted"; Shackleton's own words are that "it seemed to me often that we were four, not three." Eliot's headnote to Part V names the journey to Emmaus as one of the section's three themes.

### pervigilium-veneris
Problem: "The whole of the poem's spring, its birds and its silence are in these nine lines" is the closing flourish. Eliot's note, "Cf. Philomela in Parts II and III," is the fact that belongs there. Everything else, including "ceu"/"uti" and 1936, checks out.
Proposed:
note: A late Latin poem of ninety-three lines for the eve of the spring festival of Venus, with the refrain "cras amet qui numquam amavit." After the flowers and the nymphs, the last stanza turns to the poet's own silence and envies the swallow, who is Procne, sister of the raped Philomela. Eliot's note cites the poem and adds "Cf. Philomela in Parts II and III." His 1922 text reads "ceu" for the poem's "uti," and so do his manuscripts; the line was changed to "uti" in Collected Poems 1909–1935 (1936).

### nerval-desdichado
Problem: "written in the intervals of madness a year before he hanged himself in a Paris alley" is melodrama where dates will do; "and can still play" and "leaves the consolation" are flourishes. "The disinherited one" is Scott's sense of the word, not the Spanish.
Proposed:
note: The first of Nerval's Chimères, first published in December 1853, in the last years of his illness; he hanged himself in a Paris street on 26 January 1855. The title is usually traced to the shield of the Disinherited Knight in Scott's Ivanhoe; the Spanish word means "unfortunate" rather than "disinherited." The "tour abolie" is often read as the Tarot's Tower. Eliot's note cites the sonnet; he quotes its second line only.
Source: Le Mousquetaire, 10 December 1853; Les Filles du feu (1854).

### kyd-spanish-tragedy
Problem: The last sentence is the epigram the brief names. The rest is plain and right.
Proposed:
note: Hieronimo, Knight Marshal of Spain, whose son Horatio has been murdered and hanged in an arbour, is asked by the murderers to write a play for the court. "I'll fit you" means both I'll supply what you want and I'll pay you back. The play he supplies is written, as the early quartos' note to the reader says, "in sundry languages," and in the performance the murderers are stabbed for real. "Hieronimo is mad againe" is from the title page of the 1615 quarto and of the reprints that followed it. Eliot's note cites the play.

### london-bridge
Problem: "and the only one in English a child would know" is a flourish; "the first of the fragments shored against the ruins" can be stated as what it is. The folklore claim gains from a name and from the Opies' caution.
Proposed:
note: The rhyme runs through materials that fail until a watchman is set. Folklorists of Frazer's generation, Alice Gomme among them, connected it with the custom of burying a victim under a bridge's foundations to make it stand; the Opies, in The Oxford Dictionary of Nursery Rhymes, treat the connection with caution. The crowd flowed over the real bridge at line 62. Here the rhyme opens the run of quotations, lines 427–430, before "These fragments I have shored against my ruins."

### shakespearian-rag
Problem: The Follies claim contradicts most of the scholarship (which calls the song a Follies hit), so a reader needs to know where it comes from; "misquote one on purpose" states an inference as fact.
Proposed:
note: Published in 1912 and advertised as a number from that year's Ziegfeld Follies. Matthew Guerrieri's search of Variety (2017) found no sign that it was sung in the show, and the first performance he could find was in vaudeville, at the Columbia Theatre, New York, on 5 January 1913. The lyric has Shakespeare's characters dancing the ragtime steps of the day. Eliot's "Shakespeherian," with its extra syllable, is generally read as deliberate, giving the word the rag's syncopation; the poem also reverses the song's "intelligent … elegant." No recording from the period is known.
Source: Matthew Guerrieri, "That old classical drag," Soho the Dog, 10 March 2017, citing Variety 1912–13.

### dans-le-restaurant
Problem: "then, out of nowhere, the drowned Phoenician" is chat, and "He is the only poet the poem quotes in two languages" is the epigram the brief names. "in 1917–18" is loose (the four are the French poems in Poems, 1920). Pound's cutting of the shipwreck narrative and his insistence that Phlebas stay is the fact a reader wants here.
Proposed:
note: One of the four poems in French in Eliot's Poems (1920). ### death-of-st-narcissus
Problem: "a saint who wanted to be a tree, a fish and a girl" misreads the poem: Narcissus is sure he had been each of them. "The gray rock's shadow, made red, became the poem's first prophecy" is a flourish. The Waste Land keeps only the second line as it stands. The rule sentence stays.
Proposed:
note: Set in type for Poetry (Chicago) in 1915 and withdrawn in proof; first published in 1950. The poem is about a saint who is sure he had been a tree, a fish and a young girl, and who dies pierced with arrows. The manuscript survived among the papers Eliot sent to John Quinn in 1922 with the Waste Land drafts, now in the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library. Its opening goes on, in the cadence the later poem keeps, to promise the listener something other than his own shadow on the sand or against the fire. The Waste Land keeps the second line, with "gray" changed to "red," and reshapes the first. The poem is still in copyright, so only its first two lines are given here.
Source: Poems Written in Early Youth (1950); the Facsimile (1971), pp. 90–97.

### conrad-hod
Problem: "still answers to it" and "is worth rereading with Part III in mind" are a reading and an instruction; the "what" line is coy. Pound withdrew his objection, which the note leaves out.
Proposed:
what: The epigraph of the typescript, replaced by Petronius.

### huxley-crome-yellow

## The batch as a whole
The facts in this batch are mostly sound; of twenty-eight records only two had errors of substance (Goldsmith's meter, Augustine's "six books later"), and a third misdescribed Narcissus. The trouble is the endings: twenty or so notes close on an epigram, a metaphor or a wink ("He is the only poet the poem quotes in two languages," "the poem's river in prose," "she is allowed not to die," "Take the notes as part of the poem and as a joke"), and in most of them a real fact was waiting to take the last sentence's place: Eliot's own note, a date, a letter, what Pound did. Several notes also present critics' readings as facts (the Lyly and Tennyson sources, the Fire Sermon as "the text behind the whole third section," the "spring hunt" in Day). What I have proposed is to keep every fact that checks, add the ones the reader would want with their source, and let the notes end on those. One rights matter: the Frontiers of Criticism passage quotes two sentences of a 1956 text and should be cut to phrases.
