# Batch E, final text (applied to art/tarot/NOTES.md and the vignette titles)

## back
ANSWERS: "Fear death by water" (55), and Part IV, Death by Water: "A current under sea / Picked his bones in whispers" (315–316). The pearl is one of the drowned sailor's eyes, "Those are pearls that were his eyes" (48).

## bad-nerves
ANSWERS: 111–138, "My nerves are bad to-night. Yes, bad. Stay with me. / Speak to me. Why do you never speak. Speak."; also "The wind under the door" (118), "rats' alley" (115) and "we shall play a game of chess" (137).

## belladonna
SHOWS: A woman seated in a grotto of stone, in a black mantle and gown that leave only her face and hands bare; the pupils of her eyes are wide, as belladonna drops make them; her lips are violet; she holds up a sprig of deadly nightshade, leaves, bells and berries. Sea visible through the mouth of the cave.
ANSWERS: 49–50, "Here is Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks, / The lady of situations." "The Lady of the Rocks" recalls Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i> and Walter Pater's description of the Mona Lisa in <i>The Renaissance</i> (1873): "She is older than the rocks among which she sits." <i>Belladonna</i> is Italian for "beautiful lady". It is also the name of deadly nightshade, a poisonous plant, and of the eye-drops made from it, which women once used to widen their pupils.

## blank
SHOWS: Bare paper inside the frame. Very faintly, the merchant's roped bundle shows through, reversed, as if printed on the other side of the card.

## crowd-in-a-ring
ANSWERS: 56, "I see crowds of people, walking round in a ring." A few lines later a crowd flows over London Bridge (62–65); Eliot's notes on 63 and 64 compare it with Dante's <i>Inferno</i>, iii. 55–7 (the long train of the dead outside the gate of Hell) and iv. 25–7 (the sighs of Limbo).

## elizabeth-and-leicester
ANSWERS: 279–291, "Elizabeth and Leicester / Beating oars / The stern was formed / A gilded shell / Red and gold", and the "White towers" of line 289. Eliot's note on 279 quotes, from Froude's history of Elizabeth, a letter of the Spanish ambassador De Quadra about an afternoon in the royal barge when the Queen sat with Lord Robert Dudley, later Earl of Leicester, and there was talk of their marrying.

## eugenides
ANSWERS: 207–214, "Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant / Unshaven, with a pocket full of currants". He is the one-eyed merchant of Madame Sosostris's pack (52), drawn there in profile and here full-face; Eliot's note on 218 calls that merchant "seller of currants".

## fisher-king
ANSWERS: 187–195, "While I was fishing in the dull canal / On a winter evening round behind the gashouse". The Fisher King is the wounded king of the Grail legend, whose land lies waste until he is healed. The opening paragraph of Eliot's notes says that "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem were suggested by" Jessie L. Weston's book on the legend, <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920). His note on line 425, "Fishing, with the arid plain behind me", sends the reader to her chapter on the Fisher King, and his note on 46 links the king to the Man with Three Staves, a card of Madame Sosostris's reading.

## hanged-man
SHOWS: A dead tree with one long bough for a beam and a rope tied to it, the noose hanging open. The air is dense cross-hatching; where the Hanged Man would hang, head down with one leg crooked, the paper is left bare in his outline, with a gold halo where the head would be. Far off at the bottom, on a road, two walkers and a hooded third. A green shoot on the dead bough.
ANSWERS: 54–55, "I do not find / The Hanged Man." Eliot's note on 46 says the card "fits my purpose in two ways". It is "associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer", the sacrificed god of Frazer's book <i>The Golden Bough</i>, killed so that the land may live again. Eliot also associates him with "the hooded figure in the passage of the disciples to Emmaus in Part V" (360–366), where the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples (Luke 24). The walkers on the road and the shoot on the dead bough come from that note. The head-down pose and the crooked leg are traditional to the card; the halo follows Pamela Colman Smith's drawing of 1909 for the Rider tarot pack.

## hyacinth-girl
SHOWS: A girl standing in a garden with her arms full of hyacinth spikes, hair wet, looking straight out. Round her the hatching of the garden opens into a bare oval, the "heart of light" of line 41. Dark petals falling at the edges.

## london-bridge-falling
ANSWERS: 427, "London Bridge is falling down falling down falling down", the nursery rhyme. The same bridge, still standing and with the crowd on it, is on the card The Crowd on London Bridge (60–68).

## madame-sosostris
ANSWERS: 43–59, "Madame Sosostris, famous clairvoyante, / Had a bad cold, nevertheless / Is known to be the wisest woman in Europe, / With a wicked pack of cards."

## marie
ANSWERS: 13–18, "Marie, / Marie, hold on tight. And down we went. / In the mountains, there you feel free." Eliot's widow, Valerie Eliot, in her 1971 facsimile edition of the drafts, identifies Marie with the Countess Marie Larisch (1858–1940), a niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria and author of a memoir, <i>My Past</i> (1913). Eliot had met the Countess, and the sledding, Valerie Eliot says, was taken from his conversation with her.

## one-eyed-merchant
SHOWS: Mr. Eugenides in profile, so that only one eye shows. Bowler hat, astrakhan collar, long overcoat, unshaven; a pocket full of currants and a handful held out. On his back a large roped bundle, its contents hidden. A wall and a doorway behind.
ANSWERS: 52, "the one-eyed merchant". Eliot's note on 218 speaks of "the one-eyed merchant, seller of currants", who "melts into the Phoenician Sailor". The seller of currants is Mr. Eugenides, "the Smyrna merchant / Unshaven, with a pocket full of currants" (209–210), so the card draws him as Eugenides.

## philomel
SHOWS: A picture over an antique mantel, framed "as though a window gave upon the sylvan scene" (98). In it Philomel runs to the left, her arms already turning to wings, her head turned back; behind her the barbarous king, a black figure, crowned, with his sword raised; in the tree above, the nightingale sings. Below the mantelshelf, the dark of the fireplace.
ANSWERS: 97–103, "The change of Philomel, by the barbarous king / So rudely forced; yet there the nightingale / Filled all the desert with inviolable voice". Eliot's note refers to Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> vi, where Tereus, king of Thrace, rapes his wife's sister Philomela and cuts out her tongue. In the telling Eliot follows, she becomes the nightingale.

## phoenician-sailor
ANSWERS: 47–48, "Is your card, the drowned Phoenician Sailor, / (Those are pearls that were his eyes. Look!)", and Part IV, Death by Water (312–321). The pearls are from Ariel's song in <i>The Tempest</i> (I. ii). He is drawn head down, in the posture of the Tarot's Hanged Man, the card Madame Sosostris does not find (54–55). The likeness is the artist's. Eliot's note on 46 connects the Hanged Man with the Hanged God of Frazer's <i>Golden Bough</i> and with the hooded figure of Part V (360–366), and says of the Sailor only that he and the Merchant "appear later".

## road-with-no-water
SHOWS: A sandy road winding up between two masses of rock toward a gap; in the right-hand mass a black cave mouth with broken teeth hanging in it; cracked earth either side of the road; three flat-roofed mudcracked houses with a red face sneering from each door; dry grass; a cicada on a stone; two dry cracks of thunder in the sky and no cloud.

## sibyl
ANSWERS: The epigraph, from Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i> (chapter 48). Trimalchio, the rich host of the dinner party in the book, says he saw the Sibyl at Cumae with his own eyes, hanging in a jar, and that when the boys asked her "Sibyl, what do you want?" she answered "I want to die." How she came to that state is told in Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> xiv: Apollo granted her as many years of life as there were grains in a handful of sand, and she had forgotten to ask for youth to go with them.

## swallow
ANSWERS: 429, "Quando fiam ceu chelidon—O swallow swallow". The Latin is from the last stanza of the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i> ("The Vigil of Venus"), an anonymous Latin poem of uncertain date, probably of the fourth century AD. The poet has listened to the nightingale, "the girl of Tereus", and asks: "when shall I become as the swallow, that I may cease to be silent?" (The poem reads <i>uti chelidon</i>; Eliot printed <i>ceu</i>.) The nightingale is Philomela, whose story is on the card Philomel (97–103), and Eliot's note adds "Cf. Philomela in Parts II and III"; in the Latin telling of the story, which Eliot follows, Philomela became the nightingale and her sister Procne the swallow. "O swallow swallow" echoes a song in Tennyson's <i>The Princess</i> (1847), "O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South".

## thames-daughters
ANSWERS: 266–306, "The river sweats / Oil and tar / The barges drift / With the turning tide / Red sails / Wide". The name comes from Eliot's note on 266: "The Song of the (three) Thames-daughters begins here. From line 292 to 306 inclusive they speak in turn"; the note refers to the Rhine-daughters of Wagner's <i>Götterdämmerung</i>.

## the-third
ANSWERS: 360–366, "Who is the third who walks always beside you? / When I count, there are only you and I together". Eliot's note says the lines were "stimulated by the account of one of the Antarctic expeditions (I forget which, but I think one of Shackleton's)", whose party, at the end of its strength, "had the constant delusion that there was one more member than could actually be counted". His note on Part V as a whole names "the journey to Emmaus" (Luke 24), where the risen Christ walks unrecognised beside two disciples.

## three-staves
ANSWERS: 51, "Here is the man with three staves". Eliot's note says: "The Man with Three Staves (an authentic member of the Tarot pack) I associate, quite arbitrarily, with the Fisher King himself." The Fisher King is the wounded king of the Grail legend, whose land lies waste. The drawing gives him a crutch for the wound, a rod to fish with, and a staff set in the dry ground, which has begun to sprout. The composition follows the Three of Wands of the Rider tarot pack (1909), drawn by Pamela Colman Smith, where a man with his back turned stands on a height looking out over the sea at ships.

## thunder
SHOWS: One great black cloud filling the top of the card, its underside lobed, and three forks of lightning out of it, one for each of the thunder's three words. Below, the white peaks of Himavant, the jungle crouched in humps, and Ganga sunken to a thread between wide cracked banks with limp leaves hanging at the edge. On the water a small boat responding to its oar and sail; on the near bank a gold key.
ANSWERS: 396–423, "Ganga was sunken, and the limp leaves / Waited for rain, while the black clouds / Gathered far distant, over Himavant". Ganga is the Ganges and Himavant the Himalaya. The thunder then speaks three times, "DA", and each time the speaker hears in it one of three Sanskrit words, <i>Datta</i>, <i>Dayadhvam</i>, <i>Damyata</i>, which Eliot's note translates "Give, sympathise, control" and traces to a fable in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, one of the ancient Hindu scriptures. The key on the bank is for <i>Dayadhvam</i> ("I have heard the key / Turn in the door once", 412–413); the boat is for <i>Damyata</i> ("The boat responded / Gaily, to the hand expert with sail and oar", 419–420).

## tiresias
ANSWERS: 215–248, "I Tiresias, though blind, throbbing between two lives, / Old man with wrinkled female breasts". Eliot's note on 218 quotes the passage of Ovid (<i>Metamorphoses</i> iii) in which Tiresias strikes two coupling snakes with his staff and is turned into a woman for seven years, then strikes them again and becomes a man once more. Hence the snakes at his feet.

## tower
ANSWERS: 430, "Le Prince d'Aquitaine à la tour abolie" (the Prince of Aquitaine of the ruined tower), the second line of <i>El Desdichado</i> ("The Disinherited"), a sonnet by the French poet Gérard de Nerval, collected in his <i>Les Chimères</i> (1854); Eliot's note cites it. The star, the lute and the black sun on the card are from the same sonnet: "ma seule étoile est morte" (my only star is dead) and "mon luth constellé / Porte le soleil noir de la Mélancolie" (my star-strewn lute bears the black sun of Melancholy). The Tower is also a card of the real Tarot pack, trump XVI, where it is shown struck by lightning.

## typist
SHOWS: The typist's bed-sitter at teatime: the window with her combinations (a one-piece undergarment) drying on the line, the gramophone with its horn on a table, the divan with stockings and slippers piled on it, tins and a stove, and the typist standing, smoothing her hair with one automatic hand.

## wheel
ANSWERS: 51, "and here the Wheel". It is drawn as a ship's wheel because of line 320, "O you who turn the wheel and look to windward". The four figures follow the medieval pictures of Fortune's wheel, as in the Carmina Burana manuscript, where a king is shown at four points of the turn: <i>regno</i> (I reign) at the top, <i>regnavi</i> (I have reigned) falling, <i>sum sine regno</i> (I have no kingdom) beneath, <i>regnabo</i> (I shall reign) climbing.
