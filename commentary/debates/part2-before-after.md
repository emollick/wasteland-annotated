# Part II: before and after, eight places

Picked so the change of voice can be judged in a minute. The whole of every note, with the argument over it, is in part2.md beside this file.


## The end of the note on the woman with bad nerves (lines 111–114)

Before:

    He is a man whose inner life is made of other people's lines. That is a diagnosis of the poem as much as of the marriage.

After:

    What he thinks in reply is partly other people's words: a line adapted from a play by Webster (118), Ariel's song from The Tempest (125), a ragtime song (128). The rest, rats' alley, "Nothing again nothing", the hot water at ten, is his own.


## The way in called "Watch the clock"

Before:

    Hot water at ten, a closed car at four, to-night, tomorrow, HURRY UP PLEASE ITS TIME, good night. The section is a timetable. The clock shows every hour the poem names; most of them are here.

After:

    The poem gives three hours by the clock; two of them are here, ten and four (lines 135–136), and the third is nine, at line 68. The section also has "tonight", "tomorrow" and closing time, called five times. The Clock lens marks every time of day the poem names.


## The end of the note on the Shakespearian Rag (line 128)

Before:

    The high and the low do not so much collide as turn out to have been the same thing all along: Shakespeare is what you can hum.

After:

    Some readers hear in the four "O"s the "O, o, o, o" given to the dying Hamlet in the 1623 Folio and to the dying Lear in the 1608 Quarto.


## The end of the note on Cleopatra's barge (line 77)

Before:

    Cleopatra, Dido, Eve: three women about to be undone in their gardens and palaces, and then Philomel.

After:

    So the room holds Cleopatra, Dido and Eve before the eye reaches Philomel on the wall (line 99), four women whose stories end badly. Commentators commonly read the room as furnished with ruined loves.


## Vivien Eliot's pencil, in the note on the pub scene (line 139)

Before:

    In the typescript Vivien wrote "WONDERFUL" and "Yes" beside these lines, and the line "What you get married for if you dont want children?" is hers, pencilled in the margin and taken into the text.

After:

    Two of its lines are by Vivien Eliot, his wife, pencilled in the margin of the typescript: line 153, and line 164, which replaced a line of Eliot's own. At the end she wrote "Splendid last lines". (The "WONDERFUL" is beside the nervous dialogue, not here; the note on that scene now has it.)


## The pathway stop at the pub (line 139)

Before:

    A pub at closing time. The poem's most ordinary speech, and its most overheard. The publican's shout is also the poem's clock running out.

After:

    A pub at closing time. A woman tells a friend what she said to Lil, whose husband is back from the army. The line in capitals is the landlord calling time.


## The note on the wind under the door (line 118)

Before:

    Two surgeons have watched Romelio stab the wounded Contarino and have taken him for dead; when he groans, one asks the other whether the wind is "in that door still," meaning is that how things stand. The man is not dead: the stab has lanced his old wound and saved him. Neither is the man in this room, though "Nothing again nothing" is his answer to what the wind is doing.

After:

    Romelio, disguised as a physician, bribes the two surgeons attending the wounded Contarino to leave him alone with the patient, and stabs him; the surgeons, who have not gone far, come back. When Contarino groans, one asks "Did he not groan?" and the other, "Is the wind in that door still?", an idiom of the time for "so that is how things stand?". The surgeon means that the man is alive after all. So he is: the stab has drained the old wound, and Contarino recovers. In the poem the wind is his answer to "What is that noise?". In Webster the line asks whether a man is alive; she asks him the same at line 126, "Are you alive, or not?"


## The way in called "Who is not in the room", its last sentence

Before:

    The section's subject is an absence, and the quotations are its shape.

After:

    Two of his unspoken answers point to the dead: the men of the trenches, and the drowned father in Ariel's song from The Tempest.
