# Debate record: Part IV, Death by Water (lines 312–321)

20 September 2026. Editor: the Part IV debate thread. Two agents took part: an Eliot scholar, briefed on the facsimile, Ricks and McCue, Southam, Rainey, Grover Smith, Kenner, Gardner, Gordon and Crawford, with the web to check sources; and a reader, an intelligent non-specialist reading on a screen, with no web. Each read every note independently in round 1. The editor then rewrote, and both read the rewrite in round 2; a third round was used only where one side still objected. On substance the scholar had the last word, on wording the reader. Unsettled points are listed at the end.

What was under debate: every editable piece of text hung on the ten lines in `/mnt/project-files/commentary/part4/`: the two glosses (titles, bodies, the translation of the French), the six "ways into" the part and the list's heading, the five pathway stops that fall in the part, and the drafts-page record. Two things opened from these lines live outside the part's files (the library record for "Dans le Restaurant" and the tarot tool's notes on the two cards); those were debated too, and the result went to `shared-proposals-part4.md` for the thread that owns them.

The standard, from Ethan's message of 17:28 UTC: the commentary must be clear, good in its wording, and real. What had to go, taken from his two examples ("Where the poem is standing"; "The poem's argument is a weather report: rain that is unwelcome, rivers that are dirty, a sea that drowns, and then twenty-nine lines of rock before three words of rain"): tour-guide framing; the aphoristic "X is a Y:" followed by a rhythmic list; the flourish that counts things to sound clever; metaphors that describe the poem instead of explaining it; the last sentence that ties a bow. What was aimed at: a scholarly editor writing plainly for readers, saying what the line refers to, what is known and how, what is disputed, and whose reading a reading is; then stopping.

Item ids used below (the text of each as it stood is in the "Before" column of each entry): G1–G5 the two glosses, W0–W6 the ways in, P1–P5 the pathway stops, D1 the drafts record, S1 the library record for "Dans le Restaurant", S2 the tarot card notes.

## The reader's preamble, round 1

How I read: I took each item as it appears on the screen, a card beside the line, a paragraph at the head of the section, a line on a pathway, and asked whether a person explaining the poem to me would say it that way. Where I say a fact looks doubtful I mean only that it looked doubtful to me; the scholar will know.

Three things run across the whole set and are better fixed together than item by item:

1. The count of the cut lines is given four ways: "Eighty-odd" (G4), "some ninety lines" (G5), "eighty-three" and "ninety-three-line" (W2), "ninety-three lines" (D1). Pick one pair of figures and use it everywhere.
2. The story of Pound's cut is told three times (G5, W2, D1), with the same Pound quotation in full twice. A reader who opens the draft card and then the drafts page reads the same paragraph twice with different adjectives. One full telling (D1) and two short pointers would do.
3. The same three tells recur: a pun in the title (G4, W2), a colon followed by a rhythmic list (G3, G5, W5), and a closing instruction that performs wisdom (W4, W6, P1). Once those go, most of what is left is fine.

## The editor's decisions after round 1

Rules applied: on substance the scholar has the last word, on wording the reader. Where the two drafts differed I took the scholar's facts and the reader's sentences. Three decisions that run through everything:

1. The count of the cut lines. Perloff says 92 (82 cut), Martz 82 preceding lines, most web summaries 93, and the facsimile could not be reached. Every note now says "some ninety lines", which all the sources support, and no note gives a figure for the lines cut.
2. Pound's reply. The spelling of the long word differs in every printing found (Paige's 1950 edition, on two scanned copies, reads "the drowned phoen. sailor. And he is needed ABsolootly where he is."; Perloff prints ABSOLOOTLY, Martz ABSoloootly, the site had ABSOloootly). The Letters of T. S. Eliot vol. 1 would settle it and could not be reached. The notes now quote the letter up to "the drowned phoen. sailor", paraphrase the disputed clause, and quote "Must stay in."
3. "??doubtful" on "Dirge" could not be verified by anyone; it is dropped. The exclusion of "Dirge" is told from the letters that were checked (Pound advising that the "superfluities at the end" be abolished; Eliot: "Certainly omit miscellaneous pieces").

Also dropped everywhere: "shaken" / "unnerved" (a reading; the three question marks do the work); "Gloucester" as a place named in the draft (the draft names the cape and the Dry Salvages; Gloucester survives only in P5, as the family's summer place); "cards" as a pastime in the draft (unverified); "iceberg" as a fact (the draft has a wall of ice); Tennyson as Eliot's own model (Dante is Eliot's word; Tennyson is the critics'); "written in 1918" (published September 1918; written by 1917 on Schuchard's account).

## The scholar's answers to the editor's questions, round 2

**1. W6, the gravestone formula.** The epitaph reading is recognised. Herbert Musurillo, "A Note on The Waste Land (Part IV)", Classical Philology 51 (1956), 174–175, compared the section to the sepulchral epigrams for drowned sailors in the Greek Anthology, book VII (from 7.263 on), which characteristically speak to the sailor or traveller passing the tomb and warn him; Adam Roberts repeats the point in his essay on Phlebas. I could not open Ricks and McCue, Southam or Rainey to see whether they cite Musurillo or the Anthology. The English churchyard formula "as you are now, so once was I" belongs to the same memento mori tradition (its Latin ancestor, "sum quod eris, quod es ante fui", is a Roman epitaph formula), but I found no scholar applying that formula to line 321 by name. Ruling: it may stand as an observation about the line's form, since it is a comparison a reader can test against the line and claims no source; it should sit beside the attributed reading so that the site is not seen to invent the epitaph idea. Counter-draft under W6.

**2. W3, Ophelia.** Right. Her exit line, "Good night, ladies; good night, sweet ladies; good night, good night", closes her second mad scene, IV.v; the Queen reports the drowning in IV.vii ("There is a willow grows aslant a brook ... Fell in the weeping brook"). She is not seen alive again after IV.v. Eliot's line 172 alters the punctuation only. "Not long before she drowns" is fair.

**3. D1, the draft details.**
"Four rhymed quatrains on the sailor ashore": not fair, and the number was mine. Perloff confirms rhymed quatrains (abab) introducing "a foolish sailor on shore leave"; my recollection is three, twelve lines; unverified from anything reachable.
"A song heard one night on watch from three women in the rigging": fair. Perloff: "a mysterious Sirens' song heard one night on watch (lines 65-72)". The three women with white hair streaming behind them, seen "in the fore cross-trees", are my recollection of the draft; the cross-trees are at the masthead, so "high in the mast" or "aloft" is closer than "in the rigging", which is not wrong.
"A wall of ice stands dead ahead": fair. The draft: "A line, a white line, a long white line, / A wall, a barrier, towards which we drove" (source 6); the crew's next words are about bears on it and cracked ice for a cocktail; the Eliot Foundation page reads it as "hitting ice". "Dead ahead" renders "towards which we drove". The ship's end is not narrated, only "no more noise now", so "and the ship is lost" is the right degree of statement.
"Told by one of the crew": fair. Ellmann's quoted lines are in the first person plural ("We beat around the cape"); Auchincloss: "seen through the eyes of one of the fishermen".
"The gear fails": fair; the Eliot Foundation page quotes "A water cask was opened, smelt of oil, / Another brackish. / Then the main gaffjaws / Jammed".

**4. D1, Bleistein and "Dirge".** "Bleistein, the Jewish caricature of 'Burbank with a Baedeker' (1919)": accurate. Bleistein ("Chicago Semite Viennese") is the poem's antisemitic caricature; the poem was written in 1919 and first printed in Art & Letters (Summer 1919) before Ara Vos Prec and Poems in 1920, so "(1919)" is right whichever date the parenthesis means. "And the sea-change is decay": fair. Ellmann quotes the parody's "Graves' Disease in a dead jew's/ man's eyes! / Where the crabs have eat the lids", "That is lace that was his nose", "See the lips unfold unfold / From the teeth gold in gold"; the draft keeps Shakespeare's phrase in the line about suffering "a sea-change" and has the body rot round it. (The parody's other barb, that the drowned Jew stays expensive, need not be added.) "Among the short poems Eliot thought of adding to the sequence was 'Dirge'": on the letters alone this cannot be said, because the letters name no poem. Pound's letter of 24 January speaks of "the remaining superfluities at the end", "the last three", "The song" and "the sovegna" (the last identifies "Exequy", which ends with Arnaut's line); Eliot answers "Certainly omit miscellaneous pieces. Those at end." That the third was "Dirge" comes from the facsimile, and Ellmann's 1971 review of the facsimile states it in almost the editor's words: poems "for a time intended to be placed between the poem's sections, then at the end of it", one of them "a renewed thrust at poor Bleistein, drowned now". So the sentence stands, on the facsimile as reported by Ellmann; the site should cite the facsimile, not the letters, for it. "??doubtful" stays out.

**5. G3, "written in 1917", and the Pauline citations.** Keep "written in 1917". Schuchard's sentence sits between Eliot's letter to his mother of spring 1917 announcing French poems for the Little Review and Vivien's letter of 30 April 1917, so he dates all four to about April 1917; he calls the manuscripts undated, and he is the general editor of Eliot's prose and cites the Letters. Every other reachable source says 1916–17; none says 1918, and the site's former "1918" was the publication year. "Written in 1917 or early 1918" would invent a doubt no source expresses; "by 1917" is no clearer. If the card wants the honest margin in three words, "written in 1917 (the manuscript is undated)". On the citations: give one. Eliot's "Gentile or Jew" matches no verse (Paul's order is always Jew first, and his usual pair in the KJV is "Jew and Greek"), so two references look like a source found when there is only a habit of Paul's. It began as a narrative of a fishing schooner sailing out of Gloucester, Massachusetts, past the Dry Salvages, north to the Banks, in a voice modelled on Dante's Ulysses (<i>Inferno</i> XXVI) and Tennyson's: the crew, the drink, the log of the ship's way, an iceberg, and the wreck. ## Round 1

### Scholar

VERDICT: rewrite (the facts are mostly right; the voice and two attributions are not).

FINDINGS
1. "ran to some ninety lines": true (92 by Perloff, 93 in other accounts; source 18d).
2. "a fishing schooner sailing out of Gloucester, Massachusetts, past the Dry Salvages, north to the Banks": the Dry Salvages and "the eastern banks" are in the draft (sources 10, 17); Gloucester is an inference from the cape and from Eliot's summers there, not a name in the draft as far as any reachable quotation shows (source 18c).
3. "in a voice modelled on Dante's Ulysses (Inferno XXVI) and Tennyson's": Dante is Eliot's own attribution (source 15); Tennyson is the critics' (Perloff's "Tennysonian blank verse"; the draft's quoted phrase "much seen and much endured" recalls Tennyson's "Much have I seen and known"). The gloss should not put Tennyson in Eliot's mouth.
4. "the crew, the drink, the log of the ship's way, an iceberg, and the wreck": a summary; "iceberg" is the standard reading of a wall of ice ahead (Perloff, the Eliot Foundation page); "the log of the ship's way" describes the knots-and-watches lines and is unverifiable from here.
5. "Pound cut all of it except the ten lines that stand": true (sources 10, 11, 13).
6. "wrote 'Bad' against the manuscript of the sea narrative, adding that he could not attack it until he had a typescript": true as reported by Perloff (source 13): "In the margin of the manuscript, Pound wrote, 'Bad--but cant attack until I get typescript.'"
7. "Eliot, shaken, wrote back": "shaken" is a reading. The letter is a numbered list of five practical questions (source 7).
8. "Perhaps better omit Phlebas also???": true, exact (source 7).
9. "Pound's reply is the most quoted sentence in the story of the poem": unverifiable flourish.
10. The Pound quotation: the site prints "the drowned phoen. sailor, and he is needed ABSOloootly where he is." Paige prints "the drowned phoen. sailor. And he is needed ABsolootly where he is." The site's punctuation matches Martz's quotation (1993), which presumably follows The Letters of T. S. Eliot vol. 1; the spelling of the long word differs in every printing I found. Check against Letters vol. 1 and cite it; failing that, follow Paige, which is the edition the site names.

## Round 2

### Scholar

OBJECT on two clauses; the rest ACCEPT.
1. "once he had the typescript, struck out everything but the last ten lines". Ellmann, who had the facsimile open, describes something different: Pound worked through the voyage line by line, "was willing to spare only" a few scraps of it ("with a light fair breeze / We beat around the cape from the Dry Salvages. / A porpoise snored on the swell."), and "It became clear that the whole passage might as well go". The result was the same, but Pound's pencil did not simply strike everything above the Phlebas lines. Fix: "once he had the typescript, cut so much of the voyage that the whole of it went; the ten lines on Phlebas survived."
2. "the ship drives onto a wall of ice". The draft's words are "A wall, a barrier, towards which we drove" (source 6); the collision is not narrated, only the silence after it. "Ice" is everyone's reading of the white line, the bears and the crew's joke about cracked ice, and the Eliot Foundation page says "after hitting ice", so "ice" may stand. Fix: "and the ship drives at a wall of ice and is lost."
3. The Pound quotation may now be given in full (answer 7); the truncation is acceptable if the editor prefers it.

### Reader

OBJECT (two places). The rest reads well; "Pound wrote "Bad" on the manuscript and, once he had the typescript, struck out everything but the last ten lines" clears up the muddle I complained of in round 1, and the voyage now has verbs.

1. "to the banks": W2 says "the fishing banks". A reader who does not know the Grand Banks needs the word here too, since this card may be the first one opened.
2. "He was needed, Pound went on, absolutely where he was. "Must stay in."" On the editor's question: no, it does not read naturally. "Pound replied" and "Pound went on" fall within two sentences, and the two-word quotation left standing alone after a sentence of reported speech looks like our fragment rather than his. A reader senses that something is being stepped round and does not know what. Suggested, keeping the same division between quotation and paraphrase: "Phlebas, he went on, was needed absolutely where he was: "Must stay in."" One sentence, the name not repeated, and the quotation attached to the clause it concludes.

The last three sentences would then read: Eliot asked, "Perhaps better omit Phlebas also???" Pound replied: "I DO advise keeping Phlebas. In fact I more'n advise. Phlebas is an integral part of the poem; the card pack introduces him, the drowned phoen. sailor." Phlebas, he went on, was needed absolutely where he was: "Must stay in."

# What was not settled, and what the site should know

1. **The length of the cut voyage.** Perloff (1981) gives 92 lines for the typescript of Part IV (82 cut), Martz (1993) 82 lines before the Phlebas lyric, most web summaries 93; the 1971 facsimile could not be opened from here, and the difference is probably how a broken line is counted. Every note now says "some ninety lines". Whoever next has the facsimile open can put a figure in.
2. **Pound's "ABSOlootly".** The site now prints the sentence as the facsimile's editorial notes give it (p. 129, in a transcription the scholar found, agreeing with Paige's 1950 text on words and punctuation: "the drowned phoen. sailor. And he is needed ABSOlootly where he is. Must stay in."). The capitalisation of the odd word rests on that transcription; Perloff normalises it to ABSOLOOTLY, Martz prints ABSoloootly, the site had ABSOloootly. The Letters of T. S. Eliot vol. 1 would settle it.
3. **Pound's "??doubtful" on "Dirge".** The scholar remembers it from the facsimile and could not verify it from anything reachable. It is out of the notes. If someone confirms it, the drafts record can take it back in one clause.
4. **The opening quatrains of the draft.** Perloff confirms rhymed quatrains (abab) about a sailor on shore leave; the scholar's memory says three; no count is printed.
5. **"Cornouaille".** Eliot's French has "la houle de Cornouaille", which without the s is strictly the Cornouaille of Brittany; "Cornouailles" is Cornwall. The tin cargo points to Cornwall, and every translation gives Cornwall. The card keeps "Cornwall"; the parenthesis proposed for the library record says why.
6. **"Written in 1917".** Schuchard dates all four French poems to about April 1917 and calls the manuscripts undated; other sources say 1916–17; the site's former "written in 1918" was the year of publication (the Little Review, September 1918). The scholar ruled for "written in 1917". A cautious editor might add "(the manuscript is undated)".
7. **The gravestone comparison (W6).** Musurillo (Classical Philology, 1956) compared the section to the Greek Anthology's epitaphs for drowned sailors, which speak to the passer-by. The English churchyard formula "as you are now, so once was I" is the reader's comparison, allowed by the scholar as an observation about the line's shape, not as a source; no scholar was found applying it to line 321 by name.
8. **Whether the site quotes Pound's letters at all** was not raised by either side: the rights rule covers Eliot's later texts, and Pound's 1922 letter has been printed since 1950. Ethan may want a view on it.
