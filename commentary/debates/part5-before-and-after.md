# Part V, before and after

Every item of Part V whose text or title changed in the debate (44 of 51), as it was on the site this morning and as it is now. The reasons are in part5.md, under the same handles.

## gloss:torchlight: The Passion

Title before: The Passion, in nine lines, without the resurrection

Title after: The Passion

**Before**

Gethsemane by torchlight (the arrest, John 18:3), "the frosty silence in the gardens," "the agony in stony places," the shouting of the crowd, "Prison and palace" (Caiaphas's house, Pilate's), the thunder and earthquake at the crucifixion. It is Good Friday, told in the past tense by people who were there. "He who was living is now dead / We who were living are now dying / With a little patience." No Easter follows. Eliot's headnote to Part V names three themes: "the journey to Emmaus, the approach to the Chapel Perilous (see Miss Weston's book) and the present decay of eastern Europe." He wrote the section at Lausanne in a few weeks, and, unlike everything before it, Pound barely touched it: his note at its head reads "OK from here on I think." Eliot told Bertrand Russell in October 1923 that Part V was "not only the best part, but the only part that justifies the whole, at all."

**After**

Lines 322 to 330 look back on the arrest, trial and death of Christ without naming him. The torchlight is the arrest in the garden at night, when Judas comes "with lanterns and torches and weapons" (John 18:3). The gardens are Gethsemane, where he prayed "being in an agony" (Luke 22:44). John says of that night, when Peter stood warming himself at a fire in the high priest's courtyard, that "it was cold" (18:18). "The shouting and the crying" is the crowd; "Prison and palace" has been read as the high priest's house and Pilate's. No Gospel has thunder at the crucifixion (Matthew has an earthquake, 27:51); the "thunder of spring over distant mountains" is the poem's own. The lines stop at the death: "He who was living is now dead." Eliot's headnote to Part V names three themes for what follows: "the journey to Emmaus, the approach to the Chapel Perilous (see Miss Weston's book) and the present decay of eastern Europe." He wrote the section at Lausanne between late November 1921 and the first days of 1922. Pound, who had cut heavily in the earlier parts, wrote at the head of the typescript "OK from here on I think."

## gloss:no-water: The road through the mountains

**Before**

Twenty-nine lines with almost no punctuation and no full stops, driven by the two words "rock" and "water" as if the speaker could think of nothing else, because he can't. It is the road to Emmaus, the approach to the Grail chapel through a waste land, the retreat through the Carpathians of a beaten army, and a man's own dry mouth: "Dead mountain mouth of carious teeth that cannot spit." The hymn of "If there were water" that follows (lines 346–358) is the poem's only stretch of pure longing, and it is built on a sound the speaker cannot hear.

Eliot's note on the hermit-thrush is the most personal in the apparatus: <i>Turdus aonalaschkae pallasii</i>, "which I have heard in Quebec County," and Chapman's <i>Handbook of Birds of Eastern North America</i> on its "purity and sweetness of tone." Its "water-dripping song" is "justly celebrated": "Drip drop drip drop drop drop drop / But there is no water." The thrush is also Whitman's, from the lilac poem behind line 2, the bird that sings the carol of death in the swamp. Listen to a recording and the poem's onomatopoeia turns out to be exact.

**After**

From line 331 to line 359 there is no punctuation, and the lines keep returning to two words, "rock" and "water." Eliot's headnote gives the first part of the section three themes: the journey to Emmaus, the approach to the Chapel Perilous, and "the present decay of eastern Europe." The road through the mountains can be read as any of them. Lines 346 to 358 imagine the water that is not there, and end with the sound of a bird.

Eliot's note on line 357 names the bird, the hermit-thrush, "which I have heard in Quebec County," and quotes Chapman's <i>Handbook of Birds of Eastern North America</i> (1895) on the "purity and sweetness of tone" of its song. His copy of Chapman was a present from his mother for his fourteenth birthday, in 1902. The note calls the bird's "water-dripping song" justly celebrated; the phrase is not Chapman's, and no source for it is agreed; ornithologists describe the song as a series of flute-like phrases. Commentators also point to the hermit-thrush of Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd," the elegy often cited for the lilacs of line 2; Eliot said in 1928 that he had not read Whitman until late in life. Recordings of the bird are in the listening room.

## gloss:third: Emmaus, and the men on South Georgia

**Before**

Luke 24: two disciples walking to Emmaus on the third day are joined by a stranger who explains the scriptures to them and whom they recognise only when he breaks bread, at which point he vanishes. "Their eyes were holden that they should not know him." Eliot's note adds the second source, from memory ("I forget which, but I think one of Shackleton's"): the crossing of South Georgia in May 1916 by Shackleton, Worsley and Crean at the end of the <i>Endurance</i> expedition, told in Shackleton's <i>South</i> (1919), where each of the three men privately felt a fourth walking with them. The hooded figure, "I do not know whether a man or a woman," is the Hanged Man that Madame Sosostris could not find, and also Tiresias, and also nobody: "When I count, there are only you and I together." The question is asked and, as in the Grail castle, not answered.

**After**

In Luke 24, on the day of the resurrection, two disciples walking to Emmaus are joined by a stranger, "but their eyes were holden that they should not know him"; he expounds the scriptures to them, breaks bread at supper, and is gone: "he vanished out of their sight." Eliot's note gives the second source from memory ("I forget which, but I think one of Shackleton's"): the crossing of South Georgia by Shackleton, Worsley and Crean on 19 and 20 May 1916, at the end of the <i>Endurance</i> expedition, told in Shackleton's <i>South</i> (1919). Each of the three men felt that a fourth was walking with them, and said nothing of it until afterwards. Eliot's note on the Tarot pack at line 46 says he associates the Hanged Man "with the hooded figure in the passage of the disciples to Emmaus in Part V"; that is the card Madame Sosostris could not find. Some readers also hear Tiresias in "I do not know whether a man or a woman"; Eliot's notes do not say so.

## gloss:hordes: Eastern Europe, 1920

**Before**

Hermann Hesse's <i>Blick ins Chaos</i> (1920; <i>In Sight of Chaos</i> in the English translation of 1923), three essays, two of them on Dostoevsky, quoted in Eliot's note. Eliot admired the book enough to visit Hesse at Montagnola in May 1922 and to print him in the first number of <i>The Criterion</i> that October, alongside the poem. The "hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" are the refugees and armies of the collapse of the Russian and Austro-Hungarian empires; the "maternal lamentation" is the women at the cross ("Daughters of Jerusalem, weep not for me," Luke 23:28) and every mother in Europe in 1919. Then the roll-call: "Jerusalem Athens Alexandria / Vienna London," the cities that were each in turn the centre of the world, each "Unreal," the word from line 60 now standing on its own with no city after it. London is the last name on the list.

**After**

Eliot's note on these lines quotes Hermann Hesse's <i>Blick ins Chaos</i> (Bern, 1920), three essays, two of them on Dostoevsky; the passage is the close of the first, "The Brothers Karamazov, or the Downfall of Europe." Eliot wrote to Hesse in March 1922 to praise the book, visited him at Montagnola on 28 May 1922, and printed him in the first number of <i>The Criterion</i>, October 1922, beside <i>The Waste Land</i>. The headnote calls this theme "the present decay of eastern Europe," and the "hooded hordes swarming / Over endless plains" are usually taken to refer to it. "Maternal lamentation" has been connected with the women who "bewailed and lamented" Christ on the way to the cross (Luke 23:27–28). Lines 375 to 377 name five cities and then "Unreal," the word of "Unreal City" at lines 60 and 207, this time with no city after it.

## gloss:bats: From an earlier fragment

Title before: A nightmare from an older notebook

Title after: From an earlier fragment

**Before**

These eight lines predate the rest of the poem. They come from a fragment in Eliot's papers that begins "So through the evening, through the violet air," printed in the facsimile edition, and the images are Gothic and painterly: the woman fiddling on her own hair, the bats with baby faces, the towers upside down in air, bells that "kept the hours" like St Mary Woolnoth's, and voices "singing out of empty cisterns and exhausted wells," which is Jeremiah's "broken cisterns, that can hold no water." The violet hour of Part III has become "violet light." The poem's phrase for the whole passage would be the one it used of the pictures beside Philomel's: "withered stumps of time," pictures on the wall of the mind.

**After**

These lines rework an earlier fragment among Eliot's papers, beginning "So through the evening, through the violet air," which is printed in the 1971 facsimile of the drafts. The woman fiddling on her hair, the bats with baby faces, the towers upside down, the bells and the voices from cisterns and wells are already there. The bells that "kept the hours" repeat line 67, where Saint Mary Woolnoth kept them; the "violet hour" of lines 215 and 220 becomes "violet light." Commentators hear Jeremiah 2:13, "broken cisterns, that can hold no water," in "empty cisterns and exhausted wells"; Eliot's notes do not mention it.

## gloss:chapel: The Chapel Perilous

**Before**

Weston's chapter "The Perilous Chapel" collects the episode in which the questing knight must spend a night in a chapel in a waste place where a dead body lies on the altar, a black hand puts out the candle, and voices and storms assail him; survive it, and he is fit for the Grail. Eliot's chapel is worse than perilous: it is empty, windowless, the door swinging, "Dry bones can harm no one," the test already over. Then a cock on the rooftree crows "Co co rico," in French, the cock that crowed for Peter's denial and the "bird of dawning" that sends ghosts back to their graves in the first scene of <i>Hamlet</i>. Lightning, "a damp gust / Bringing rain." Three words. It is the first rain in the poem since the shower over the Starnbergersee at line 9.

**After**

Weston's chapter "The Perilous Chapel" collects the versions of an adventure in which the knight, sheltering in a chapel in a waste place, finds a dead body on the altar, or sees a black hand put out the candle and hears "strange and threatening voices"; she reads it as a test of initiation on the way to the Grail. In the poem the chapel is empty, without windows, its door swinging: "Dry bones can harm no one." "Co co rico" is the French spelling of a cock-crow. Commentators compare the cock that crowed at Peter's denial (Matthew 26:74) and the cock of <i>Hamlet</i> I.i, "the trumpet to the morn," at whose crowing the ghost fades. The "damp gust / Bringing rain" is the first rain in the poem since the shower of line 9. The poem does not say that it falls; at line 397 the leaves are still waiting for it.

## gloss:ganga: The river and the mountain

**Before**

Ganga is the Ganges; Himavant, "the snowy," is the Himalaya, the mountain father of the goddess Ganga in the Puranas. The scene moves in a line from the chapel in the mountains to the Indian monsoon, the whole subcontinent crouched "humped in silence" waiting for the rains, and then the thunder speaks, in the syllable of a language Eliot had studied at Harvard for two years under Charles Lanman and never pretended to command. In <i>After Strange Gods</i> (1934) he said the Sanskrit and the year of Patañjali's metaphysics had left him "in a state of enlightened mystification."

**After**

Ganga is the Ganges; Himavant, "the snowy," is the Himalaya, in the Ramayana and the Puranas the mountain who is the goddess Ganga's father. The scene moves from the chapel in the mountains to India before the monsoon breaks. Then the thunder speaks, in Sanskrit, which Eliot studied for two years at Harvard, 1911 to 1913, under Charles Lanman. In <i>After Strange Gods</i> (1934) he wrote that those years, and a year of Patañjali's metaphysics under James Woods, "left me in a state of enlightened mystification."

## gloss:da: What the thunder said

**Before**

<i>Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad</i> 5.2, paraphrased here from the Sanskrit; Eliot's note gives Deussen's German translation as his source. The thunder says one syllable and each hearer understands the instruction he most needs. Eliot changes the order to Datta, Dayadhvam, Damyata (give, sympathise, control), and each word is followed by a confession in the poem's own voice of how the instruction has been failed. The section's title, and the poem's ending, come from a fable about hearing what you are able to hear.

**After**

<i>Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad</i> V.2, given above in paraphrase; Eliot's note numbers it "5, 1." Gods, men and demons ask their father Prajāpati for teaching. To each he says "Da," and each hears a different word in it: the gods <i>dāmyata</i>, restrain yourselves; the men <i>datta</i>, give; the demons <i>dayadhvam</i>, be compassionate. "The divine voice of thunder repeats the same, Da Da Da" (Max Müller's translation, 1884). Eliot's note glosses the three as "Give, sympathise, control" and points to Deussen's German translation of 1897. The poem takes the words in the order <i>datta, dayadhvam, damyata</i> and follows each with a passage in its own voice; in the usual reading each passage admits that the command was not kept.

## gloss:datta: Give

**Before**

The one thing given was "The awful daring of a moment's surrender / Which an age of prudence can never retract," and by that alone "we have existed." It will not appear in the obituaries, nor in "memories draped by the beneficent spider" (Flamineo in Webster's <i>White Devil</i>, Act V scene vi, on how quickly widows remarry, quoted in the note), nor in the will read out by "the lean solicitor" after the seals are broken. This is the poem's one statement about what a life is for, and it is made in the vocabulary of probate.

**After**

The one thing given is "The awful daring of a moment's surrender / Which an age of prudence can never retract," and "by this, and this only, we have existed." It is not in the obituaries, nor in "memories draped by the beneficent spider," nor "under seals broken by the lean solicitor," the will opened after a death. For the spider Eliot's note quotes Flamineo in Webster's <i>The White Devil</i>, Act V scene vi, on widows who remarry "ere the spider / Make a thin curtain for your epitaphs."

## gloss:dayadhvam: Sympathise: Ugolino and Bradley

Title before: Sympathise: Ugolino's tower, and Bradley's circle

Title after: Sympathise: Ugolino and Bradley

**Before**

<i>Inferno</i> XXXIII: Count Ugolino, locked in a tower at Pisa with his sons and grandsons to starve, hears the door nailed shut. Eliot's note adds a page of F. H. Bradley's <i>Appearance and Reality</i> (1893), the philosopher on whom he wrote his Harvard doctoral thesis: "My external sensations are no less private to myself than are my thoughts or my feelings . . . the whole world for each is peculiar and private to that soul." Each of us in a prison, thinking of a key, and the thinking of the key is what confirms the prison. "A broken Coriolanus," Shakespeare's general who could not bend, is revived "for a moment" by "aetherial rumours" at nightfall, and only for a moment. Sympathy is the instruction; solitude is the report.

**After**

<i>Inferno</i> XXXIII, 46 to 48: Count Ugolino, shut in a tower at Pisa with his sons and grandsons to starve, hears the door below "chiavar," which can mean nailed up or locked; Eliot's "key" takes the second sense. Eliot's note adds a page of <i>Appearance and Reality</i> (1893) by F. H. Bradley, on whom Eliot wrote his Harvard dissertation: "My external sensations are no less private to myself than are my thoughts or my feelings . . . the whole world for each is peculiar and private to that soul." Eliot's note sets Bradley's closed circle beside the "prison" of lines 414 and 415, where the key is thought of, not turned. Coriolanus is Shakespeare's Roman general, banished from Rome for his contempt of its people; he leads its enemies against it, yields at last to his mother's plea to spare it, and is killed for yielding. "A broken Coriolanus" is revived here only "for a moment," by "aetherial rumours," at nightfall.

## gloss:damyata: Control

Title before: Control: a boat off Cape Ann

Title after: Control

**Before**

The third instruction gets the poem's only memory of things going well: a sailing boat answering "Gaily, to the hand expert with sail and oar," the sea calm. Eliot had sailed off Gloucester and Cape Ann as a boy, and the Dry Salvages of his later poem lie off that coast. Then the grammar turns conditional: "your heart would have responded / Gaily, when invited." Would have. The most intimate address in the poem, to a "you" who is never named, is a sentence about what did not happen.

**After**

The third word is answered by a boat under control: "The boat responded / Gaily, to the hand expert with sail and oar," on a calm sea. Eliot sailed off Gloucester and Cape Ann, Massachusetts, in the summers of his youth, and the boat is generally taken as a memory of it; the Dry Salvages of his later poem lie off that coast. The sentence that follows is conditional, "your heart would have responded / Gaily, when invited," and the "you" is not named.

## gloss:fisher-king: The Fisher King, and Hezekiah

**Before**

Eliot's note sends you to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King, the wounded keeper of the Grail who fishes because he can do nothing else, while his land lies waste behind him. "Shall I at least set my lands in order?" is Isaiah 38:1, the prophet's message to the dying king Hezekiah, who turns his face to the wall and weeps and is given fifteen more years. It is also what a man does with his papers. From here to the end the poem stops narrating and starts stacking.

**After**

Eliot's note points to Weston's chapter on the Fisher King, the wounded keeper of the Grail whose land lies waste with him. Weston argues that his title comes from the fish as an ancient symbol of life, and dismisses the romances' own explanation, that the king fishes for pastime because his wound keeps him from riding, as a later invention. "Shall I at least set my lands in order?" echoes Isaiah 38:1, the prophet's message to the dying king Hezekiah: "Set thine house in order: for thou shalt die, and not live." Hezekiah turned his face to the wall and wept, and was given fifteen more years. Eliot's notes do not mention Isaiah; the poem says "lands," not "house," and the question gets no answer.

## gloss:fragments: The last eight lines

Title before: The last eight lines, one by one

Title after: The last eight lines

**Before**

<b>London Bridge is falling down falling down falling down.</b> The nursery rhyme, whose verses try one material after another (wood and clay, bricks and mortar, iron and steel, silver and gold) and whose oldest versions end with setting a watchman, or, in the folklorists' reading that Frazer would have relished, burying someone in the foundations. The bridge the commuters crossed at line 62.

<b>Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina.</b> "Then he hid himself in the fire that refines them." <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, 148: Arnaut Daniel, having spoken to Dante in his own Provençal (the <i>Comedy</i>'s one passage in a vernacular other than Italian), steps back into the purifying flame. He goes willingly. Eliot's note quotes the end of Arnaut's speech, from "Ara vos prec," the words Eliot had taken for the title of his 1920 collection. Set this fire against the burning of the Fire Sermon.

<b>Quando fiam ceu chelidon.</b> "When shall I become like the swallow?" From the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i>, an anonymous Latin poem of the second to fourth century celebrating the eve of Venus's spring festival, whose last stanza suddenly turns on the poet: the nightingale sings, and he, silent, asks when his own spring will come, "when shall I become like the swallow, so that I may stop being silent?" The 1922 printings have "ceu"; the Latin poem has "uti," and Eliot's later editions restored it. "O swallow swallow" is Tennyson's, from <i>The Princess</i>: "O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South." Philomel's sister, still singing.

<b>Le Prince d'Aquitaine à la tour abolie.</b> The second line of Gérard de Nerval's sonnet "El Desdichado" (1854): "I am the dark one, the widower, the unconsoled, the Prince of Aquitaine with the ruined tower." The Tower is a Tarot card (XVI, struck by lightning), and Nerval's speaker, who has "twice crossed the Acheron," is another man who has been among the dead.

<b>These fragments I have shored against my ruins.</b> The poem's own line, its method confessed in a single sentence: quotations propped like timbers against a wall that is coming down.

<b>Why then Ile fit you. Hieronymo's mad againe.</b> Kyd's <i>The Spanish Tragedy</i> (c. 1587), Act IV. Hieronimo, whose son has been murdered, is asked to write an entertainment for the court and answers "Why then, I'll fit you," meaning both "I'll oblige" and "I'll pay you out"; the play he writes is in four languages, and the murderers, acting in it, are killed for real. "Hieronimo is mad again" was the subtitle on the 1615 title page. A poem in seven languages, written as revenge.

<b>Datta. Dayadhvam. Damyata.</b> The thunder's three words, now without their glosses.

<b>Shantih shantih shantih.</b> The formal ending of an Upanishad, as Eliot's note says, "the Peace which passeth understanding" (Philippians 4:7), for which the 1922 note called the English "a feeble translation" and later notes "our equivalent." Whether the last line is a benediction or a man saying "peace, peace" to himself until he can sleep, the poem does not decide, and neither should you on a first reading.

**After**

<b>London Bridge is falling down falling down falling down.</b> The nursery rhyme, first printed about 1744. Its verses try one material after another (wood and clay, bricks and mortar, iron and steel, silver and gold), and the oldest versions end by setting a man to watch all night. The folklorist Alice Gomme, in 1894, connected the rhyme with the old custom of burying a victim under a bridge's foundations. The crowd of line 62 crossed this bridge.

<b>Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina.</b> "Then he hid himself in the fire that refines them." <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, 148. Arnaut Daniel, having spoken to Dante in his own Provençal (the <i>Comedy</i>'s one passage in a vernacular other than Italian), steps back into the flame that purifies the lustful. He goes willingly. Eliot's note quotes the end of Arnaut's speech, from "Ara vos prec," the words Eliot took for the title of his 1920 collection.

<b>Quando fiam ceu chelidon.</b> "When shall I become like the swallow?" From the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i>, an anonymous Latin poem of the second to fourth century for the eve of Venus's spring festival. Its last stanza turns from the nightingale to the poet's own silence: "she sings; we are silent. When does my spring come? When shall I become like the swallow, so that I may stop being silent?" Every printing from 1922 to 1932, and Eliot's fair copies, read "ceu"; the standard Latin text has "uti," and Eliot's line was changed to "uti" in <i>Collected Poems 1909–1935</i> (1936). Lawrence Rainey, in his annotated edition of the poem, doubts that the change was Eliot's. "O swallow swallow" is Tennyson's, from <i>The Princess</i> ("O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South"); commentators also cite Swinburne's "Itylus" ("Swallow, my sister, O sister swallow"). The swallow is Procne, sister of the Philomela of lines 99 and 203.

<b>Le Prince d'Aquitaine à la tour abolie.</b> The second line of Gérard de Nerval's sonnet "El Desdichado" (1854): "I am the dark one, the widower, the unconsoled, the Prince of Aquitaine with the ruined tower." Some commentators connect the tower with the Tarot's Tower, struck by lightning; Nerval does not say so. Later in the sonnet the speaker says he has twice crossed Acheron, the river of the dead.

<b>These fragments I have shored against my ruins.</b> The poem's own line among the quotations. To shore is to prop up, as a builder shores a wall with timbers.

<b>Why then Ile fit you. Hieronymo's mad againe.</b> Kyd's <i>The Spanish Tragedy</i> (c. 1587), Act IV scene i. Hieronimo, whose son has been murdered, is asked by the murderers to write an entertainment for the court and answers "Why then, I'll fit you," meaning both "I'll oblige" and "I'll pay you out." The play he gives them is acted in four languages, Latin, Greek, Italian and French, and in it the murderers are stabbed in earnest. "Hieronimo is mad againe" is the subtitle on the title page of the 1615 quarto. Hieronimo's play in four languages has been compared with the poem, whose text has six (English, German, French, Italian, Latin and Sanskrit), with Greek in the epigraph and Provençal in the notes.

<b>Datta. Dayadhvam. Damyata.</b> The thunder's three words, repeated without the passages that answered them.

<b>Shantih shantih shantih.</b> Eliot's note: "Repeated as here, a formal ending to an Upanishad." "The Peace which passeth understanding" is Philippians 4:7 in the words of the Anglican blessing; the 1922 note called it "a feeble translation of the content of this word," and from <i>Poems 1909–1925</i> (1925) the note reads "our equivalent to this word."

Title after: The fable in the Upanishad

**Before**

Title before: Peace, or the word for it

**Before**

"Shantih shantih shantih." A benediction, or a man saying "peace" until he sleeps? Eliot's 1922 note called the English "a feeble translation." Read the last line once as a blessing and once as a lullaby, and keep the one that lets you close the book.

## draft:ok-from-here: "OK from here on I think"

**Before**

At the head of Part V Pound wrote "OK from here on I think," and cut almost nothing. Eliot wrote the section at Lausanne in November and December 1921, fast, and later said it had come "in a trance." Three of the poem's passages are older than the rest: the woman with the long black hair and the bats with baby faces (lines 378–385) come from a fragment beginning "So through the evening, through the violet air"; the "red rock" of Part I came from "The Death of Saint Narcissus" (1915); and Phlebas from "Dans le Restaurant" (1918).

**After**

At the head of the typescript of Part V Pound wrote "OK from here on I think," and cut almost nothing; he had cut heavily in the earlier parts. Eliot wrote the section at Lausanne between 22 November 1921 and 2 January 1922, while under treatment by Dr Roger Vittoz. In an essay on Pascal in 1931 he wrote that in some forms of illness "long passages may be produced which require little or no retouch," a sentence readers have applied to Part V, though Eliot did not. Several passages of the poem are older than 1921. Lines 322 to 330 draw on a fragment beginning "After the turning of the inspired days," and lines 378 to 385 on another beginning "So through the evening, through the violet air," both printed in the 1971 facsimile; the "red rock" of Part I came from "The Death of Saint Narcissus" (1915), and Phlebas from "Dans le Restaurant" (1918).

## draft:manuscript-fate: What happened to the papers

**Before**

Eliot gave the typescript, with Pound's and Vivien's pencil on it, to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector who had handled his American publishing, in 1922; Quinn insisted on paying $140 for it. After Quinn's death in 1924 it vanished. His niece sold it to the New York Public Library in 1958, and the library announced the acquisition in 1968, three years after Eliot's death. Valerie Eliot's facsimile edition of 1971 printed every page. The papers are in the Berg Collection; the cuts, and Pound's pencil, are the reason the poem is 434 lines long and not 800.

**After**

In October 1922 Eliot gave the drafts, with Pound's pencil and his wife Vivien's on them, to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector who had negotiated his American contracts; Quinn accepted them on condition that he could buy the notebook of Eliot's early poems, for which he paid $140. After Quinn's death in 1924 the papers passed to his sister and then to her daughter, Mary Conroy, and were thought lost. She found them in storage in the early 1950s and sold them to the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library in 1958; the library announced the acquisition in October 1968, nearly four years after Eliot's death. Valerie Eliot's facsimile edition of 1971 printed every page. Eliot himself said in 1946 that the poem had left Pound's hands "reduced to about half its size."
