## dante-purg5
Object: "In Ante-Purgatory, among those who died by violence and repented at the last, a woman speaks seven lines: her name, her birthplace, the place of her death and, without naming him, the husband who put a ring on her and, the line implies, had her killed." The colon-list with two interruptions; a reader loses the thread, and "the line implies" states the commentators' reading as the line's. Put: "In Ante-Purgatory, among those who died by violence and repented at the last, a woman speaks seven lines. She names herself, says that Siena made her and the Maremma unmade her, and says that the husband who put his ring on her knows how she died; she does not name him, and the words are taken to mean that he had her killed." And "his first Thames-daughter has the shape of Pia's sentence, birthplace and undoing, and gives no name" is cryptic; put: "his first Thames-daughter says where she was born and where she was undone in the shape of Pia's sentence, and gives no name."

## gotterdammerung
Object: "Their wordless refrain, as the vocal scores and librettos print it, is the one Eliot's Thames-daughters sing at 277–278 and 290–291". "As the vocal scores and librettos print it" is a hedge a reader cannot use: as opposed to what? Put: "Their refrain is sung syllables without meaning, and in the two-line form the librettos give it is the one Eliot's Thames-daughters sing at 277–278 and 290–291." If the scholar needs the hedge kept, say the thing plainly: that Wagner's printed poem has no refrain and the two-line form is the librettos'. Also "singing to the sun for the gold" reads as "in exchange for"; put "singing to the sun of the gold".

## ovid-met6
Object: "Tereus, king of Thrace, bringing his wife Procne's sister Philomela from Athens to visit her, rapes her in a hut deep in the woods and cuts out her tongue; she weaves the story into a cloth and sends it to her sister; Procne kills her son Itys and serves him to his father; Tereus pursues the sisters with a sword, and all three are turned into birds (VI.424–674)." Four clauses on semicolons, the stacked-clause tell, and I asked in round 1 for it to be broken. Put: "Tereus, king of Thrace, brings his wife Procne's sister Philomela from Athens to visit her, rapes her in a hut deep in the woods, and cuts out her tongue. She weaves the story into a cloth and sends it to her sister. Procne kills her son Itys and serves him to his father. The last sentence is three clauses on semicolons too; put: "The story is painted on the wall in Part II (99–103), and the bird's cry, "jug jug" and "Tereu" in the Elizabethan spelling, breaks in at 203–206 round "So rudely forc'd" from line 100. Eliot's note to the swallow at 429 refers back to "Philomela in Parts II and III"."

## ovid-met3
Object: "<i>Metamorphoses</i> III.324–331; Eliot's note quotes III.320–338 and calls the passage "of great anthropological interest"." A bare citation for a first sentence. And "He sides with Jupiter; Juno blinds him; Jupiter, who cannot undo what a god has done, gives him knowledge of the future." Three clauses on semicolons in the historic present, the brisk tell. Put: "The passage is <i>Metamorphoses</i> III.324–331, the middle of the longer stretch (III.320–338) that Eliot's note quotes and calls "of great anthropological interest". Jupiter and Juno, disputing which sex has the greater pleasure in love, put the question to Tiresias, who had been both: he had struck two coupling snakes with his staff and lived seven years as a woman, then struck them again and become a man. He sided with Jupiter. Juno blinded him for it, and Jupiter, who could not undo what another god had done, gave him knowledge of the future instead. Eliot's note calls him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest"." ("Calls him", not "makes him": that is what a note does.)

## marvell-coy-mistress
Object: "Eliot twice takes "But at my back I always hear" (185, 196) and each time gives it a new object: the rattle of the bones, the horns and motors." He does not take the line whole (his lines drop "always"), and "object" will be read as purpose, not grammar. ## day-parliament-of-bees
Object: "describes the palace he will build, with a roof of forests where, at a sudden "noise of horns and hunting", Actaeon will come upon Diana bathing". "A roof of forests" is baffling without a word of explanation, and the palace is not what the reader needs. Put: "describes the palace he will build, where, at a sudden "noise of horns and hunting", Actaeon will come upon Diana bathing and be torn by his own hounds (Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> III)." And "The only edition is the quarto of 1641; an edition of 1607 was reported by early cataloguers but has never been found, so when Day wrote it is not known." The phantom 1607 edition is more than a library note needs; the date field already says the date of writing is unknown. Put: "The only edition is the quarto of 1641; when Day wrote it is not known." The rest, including the two senses of "spring", is clear and I accept it.

## lyly-campaspe
Object: "Eliot's "'Jug Jug' to dirty ears" (103) and the cry at 203–206 use the convention's words". No convention has been named in this note (the scholar's opening sentence, which named it, was dropped), so "the convention's words" points at nothing. Put: "Eliot's "'Jug Jug' to dirty ears" (103) and the cry at 203–206 take the song's "jug jug" and "tereu"; "So rudely forc'd" (205) repeats line 100." And "The songs in Lyly's plays are not in the quartos and were first printed in Blount's <i>Sixe Court Comedies</i> of 1632, long after his death": "the quartos" means nothing to a reader, and the publisher and title are more than the note needs. Put: "The songs in Lyly's plays are missing from the early printings and first appeared in a collected edition of 1632, long after his death; whether he wrote them has been disputed since W. W. Greg questioned it in 1905." (If the scholar wants the 1632 title kept for checking, put it in brackets after "1632".)

## sappho-104
Object: "Two lines pieced together by editors from two quotations: Demetrius, <i>On Style</i> 141, gives the address to the Evening Star with its three "you bring" as an example of graceful repetition, and the Byzantine lexicon <i>Etymologicum Magnum</i> preserves the words "all that shining Dawn scattered"." The note now opens with the textual history and never says in plain words what Sappho says; a reader wants that first, and the sentence is too long to carry both. Put: "Sappho's address to the evening star, which brings back all that the dawn scattered. The two lines survive only as quotations, and editors have joined them: Demetrius, <i>On Style</i> 141, quotes the star and its three "you bring" as an example of graceful repetition, and a Byzantine dictionary, the <i>Etymologicum Magnum</i>, preserves "all that shining Dawn scattered"." The Byron sentence does not help with line 221 (nothing in it is echoed in Eliot's words); cut it, or keep it only if the scholar can tie it to "home". The last sentence joins Eliot's note and Stevenson on a semicolon; put a full stop after "nightfall" and begin: ""Brings the sailor home from sea" also echoes Stevenson's "Requiem", "Home is the sailor, home from sea"."

## buddha-fire-sermon
Object: "Preached, in the Mahāvagga's narrative, on Gayā Head to a thousand monks who had been matted-hair ascetics and tenders of the sacred fire, soon after the enlightenment: every sense and its objects are on fire with passion, hatred and infatuation." A fragment with three interruptions and a colon, and "the Mahāvagga's narrative" is unexplained. Put: "According to the Mahāvagga, the Pali text Warren translates it from, the Buddha preached it soon after his enlightenment, on Gayā Head, to a thousand monks who had been matted-hair ascetics and tenders of the sacred fire. It says that every sense and everything it meets is on fire, with passion, hatred and infatuation." The second sentence is too long and the bracket too heavy; put: "Eliot's note names Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (Harvard Oriental Series, 1896), where the sermon is §73, pp. 351–353. It was the standard English anthology of the Pali texts when Eliot studied Sanskrit and Indian philosophy at Harvard in 1911–13, and it is generally assumed he met the sermon there." In the last sentence "it gives Part III its title" has an ambiguous "it" (the note or the sermon); put: "The sermon gives Part III its title and, at 308 and 311, its "burning"; Eliot's note says it "corresponds in importance to the Sermon on the Mount"."
