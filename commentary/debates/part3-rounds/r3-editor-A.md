# Round 3, editor's final texts, batch A (glosses sweet-thames to tiresias)

How these were made: the round-2 texts with the scholar's two corrections (fishing-canal, eugenides) and the reader's nine rewordings, all taken. Where the editor altered the reader's wording for accuracy the alteration is named under Decisions. Fixed fields untouched.

## sweet-thames
Decisions: the reader's last two sentences taken; "repeats the refrain at 183–184" made exact (183 repeats it, 184 varies it, as the Spenser way says).
title: Spenser's wedding river
body:
The refrain of Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596), a "spousal verse" for a double wedding, in which the poet walks by the Thames and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans that stand for the brides. Eliot repeats the refrain at 183 and varies it at 184. His nymphs and "their friends, the loitering heirs of city directors" are usually taken as the girls and young men of the summer, gone with the season, and the litter of summer nights with them.

## leman
Decisions: the reader's cut of the doctor's name taken (it stays on the Eliot pathway at 182, where the biography is the point); the sentence broken.
title: Psalm 137 and Lake Geneva
quote: By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept: when we remembered thee, O Sion.
body:
Psalm 137 in the Prayer Book wording, "By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept" (the King James Bible has "rivers"), with Leman for Babylon and "I" for "we". Leman is Lac Léman, Lake Geneva. Eliot was at Lausanne, on its shore, from late November 1921 to early January 1922, on sick leave from Lloyds Bank and under a doctor's care. Parts IV and V were written there. "Leman" is also an old word for a lover or mistress, a second sense critics have noted.

## at-my-back
Decisions: the reader's text taken whole; the numbering checked again against the 1922 pages (196 Day, 197 Marvell).
title: Time's chariot, twice
body:
Marvell, "To His Coy Mistress" (published 1681), in which the lover urges his mistress to yield because time is short and the grave is near. Eliot uses the line twice. Here what is heard "at my back" is "the rattle of the bones, and chuckle spread from ear to ear"; at line 196 it is "the sound of horns and motors". Eliot's note gives the source at the second use, line 196. In the 1922 notes it is numbered 197 and the Day note 196, the wrong way round.

## fishing-canal
Decisions: the scholar's "424–425"; the reader's two rewordings, with the headnote's claim stated as the headnote states it (title and plan, not "source").
title: Ferdinand on the bank, and the Fisher King
quote: Sitting on a bank,<br>Weeping again the king my father’s wreck,<br>This music crept by me upon the waters,<br>Allaying both their fury and my passion<br>With its sweet air.
body:
Ferdinand in <i>The Tempest</i>, Act I scene ii, sitting on the shore and weeping for the father he believes drowned, just before Ariel sings "Full fathom five". Eliot's note gives the source at line 192, and the same speech returns at 257. Eliot adds a brother to Shakespeare's father and puts the fisherman by a canal behind a gashouse in winter. Because Eliot's headnote says the poem's title and plan came from Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, the man fishing is usually taken also as the Fisher King of the Grail legend, whose wound leaves his land barren. He fishes again at 424–425. Eliot himself links the rat and the bones to line 115: his note to "rats' alley" there reads "Cf. Part III, l. 195". The "White bodies naked on the low damp ground" have been read as the war dead.

## mrs-porter
Decisions: the reader's two rewordings taken.
title: Actaeon and Mrs Porter
body:
John Day's <i>The Parliament of Bees</i> (printed 1641), quoted in Eliot's note: a noise of horns and hunting brings Actaeon to Diana bathing. In Ovid the hunter who sees the goddess naked is turned into a stag and torn apart by his own hounds. In Eliot the horns are motor horns, the hunter is Sweeney, the "Apeneck Sweeney" of his poems of 1918–20, and in Diana's place Mrs Porter and her daughter wash their feet in soda water. The three lines of song are from a bawdy ballad sung by Australian troops in the First World War. Its Mrs Porter is said to have kept a brothel in Cairo, and the soldiers' versions were cruder than this. Eliot's note says only that it "was reported to me from Sydney, Australia".

## verlaine
Decisions: accepted by both; unchanged from round 2.
title: The children's voices in the dome
body:
The last line of Verlaine's sonnet "Parsifal" (1886), on Wagner's opera: the knight has resisted the Flower-maidens and Kundry, healed the wounded king Amfortas and adores the Grail, and the sonnet ends on the sound of the boys' choir in the dome of the Grail temple. Eliot's note gives the source. The line follows Mrs Porter and her daughter washing their feet; in Wagner's third act Kundry washes Parsifal's feet before he goes in to the Grail, a parallel critics have often drawn.

## tereu
Decisions: the authorship dispute cut here at the reader's asking (it stays in the library record for Lyly, proposed to the site thread); the reader's last sentence taken.
title: Tereu, the nightingale's cry
body:
The song printed with Lyly's <i>Campaspe</i> (the play 1584, its songs 1632) gives the Elizabethan convention that the nightingale sings "jug jug" and cries "tereu", the vocative of Tereus, the name of the king who raped her and cut out her tongue. The story is Ovid's (<i>Metamorphoses</i> VI) and is told at lines 99–103. Line 205 repeats line 100, "So rudely forced", in an older spelling, and Eliot's own note at line 100 points to this passage ("Cf. Part III, l. 204").

## eugenides
Decisions: the reader's four rewordings and the scholar's Weston sentence, all taken.
title: The one-eyed merchant
body:
Eugenides is a Greek surname, from <i>eugenēs</i>, well-born. Smyrna, now İzmir, was the chief port of Asia Minor and a centre of the dried-fruit trade. The currants in his pocket are presumably samples. "C.i.f. London: documents at sight" is trade shorthand of the kind Eliot handled at Lloyds Bank. The price covers cost, insurance and freight to London, and the shipping documents are handed over when the sight draft is paid. Eliot's note expands it as "carriage and insurance free", which is not the trade usage. French was the trading language of the Levant, and Mr. Eugenides speaks the "demotic", or colloquial, kind. The Cannon Street Hotel formed the front of Cannon Street station in the City and was much used for business meetings. The Metropole is usually taken as the Hotel Metropole on the Brighton seafront, and the weekend as a proposition, often read as a homosexual one.

Eliot's note to line 218 makes him the "one-eyed merchant, seller of currants" of Madame Sosostris's pack, who "melts into the Phoenician Sailor". His one eye is sometimes explained by the playing cards that show a face in profile. Weston (<i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, chapter XII) counts Syrian merchants, with slaves, officials and soldiers, among the carriers of the mystery cults behind the Grail to the western Roman Empire.

## violet-hour
Decisions: the reader wanted the dory tied to Gloucester; the tie is made without calling the dory Gloucester's own boat, which it was not only. The reader's split of the last sentence taken.
title: Sappho's evening star
body:
Sappho's two lines on the evening star, which brings back all that dawn scattered, are behind "the evening hour that strives / Homeward, and brings the sailor home from sea". Eliot's note concedes that his lines "may not appear as exact as Sappho's" and says he had in mind the "longshore" or "dory" fisherman coming in at nightfall. The dory is the small boat of the New England fishing fleets, and Gloucester, Massachusetts, where the Eliots spent their summers, was one of their ports. Stevenson's "Requiem" ("Home is the sailor, home from sea") is also usually cited. The violet hour is dusk, the end of the working day, when "the eyes and back / Turn upward from the desk". The word returns in Part V, in "the violet air" (373) and "the violet light" (380).

## tiresias
Decisions: the reader's first rewording taken. For the second, the reader asked what Tiresias was to make up his mind about. Round 3: the scholar placed Pound's two comments, "Perhaps be damned" beside "Perhaps a cheap house agent's clerk" and "make up yr. mind" beside the typist's "one half-formed thought may pass" (facsimile p. 47, as cited by Zeligs and Vendler), which the printed line 251, "allows one half-formed thought to pass", bears out; the scholar's sentence taken. The batch C scholar's memory put the second comment beside the loiterers' lines; the drafts record is made to agree with this placing, and the difference is recorded.
title: The man who had been a woman
body:
Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> III, which Eliot's note quotes in Latin. Tiresias struck two coupling snakes with his staff and was turned into a woman; in the eighth year he struck them again and became a man. When Jupiter and Juno argued over which sex has the greater pleasure in love they asked him, as the one who had been both, and he sided with Jupiter, who said women do. Juno blinded him; Jupiter, who could not undo another god's act, gave him foresight instead. He is also the blind prophet of Thebes in Sophocles' <i>Oedipus</i>, and the dead seer Odysseus consults in <i>Odyssey</i> XI: "I who have sat by Thebes below the wall / And walked among the lowest of the dead."

Eliot's note calls him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest", and says "What Tiresias <i>sees</i>, in fact, is the substance of the poem." The claim is Eliot's, made in the notes he wrote for the book edition of December 1922, a year after the scene was typed. In 1956 he said the notes had been expanded to fill out the volume and called them "bogus scholarship". Critics divide: some read the whole poem as what Tiresias sees, others take him as one voice among many and the note as an afterthought. In the poem he names himself three times, at 218, 228 and 243, all in this scene. Pound, in the margin of the typescript, objected to the scene's hedging: beside "Perhaps a cheap house agent's clerk" he wrote "Perhaps be damned", and beside the typist's "one half-formed thought may pass" he told Eliot that Tiresias should "make up yr. mind" whether he knew or not.
