## lovely-woman
Object: "some days after her father has brought her home from the squire who seduced her". The Vicar did not fetch her from the squire: he found her at an inn (the Harrow), where the landlady was turning her out after the squire had cast her off (ch. 21), and brought her home; and in ch. 23 more than a week passes before the song, plus George's three-day errand (Gutenberg #2667, chs. 21–24). Replace the first sentence with: "Olivia Primrose's song in <i>The Vicar of Wakefield</i> (1766), chapter 24, sung to her family some weeks after her father has found her at an inn, cast off by the squire who seduced her, and brought her home."

## thames-daughters
Accept. The reader's two facts confirmed: Thames sailing barges' flax sails were dressed with red ochre and oil, which gave them their rust-red colour, and the rig is a spritsail, the mainsail loose-footed and set on the sprit, a heavy spar (Wikipedia, Thames sailing barge: "red ochre, cod oil, urine and seawater"; the example barge's sprit 59 ft long). Since red-sailed Thames barges were spritsail-rigged, "the "heavy spar" the sprit" needs no hedge.

## margate
Accept. The shelter sentence is in the same letter: the 4 November 1921 letter to Schiff runs "I have done a rough draft of part of part III, but I do not know whether it will do, and must wait for Vivien's opinion as to whether it is printable. I have done this while sitting in a shelter on the front – as I am out all day except when taking rest" (Letters I, as quoted by Kent Maps Online and the Historic England listing; the listing's own paraphrase drops "part of"). The "dirty hand" draft is now verified at second hand: two web accounts citing the facsimile, p. 53, give the first version as "I was to be grateful. On Margate sands / There were many others. I can connect / Nothing with nothing. He had / I still feel the pressure of dirty hand", revised on the leaf to "On Margate sands. / I can connect / Nothing wth nothing. / The broken fingernails of dirty hands. / My people are plain people, who expect / Nothing". For the drafts record rather than this gloss, cited as "facsimile p. 53, as reported"; neither account explains "plain" becoming "humble", and Rainey's statement that the twelve-line Margate leaf was unrevised sits oddly beside it, so the facsimile itself should settle the page before it is printed.
