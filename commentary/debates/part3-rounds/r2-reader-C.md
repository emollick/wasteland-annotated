## Tiresias in Ovid
Object: the first sentence runs a colon and a semicolon through three separate facts. Proposed text: Eliot's note at 218 quotes nineteen lines of Ovid (<i>Metamorphoses</i> III, 320–338). Tiresias, who had lived seven years as a woman, is asked to settle Jupiter and Juno's dispute over which sex has the greater pleasure in love. Juno blinds him for his answer and Jupiter gives him foresight. The library has the Latin with an English version. Read it, then the typist scene, which Tiresias says he has "foresuffered".

## Whose side is the poem on
Object: "the typist does not" is clipped to a riddle (does not die? does not stoop?); "The scene has been read as" hides who reads. Proposed text: The typist "smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone" (255–256). Eliot's note gives Goldsmith's song, in which the woman who "stoops to folly" has nothing left but to die; Eliot's typist, instead, puts a record on. Critics have read the scene as contempt for her, and as the nearest the poem comes to pity; those who read it as pity point to Tiresias's "foresuffered all" (243).

## fresca
Object: "critics also hear Swift's dressing-room poems" — "hear" is a critic's verb; and the last sentence joins the printed opening and Rainey's dating on a semicolon. Proposed: "critics also compare Swift's dressing-room poems." and, for the last sentence: The printed section begins with the river. On Rainey's dating of the drafts, its opening lines, 173–182, were the last lines written for the poem, in Paris in January 1922. The rest as written.

## typist-draft
Object: "The clerk had his age, his spotty face ... ; the typist a kimono and a Japanese print; and Tiresias hedged" — "had his age" is not English for what the draft gave him, the typist's clause has lost its verb, and the semicolons stack three things; "beside the loiterers" is beside lines, not people; "A passage before the scene addressed to London" can be read as the scene being addressed to London. Proposed text: In the typescript the scene ran in regular quatrains and was longer. The draft gave the clerk his age, a spotty face, greasy hair, table manners and boasts, and gave the typist a kimono and a Japanese print. Tiresias hedged with "Perhaps a cheap house agent's clerk", one of those loiterers who might have been seen anywhere. Pound wrote that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it. Against the hedge he wrote "Perhaps be damned", and beside the loiterers' lines he told Tiresias to make up his mind whether he knew or not. The clerk's exit, a couplet in which he stopped at a corner to urinate and spit, was marked by Pound and dropped; the dots at 248 fall where it stood. A passage that stood before the scene, addressed to London ("London, the swarming life you kill and breed"), went too.

## thames-draft
Object: the first sentence runs to a semicolon and ends 'the words he wrote here, "OK" and "echt" (genuine), approve', where "approve" hangs without an object; "the Elizabeth stanza" calls 279–289 a stanza. Proposed text: The river song, the Elizabeth and Leicester lines and the three daughters' speeches are generally taken to be the "some fifty lines" Eliot drafted at Margate in November 1921. They reached print with few marks from Pound, and the words he did write here, "OK" and "echt" (genuine), are approving. Eliot's own revision shortened the first daughter's speech. His note, which identifies three daughters who "speak in turn" from 292 to 306, was added, like all the notes, for the book edition of December 1922.
