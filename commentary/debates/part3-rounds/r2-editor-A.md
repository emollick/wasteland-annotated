# Round 2, editor's rewrites, batch A (glosses sweet-thames to tiresias)

How these were made: each rewrite takes the scholar's corrections of fact (the scholar has the last word on substance) and the reader's cuts and wording (the reader has the last word on wording). Where the two sides wanted different things the decision is noted. Fixed fields are untouched. Only `title`, `body` and, where marked, `quote` change.

## sweet-thames
Decisions: Spenser's poem summarised as both sides had it; the antithesis, "October:" (the poem names no month) and "a season you can feel on your skin" cut. The draft history (Margate; Rainey's Paris dating of 173–182) is moved out of this gloss to the drafts record "fresca", where the section's opening is the subject; the reader is right that it does not help at 176.

title: Spenser's wedding river
body:
The refrain of Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596), a "spousal verse" for a double wedding, in which the poet walks by the Thames and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans that stand for the brides. Eliot keeps the refrain (again at 183–184); his nymphs "are departed". "Their friends, the loitering heirs of city directors" are usually taken as the young men of the summer, gone with the season, and the litter of summer nights gone with them.

## leman
Decisions: the scholar's correction stands: Eliot's line follows the Prayer Book psalter, not the King James, so the quote field changes to the Prayer Book wording and the body says which. Lausanne dates as the scholar gave them. Rainey's dating of this line to Paris, after Lausanne, is left out: nothing in the rewrite implies the line was written by the lake, and the reader does not need the point; it goes in the record. "Leman" as lover kept, marked as a second sense critics note. The antithesis and the "weeping by a Swiss lake" sentence cut.

title: Psalm 137 and Lake Geneva
quote: By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept: when we remembered thee, O Sion.
body:
Psalm 137 in the Prayer Book wording, "By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept" (the King James Bible has "rivers"), with Leman for Babylon and "I" for "we". Leman is Lac Léman, Lake Geneva. Eliot was at Lausanne, on its shore, from late November 1921 to early January 1922, on sick leave from Lloyds Bank and under the care of Dr Roger Vittoz; Parts IV and V were written there. "Leman" is also an old word for a lover or mistress, a second sense critics have noted.

## at-my-back
Decisions: the numbering slip kept in a few words (confirmed against the 1922 pages). "A skull's grin" dropped: the reader liked it, the scholar called it an unowned reading, and the quotation carries the sense without it. The seduction/dying antithesis cut.

title: Time's chariot, twice
body:
Marvell, "To His Coy Mistress" (published 1681), in which the speaker urges his mistress to yield because time is short and the grave is near. Eliot uses the line twice. Here what is heard at the speaker's back is "the rattle of the bones, and chuckle spread from ear to ear"; at line 196 it is "the sound of horns and motors". Eliot's note gives the source at the second use, and in the 1922 notes the Marvell and Day notes carry each other's numbers, 197 and 196.

## fishing-canal
Decisions: Ferdinand's father not drowned but believed so (reader); the Fisher King given as the usual reading (both); Eliot's own cross-reference from line 115 added (scholar); the war dead given as a reading, the "swimmers of last summer" dropped as unowned. The quote field's "wrack" becomes "wreck", to match the library record and the modern text (Folio "wracke").

title: Ferdinand on the bank, and the Fisher King
quote: Sitting on a bank,<br>Weeping again the king my father’s wreck,<br>This music crept by me upon the waters,<br>Allaying both their fury and my passion<br>With its sweet air.
body:
Ferdinand in <i>The Tempest</i>, Act I scene ii, sitting on the shore and weeping for the father he believes drowned, just before Ariel sings "Full fathom five". Eliot's note gives the source at line 192, and the same speech returns at 257. Eliot adds a brother to Shakespeare's father and puts the fisherman by a canal behind a gashouse in winter. He is usually read as also the Fisher King of Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, named in Eliot's headnote, whose wound leaves his land barren and who fishes again at 424. The rat and the bones are Eliot's own cross-reference: his note to "rats' alley" at line 115 reads "Cf. Part III, l. 195". The "White bodies naked on the low damp ground" have been read as the war dead.

## mrs-porter
Decisions: "Apeneck Sweeney" quoted as Eliot's (reader); the song's history hedged as the scholar asked; "the most violent cut in the poem" cut.

title: Actaeon and Mrs Porter
body:
John Day's <i>The Parliament of Bees</i> (printed 1641), quoted in Eliot's note: a noise of horns and hunting brings Actaeon to Diana bathing. In Ovid the hunter who sees the goddess naked is turned into a stag and torn apart by his own hounds. In Eliot the horns are motor horns, the hunter is Sweeney, the "Apeneck Sweeney" of his poems of 1918–20, and the bathing is done in soda water. The three lines of song are from a bawdy ballad of Australian troops in the First World War, in which Mrs Porter is said to have kept a brothel in Cairo; the soldiers' versions were less printable. Eliot's note says only that it "was reported to me from Sydney, Australia".

## verlaine
Decisions: the scholar's draft, which the reader's matches in substance; the feet-washing parallel given as critics'.

title: The children's voices in the dome
body:
The last line of Verlaine's sonnet "Parsifal" (1886), on Wagner's opera: the knight has resisted the Flower-maidens and Kundry, healed the wounded king Amfortas and adores the Grail, and the sonnet ends on the sound of the boys' choir in the dome of the Grail temple. Eliot's note gives the source. The line follows Mrs Porter and her daughter washing their feet; in Wagner's third act Kundry washes Parsifal's feet before he goes in to the Grail, a parallel critics have often drawn.

## tereu
Decisions: the scholar's correction on the songs' printing and authorship; the reader's cross-reference to 99–103; Eliot's own note from 100 added; the counting and the "It is evidence" close cut; title plainer.

title: Tereu, the nightingale's cry
body:
The song printed with Lyly's <i>Campaspe</i> (the play 1584; its songs first printed 1632, and whether Lyly wrote them is disputed) gives the Elizabethan convention that the nightingale sings "jug jug" and cries "tereu", the vocative of Tereus, the name of the king who raped her and cut out her tongue. The story is Ovid's (<i>Metamorphoses</i> VI) and is told at lines 99–103. Line 205 repeats line 100, "So rudely forced", in an older spelling; Eliot's note to line 100 reads "Cf. Part III, l. 204".

## eugenides
Decisions: the scholar's corrections taken (no boat trains at Cannon Street after 1914; Smyrna a dried-fruit port rather than London's currant supplier; "usual place" and "daily" unsupported; Weston cited by chapter; Metropole hedged). The burning of Smyrna is cut, on the reader's point that it postdates the poem and does not bear on the line; the photograph's caption keeps its date. The profile explanation of the one eye kept, hedged.

title: The one-eyed merchant
body:
Eugenides is a Greek surname, from <i>eugenēs</i>, well-born. Smyrna, now İzmir, was the chief port of Asia Minor and a centre of the dried-fruit trade; the pocketful of currants is presumably his samples. "C.i.f. London: documents at sight" is trade shorthand: the price covers cost, insurance and freight to London, and the shipping documents are handed over when the sight draft is paid. Eliot's note expands it as "carriage and insurance free", which is not the trade usage; the terms were those of his work at Lloyds Bank. French was the trading language of the Levant, and "demotic" marks his as the common, colloquial kind. The Cannon Street Hotel formed the front of Cannon Street station in the City and was much used for business meetings. The Metropole is usually taken as the Hotel Metropole on the Brighton seafront, and the weekend as a proposition, often read as a homosexual one.

Eliot's note to line 218 makes him the "one-eyed merchant, seller of currants" of Madame Sosostris's pack, who "melts into the Phoenician Sailor"; the one eye has been explained by the profile faces on playing cards. Weston (<i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, chapter XII) says the mystery cults behind the Grail reached the west of the Roman Empire first through Syrian merchants.

## violet-hour
Decisions: Gloucester given as fact about the Eliots, the link left to the reader (both sides); Stevenson as a parallel usually cited; "the poem's hour" cut; the Part V lines given by number in the site's count (373 and 380, not Eliot's 372 and 379).

title: Sappho's evening star
body:
Sappho's two lines on the evening star, which brings back all that dawn scattered, are behind "the evening hour that strives / Homeward, and brings the sailor home from sea". Eliot's note concedes that his lines "may not appear as exact as Sappho's" and says he had in mind the "longshore" or "dory" fisherman coming in at nightfall; the dory is the small boat of the New England fishermen, and the Eliots summered at Gloucester, Massachusetts. Stevenson's "Requiem" ("Home is the sailor, home from sea") is also usually cited. The violet hour is dusk, the end of the working day, when "the eyes and back / Turn upward from the desk"; "violet" returns in Part V, "the violet air" (373) and "the violet light" (380).

## tiresias
Decisions: Ovid as both sides had it, without "(women do)"; the note's claim kept as Eliot's, with the date of the notes and his 1956 remark (scholar); "the drafts show" and the "Take it seriously" antithesis cut; the two critical positions given as positions; where Tiresias names himself given plainly; Pound's marginalium reduced to a phrase and not placed against a particular line, since the two scholars' memories of where it stands differ.

title: The man who had been a woman
body:
Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> III, which Eliot's note quotes in Latin. Tiresias struck two coupling snakes with his staff and was turned into a woman; in the eighth year he struck them again and became a man. When Jupiter and Juno argued over which sex has the greater pleasure in love they asked him, as the one who had been both, and he sided with Jupiter, who said women do. Juno blinded him; Jupiter, who could not undo another god's act, gave him foresight instead. He is also the blind prophet of Thebes in Sophocles' <i>Oedipus</i>, and the dead seer Odysseus consults in <i>Odyssey</i> XI: "I who have sat by Thebes below the wall / And walked among the lowest of the dead."

Eliot's note calls him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest", and says "What Tiresias <i>sees</i>, in fact, is the substance of the poem." That is Eliot's claim, made in the notes he wrote for the book edition of December 1922; the typescript with Tiresias in it had been made a year earlier. In 1956 he said the notes had been expanded to fill out the volume and called them "bogus scholarship". Critics divide: some read the whole poem as what Tiresias sees, others take him as one voice among many and the note as an afterthought. In the poem he names himself three times, at 218, 228 and 243, all in this scene. Pound, in the margin of the typescript of the scene, told Eliot that Tiresias should "make up yr. mind" whether he knew or not.
