## dante-purg5
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: "speaks seven lines and gives her name, her birthplace, the place of her death and, by implication, her murderer, her husband" (counting, then the rhythmic list); "borrows the grammar and keeps the anonymity" (bow-tying last sentence; a metaphor where an explanation should be).
Unclear or unhelpful: "keeps the anonymity" contradicts the note's own "gives her name": la Pia names herself, the Thames-daughter does not. "Among the late-repentant who died by violence" is Dante's category in Dante's shorthand. "Maremma" is not explained.
Keep: what she tells Dante; the early commentators' identification; "nothing certain is known of her".
Proposed note: The souls in this canto repented only as they died, by violence. The last to speak is a woman who names herself la Pia, says Siena made her and the Maremma (the marsh country on the Tuscan coast) unmade her, and says that the husband who put his ring on her knows how she died. Early commentators named her Pia de' Tolomei of Siena and her husband Nello dei Pannocchieschi; nothing certain is known of her. Eliot's first Thames-daughter says where she was born and where she was undone in the same shape of sentence.

## gotterdammerung
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: "Eliot's note is precise about the transfer" (annotator admiring Eliot; "transfer" is a metaphor); "the 'la la' that Eliot's Thames-daughters sing over oil and tar" (cute compression).
Unclear or unhelpful: quoting Eliot's note back is redundant; the site prints it at line 266. "Their refrain is the 'la la'" muddles: the refrain is the two Weialala lines, and "la la" at 306 is its tail.
Keep: the plot in one sentence: third act, three Rhine-daughters, the gold Alberich stole at the start of the first opera, asking Siegfried for the ring.
Proposed note: The last of the four <i>Ring</i> operas opens its third act with the three Rhine-daughters swimming and singing of the gold Alberich stole from them at the start of the first, <i>Das Rheingold</i>; Siegfried comes to the bank and they ask him for the ring. Eliot's note on line 266 sends the reader here for the song of the Thames-daughters, whose refrain is the Rhine-daughters' own.

## shakespeare-tempest
Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: "beautifully and falsely" (balanced antithesis); "everyone has suffered a sea-change for the better" (annotator's wit on the source's word); "Eliot quotes the song twice and Ferdinand's speech twice, and never allows the change" (counting, then the bow); "The drowned father ... becomes ...; Ferdinand weeping on the bank becomes ..." (matched clauses on a semicolon).
Unclear or unhelpful: "never allows the change" is a reading stated as fact. "the drowned Phoenician and Phlebas" reads as two people; they are one. "becomes the Fisher King" is a reading I cannot check from anything the note gives me, and the poem says gashouse, not gasworks. In `what`, "Ferdinand on the bank" does not tell me which words are Ferdinand's.
Keep: the plot: Ferdinand believes his father drowned, Ariel's song tells him so, Alonso is alive. That is what I need at 191 and 257.
Proposed what: Ariel's song, "Those are pearls that were his eyes"; Ferdinand's "the king my father's wreck" and "This music crept by me upon the waters."
Proposed note: Ferdinand, cast ashore by the storm, believes his father Alonso drowned in the wreck, and Ariel's song tells him so. Alonso is in fact alive, and the play ends with father and son reunited. Eliot takes Ariel's pearls for Madame Sosostris's drowned sailor (line 48) and for the memory in Part II (125), and gives Ferdinand's words to the fisher in Part III (191) and to the walker in the City (257). Critics have noted that the poem keeps the drowning and the music and leaves out the play's restoration.

## ovid-met6
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: "sits above the mantel in Part II, breaks into birdsong in Part III, and ends the poem as a swallow" (rhythmic three; metaphors for the poem instead of line numbers); "is the English to go to" (tour guide).
Unclear or unhelpful: the first sentence is four clauses on semicolons; break it. "the one Shakespeare read" does not help me with the line and I cannot check it. This is the only record with no passage; a reader wants the lines where the birds are made, in translation.
Keep: the whole story, in order; "Ovid does not say which sister became the nightingale ... Eliot follows it", which is exactly the kind of thing I want told.
Proposed note: Tereus, king of Thrace, brings his wife Procne's sister Philomela from Athens to visit her, rapes her on the way in a hut in the woods, and cuts out her tongue. She weaves what happened into a cloth and sends it to her sister. Procne kills her son Itys and serves him to Tereus at table. He pursues the sisters with a sword, and all three are turned into birds. Ovid does not say which sister became the nightingale; later tradition made Philomela the nightingale and Procne the swallow, and Eliot follows it. The story is painted on the wall in Part II (line 99), the nightingale's cry breaks in at 203–206, and the swallow comes back at 429.

## ovid-met3
Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: `what`, "The man who had been a woman, and who sees the future" (a riddle for a label); "Eliot's note makes him the poem's uniting consciousness. Ovid makes him a man who lost an argument with a goddess." (the balanced pair, the epigram to close).
Unclear or unhelpful: "Lines 324–331 of the passage" opens on a fragment and does not say whose lines. The note never says what Tiresias answered, which is the point of the story. "lost an argument with a goddess" is not what the passage says: he judged the argument and was punished for his verdict. "uniting consciousness" is critic-speak for Eliot's plain "uniting all the rest".
Keep: "consult the one person who has been both"; "his answer costs him his sight and earns him prophecy"; the pointer to Eliot's "of great anthropological interest".
Proposed what: Tiresias, who lived seven years as a woman, and how he came to see the future.
Proposed note: Jupiter and Juno, arguing which sex has the greater pleasure in love, put the question to Tiresias, who had known both. These lines tell how: he had struck two coupling snakes with his staff and been turned into a woman, and in the eighth year struck them again and became a man. He sided with Jupiter. Juno blinded him for it, and Jupiter, who could not undo what another god had done, gave him knowledge of the future instead. The passage here is <i>Metamorphoses</i> III.324–331, the middle of the passage Eliot's note quotes in full, calling it "of great anthropological interest" and Tiresias "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest".

## spenser-prothalamion
Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: "Notice that" (the tell itself); "Eliot keeps the refrain and empties the river" (epigram, metaphor); "before the wedding cheers him up" (chatty).
Unclear or unhelpful: "two swans who are the brides" is right but abrupt; say they stand for the brides. The link between Spenser's nymphs and "The nymphs are departed" is the useful thing and is only implied.
Keep: what a Prothalamion is and whose wedding; the poet out of favour, walking off his "sullein care"; the nymphs and the swans.
Proposed note: Spenser's "spousal verse" for the double wedding of the Earl of Worcester's daughters. The poet, out of favour at court and walking by the Thames to ease his "sullein care", sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans coming down the river, who stand for the brides. Eliot takes the refrain, and the nymphs he says are departed (lines 175 and 179) are usually taken as Spenser's.

## marvell-coy-mistress
Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: "The great English argument for going to bed now, because later there is only the grave" (aphoristic opening; uncheckable superlative); "gives it a rattle of bones the first time and motor horns the second" (the tidy pairing).
Unclear or unhelpful: "the turn of the argument" is precious; say which line.
Keep: the sense of Marvell's poem in a sentence; the 1921 essay and its phrase, which is Eliot's wit, not ours.
Proposed note: Marvell's speaker urges his mistress to yield now, because time is short and the grave waits. Eliot uses the opening of the lines twice, and what the speaker hears at his back is first the rattle of bones and a chuckle (185–186), then the horns and motors that bring Sweeney to Mrs Porter (196–198). Earlier in 1921 Eliot had written his essay on Marvell, praising "a tough reasonableness beneath the slight lyric grace".

## day-parliament-of-bees
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: "Eliot brings Sweeney to Mrs Porter in a motor car" (the joke to close; the `what` has already said it).
Unclear or unhelpful: "A masque-like sequence of 'characters' among the bees" does not tell me what the work is; say that the bees stand for kinds of men.
Keep: the Ovid sentence on Actaeon, which is the thing I need; "a spring hunt bringing Actaeon to the goddess bathing".
Proposed note: A sequence of verse "characters", satirical portraits in which bees stand for kinds of men. The lines Eliot's note quotes tell how a noise of horns and hunting will bring Actaeon upon Diana bathing. In Ovid the hunter who sees Diana naked is turned into a stag and torn apart by his own hounds. Eliot keeps the horns, adds motors, and the hunter and goddess become Sweeney and Mrs Porter.

## verlaine-parsifal
Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: "compresses the whole plot into thirteen lines and then, in the fourteenth, simply reports" (counting; "simply" admiring); "so that the temptation and the temple stand one line apart" (alliterated antithesis; intention as fact); "his healing of the king is the thing the poem never manages" (bow-tying; reading as fact).
Unclear or unhelpful: "a barrack-room song about a brothel": nothing in lines 199–201 tells me it is about a brothel, so I cannot tell whether to believe it. "Weston's Grail knight" assumes I know who Weston is. The note never says what happens in the opera, which the sonnet assumes.
Keep: written for the <i>Revue wagnérienne</i>; the last line reports the boys' choir in the Grail temple; Eliot quotes it straight after the Mrs Porter song.
Proposed note: Verlaine's sonnet on Wagner's <i>Parsifal</i>, written for the <i>Revue wagnérienne</i>, runs through the opera in thirteen lines: the knight resists the flower-maidens and Kundry, heals the wounded king with the spear and becomes keeper of the Grail. The last line is the children's voices singing in the dome of the Grail temple, and it is the one Eliot quotes, straight after the soldiers' song about Mrs Porter. The knight who heals the wounded king is the Grail story Eliot's headnote says the poem draws on, through Jessie Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>.

Verdict: rewrite

## goldsmith-vicar
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: "as contempt for her and as the poem's one act of mercy toward a woman: she is allowed not to die" (antithesis; an uncheckable "one act"; the epigram after the colon).
Unclear or unhelpful: "in the same meter, puts on a record" is compressed to the point of a joke; say that Eliot's line is Goldsmith's line continued. Whose readings?
Keep: the first sentence, which is exactly what I need; "has been read as", the right way to offer a reading.
Proposed note: Olivia Primrose, seduced and abandoned by the squire, sings this to her family after she has been brought home. Eliot's line 253 is Goldsmith's first line with "and" added, and the sentence runs on into the typist smoothing her hair and putting on a record, where Goldsmith's woman has nothing left but to die. ## froude-elizabeth
Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: "Eliot's Thames pageant of red and gold is this afternoon, seen from the water, with nothing coming of it" (bow-tying; metaphor; a reading as fact).
Unclear or unhelpful: "Lord Robert" in the passage is never connected to "Dudley" in the note or to Leicester in the poem. "in circumstances that made it impossible" withholds the fact it alludes to; either say it or leave it out.
Keep: who wrote the letter, to whom, when; that the marriage never happened.
Proposed note: Froude's history quotes a letter of 1561 from the Spanish ambassador, De Quadra, to Philip II, about an afternoon on the river when the queen and Lord Robert Dudley, later Earl of Leicester, talked of marrying. Eliot's note points to the letter for lines 279–289.

## augustine-confessions
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: "which is why the Fire Sermon ends with it" (Eliot's motive stated as fact); "rewrites its first sentence in his note" (a deliberate act I cannot check; it may be misquotation from memory).
Unclear or unhelpful: "the pull of beautiful things on a man trying to look at God" is nearly lyric, though it does explain X.34. "six books later" makes me count.
Keep: student years in Carthage; Book X and the plucking out; Pusey and Everyman; the Carthago/sartago pun, which is real and helps.
Proposed note: Book III opens with Augustine's arrival as a student in Carthage, where "a cauldron of unholy loves" sang around him. In Book X, long after, he says that beautiful things still catch at him and that God plucks him out. The translation is Pusey's, the one in Everyman's Library; Eliot's note quotes the Carthage sentence in slightly different words. The Latin plays on Carthago and sartago, a frying pan, a pun critics have connected with the burning in the lines that follow.

## buddha-fire-sermon
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: "It is the text behind the whole third section, in which everything, from the river to the typist, is seen as burning" (a reading as fact, with the sweeping "everything, from X to Y").
Unclear or unhelpful: the note never says what the sermon says; the passage does, and one clause of it would help.
Keep: "Preached, the tradition says, to a thousand fire-worshipping ascetics"; Warren; Eliot's comparison with the Sermon on the Mount, which is his and rightly credited.
Proposed note: The Buddha's sermon, preached, the tradition says, to a thousand fire-worshipping ascetics on Gayā Head soon after his enlightenment: everything the senses meet is on fire, with the fire of passion, of hatred, of infatuation. Eliot read it at Harvard in Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i>, the book his note recommends, and his note calls the sermon as important as the Sermon on the Mount. Part III takes its title from it, and "burning" at lines 308 and 311 is its word.
