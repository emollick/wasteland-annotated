## sweet-thames

Verdict: needs correction.

Errors:
- "Part of the section was drafted at Margate in the autumn of 1921" is true of Part III (Rainey: the Margate drafts are "O City, City", "The river sweats", "Highbury bore me", "On Margate Sands", lines 259-311; Eliot to Schiff, "some fifty lines") but placed here it says this passage was, and it was not: on Rainey's dating the ten opening lines were the last thing written, in Paris, 5-10 January 1922, after Pound had cut the Fresca opening (Revisiting, pp. 31-32, via ebin.pub; not verified against the facsimile).
- "October:" The poem names no month. Margate was late October to 12 November (Rainey p. 29 for the departure; arrival about 22 October from memory of Letters vol. 1, unverified), and these lines were not written there. Cut.
- Spenser details are right: "Spousall Verse" for the double marriage of the Earl of Worcester's daughters; nymphs gather flowers; the two swans (the brides) come down to "a stately place" where the poet once had the favour of "that great lord" (Leicester House, by 1596 Essex House), and "two gentle knights" meet them (en.wikisource.org/wiki/Prothalamion, via The Works of Edmund Spenser).

Readings passed off as fact:
- "Spenser's river is a procession; Eliot's is the morning after." Antithesis; annotator's.
- "the young men of the summer, leaving no bottles ... because the season is over": the usual reading (Southam, from memory), stated as fact.
- "it is the only part of the poem with a season you can feel on your skin": uncheckable, second person.

Missing: that the refrain returns at 183-184; the Paris dating, since the body raises the drafts at all.

Cut: the antithesis; "October:"; the last sentence entire.

Proposed body:
The refrain of Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596), a "spousal verse" for the double wedding of the Earl of Worcester's daughters: the poet walks by the Thames, nymphs gather flowers for the brides, and two swans swim down to Essex House to meet the bridegrooms. Eliot keeps the refrain (again at 183–184) and empties the river. "The nymphs" and "their friends, the loitering heirs of city directors" are usually taken as the couples of the summer, gone with the season, and the litter of summer nights gone with them. On Rainey's dating of the drafts these opening lines were the last written for the poem, in Paris in January 1922, after Pound had cut the long passage in couplets about Fresca that had stood at the head of Part III.

## leman

Verdict: needs correction.

Errors:
- "with one word changed": wrong. Eliot's line follows the Prayer Book psalter, "By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept" (Coverdale; the 1662 and 1928 books are the same here, episcopalnet.org/1928bcp/Psalter/Ps137.html), not the King James "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept" that the quote field gives. Against the Prayer Book two words change, Leman for Babylon and "I" for "we"; against the King James, four or five. The quote field should carry the Prayer Book wording or the body must say which version.
- "November and December 1921": Rainey p. 23 gives 24 November 1921 to 2 January 1922, Hotel Ste Luce, Lausanne. Say "late November 1921 to 2 January 1922".
- "writing Parts IV and V": Rainey pp. 31-32: the autograph fair copies of IV and V, plus a seventeen-line passage meant for III, were made there. Acceptable as "Parts IV and V were written there".
- The body implies the line was written by the lake. On Rainey's dating the section's opening ten lines, this among them, were written in Paris in the days after Eliot left Lausanne (pp. 31-32; unverified against the facsimile). State as his.

Readings passed off as fact:
- "The line is the poem's most private moment and its most public": antithesis for its own sake.
- "a man on sick leave from a bank, weeping by a Swiss lake": a scene inferred from the biography, told as if witnessed.
- "'Leman' is also an old English word for a lover or mistress": true of the word (OED, leman: lover, mistress); that Eliot meant it is a reading (Ricks and McCue note the sense, from memory).

Missing: which psalter; that he was on leave from Lloyds Bank (granted 12 October 1921, three months; from memory of Valerie Eliot's introduction, unverified) is worth one clause since it explains the stay.

Cut: the last sentence.

Proposed body:
Psalm 137 in the Prayer Book wording, "By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept" (the King James has "rivers"), with Leman for Babylon and "I" for "we". Leman is Lac Léman, Lake Geneva. Eliot was at Lausanne, on its shore, from late November 1921 to 2 January 1922, on sick leave from Lloyds Bank and under the care of Dr Roger Vittoz; Parts IV and V were written there. On Rainey's dating of the drafts this line, with the rest of the section's opening, was written in Paris in the days after he left. "Leman" is also an old word for a lover or mistress, a second sense critics have noted.

Title: "Psalm 137 and Lake Geneva" (the present one is a list for effect).

## at-my-back

Verdict: needs hedging.

Errors:
- None of fact. The numbering claim is confirmed: the 1922 notes carry the Day and Marvell notes with each other's numbers, 196 Day and 197 Marvell (both archive.org copies above). "A slip" is fair; "swapped" is exacter. Marvell "published 1681" (Miscellaneous Poems) is right; the quote is the standard modernised text.

Readings passed off as fact:
- "a skull's grin": reading of "chuckle spread from ear to ear", unowned.
- "Marvell's poem is a seduction: hurry, because we die." The "X is a Y:" pattern; the content is fine.
- "Eliot's borrowings from it, both times, skip the seduction and keep the dying." Antithesis named in the standard.
- "turns into Sweeney's motor horns": metaphor for "is followed by".

Missing: nothing a reader of this line needs. (Eliot's TLS essay on Marvell, March 1921, is in the library note and need not be repeated.)

Cut: the last two sentences.

Proposed body:
Marvell, "To His Coy Mistress" (published 1681). Eliot's note attaches the source to line 196, where the phrase comes a second time and what is heard is "horns and motors"; in the 1922 notes the Marvell and Day notes carry each other's numbers, 196 Day and 197 Marvell. Here, the first time, what is heard is "the rattle of the bones", and the bones return at 194–195. In Marvell the sound at the speaker's back is the argument for love now, before the grave.

## fishing-canal

Verdict: needs hedging.

Errors:
- None of fact. Ferdinand's speech does come just before "Full fathom five" (I.ii). The quote field reads "wrack" where the library record reads "wreck" (Folio "wracke"; Eliot's line "wreck"); make the two agree.
- The rat and bones link is Eliot's own: his note to line 115 reads "Cf. Part III, l. 195" (1922 text, both archive.org copies). The body treats it as the annotator's connection.

Readings passed off as fact:
- "Eliot's fisherman is both Ferdinand ... and Weston's Fisher King": the second is the standard reading (from the headnote on Weston and line 424), not something Eliot's note says.
- "which is not where either of them belongs": flourish.
- "which are the war dead, or the swimmers of last summer, or both": unowned readings.
- "(line 115 sent you here)": second person.

Missing: Eliot's cross-reference at 115; that the speech returns at 257.

Cut: "which is not where either of them belongs"; "(line 115 sent you here)"; the last clause of readings.

Proposed body:
Ferdinand in <i>The Tempest</i>, I.ii, just before Ariel sings "Full fathom five": "Sitting on a bank, / Weeping again the king my father's wreck". Eliot's note gives the source at line 192, and the speech returns at 257. Eliot's fisherman adds a brother to Shakespeare's father and sits by a canal behind a gashouse in winter; he is usually read as also the Fisher King of Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, named in Eliot's headnote, who fishes again at 424. The rat and the bones are Eliot's own cross-reference: his note to "rats' alley" at line 115 reads "Cf. Part III, l. 195".

## mrs-porter

Verdict: needs hedging.

Errors:
- "printed 1641": right. Day's lines as quoted match the 1922 note.
- "the ape-necked man of his 1920 poems": "Apeneck Sweeney" opens "Sweeney Among the Nightingales", first printed 1918, collected 1919 and 1920. Say "his poems of 1918–20".
- "a soldiers' song of the Gallipoli campaign, said to be about a Cairo brothel-keeper": the usual account (Southam, from memory) is a song of Australian troops in the First World War, Mrs Porter reputedly a Cairo brothel-keeper; Gallipoli is where it is usually placed; the sung versions vary. Keep "said to be" on all of it.
- "Which makes the next line, in French, the most violent cut in the poem." Uncheckable superlative.

Readings passed off as fact:
- "the goddess is Mrs Porter, and the bathing is a foot-wash in soda water": the plain parallel; fine as description.
- "the most violent cut in the poem": cut.

Missing: nothing.

Cut: the last sentence.

Proposed body:
John Day's <i>The Parliament of Bees</i> (printed 1641), quoted in Eliot's note: a spring hunt brings Actaeon to Diana bathing. In Ovid the hunter who sees the goddess naked is turned into a stag and torn apart by his own hounds. In Eliot the horns are motor horns, the hunter is Sweeney, the "Apeneck Sweeney" of his poems of 1918–20, and the bathing is done in soda water. The three lines of song are from a bawdy ballad of Australian troops in the First World War, in which Mrs Porter is said to have kept a brothel in Cairo; the soldiers' versions were less printable. Eliot's note says only that it "was reported to me from Sydney, Australia".

- The feet-washing parallel is a critics' observation (Southam, Grover Smith, from memory); give it as one.

Missing: nothing.

Cut: the last sentence.

Proposed body:
The last line of Verlaine's sonnet "Parsifal" (1886), on Wagner's opera: the knight has resisted the Flower-maidens and Kundry, healed the wounded king and adores the Grail, and the sonnet ends on the sound of the boys' choir in the dome of the Grail temple. Eliot's note gives the source. ## eugenides

Verdict: needs correction.

Errors:
- "Cannon Street station, the terminus for the boat trains to the Continent": not in 1921–22. The Continental boat trains were withdrawn from Cannon Street on 15 November 1914 and rerouted to Victoria, and the South Eastern and Chatham's Continental services were all at Victoria from 8 January 1920 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannon_Street_station; kentrail.uk/london_victoria_7.htm). Southam's "terminus for the Continental boat-trains" (from memory) is the source of the slip. Write "had been ... until 1914".
- "the usual place for City men to meet foreign clients": unsupported. Known: the City Terminus Hotel, opened 1867 as the station's frontage, renamed Cannon Street Hotel 1879, mostly closed 1931 with its public rooms kept for meetings (Wikipedia, as above). "Much used for business meetings" is what can be said.
- "Smyrna ... shipped the currants that London's bakers used": overstated. The dried-currant trade was Greek (Zante, Patras; Britain the chief importer); Smyrna's staples were figs and sultanas (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zante_currant; europenowjournal.org on the Greek currant trade). Say "a centre of the dried-fruit trade".
- "His own note expands the abbreviation slightly wrongly, as 'carriage and insurance free,' a banker's slip that the editors of the poem have enjoyed ever since": the note's wording is confirmed (1922 text); "cost, insurance and freight" is the trade term. "Enjoyed ever since" is decoration; "banker's slip" is a guess at its cause.
- "language Eliot handled daily at Lloyds": "daily" unsupported; he was in the Colonial and Foreign Department from 1917 (from memory of Gordon and Crawford, unverified).
- "the big hotel on the Brighton seafront": usually so taken (Hotel Metropole, Brighton, 1890), but there was a Hotel Métropole in London and one at Folkestone; hedge.
- "Weston argued that the Grail mysteries travelled Europe's trade routes with Syrian merchants": Weston, ch. XII, following Cumont, says the Attis and Mithra cults reached the farthest borders of the Roman Empire by three channels, "First, commercial, through the medium of Syrian merchants", then slaves and officials, then soldiers (Gutenberg #4090). Right in substance; cite the chapter.
- "In September 1922 ... Smyrna burned": the fire of 13–22 September 1922 (from memory); the Criterion printing was 16 October 1922. Right.
- "Eugenides means 'well-born'": Greek eugenēs, well-born, with a patronymic ending; fine.

Readings passed off as fact:
- "a pocketful is a sample": reasonable; say "presumably".
- "one-eyed, presumably, because a card shows a face in profile": a suggestion (Southam's, from memory); give it as one.
- "has generally been read as a homosexual one": "often" is the defensible word.

Missing: Eliot's note is at 218; that the identification with the one-eyed merchant is Eliot's own (the body has this).

Cut: "a banker's slip that the editors of the poem have enjoyed ever since"; "that London's bakers used"; "the usual place for City men to meet foreign clients".

Proposed body:
Eugenides is a Greek surname, from <i>eugenēs</i>, well-born. Smyrna, now İzmir, was the chief port of Asia Minor and a centre of the dried-fruit trade; the pocketful of currants is presumably his samples. "C.i.f. London: documents at sight" is trade shorthand: the price covers cost, insurance and freight to London, and the shipping documents are handed over against payment of the sight draft. Eliot's note expands it as "carriage and insurance free", which is not the trade usage; the terms were those of his work at Lloyds Bank. French was the trading language of the Levant, and "demotic" marks his as the common kind. The Cannon Street Hotel formed the front of Cannon Street station, the City terminus that had been the London end of the Continental boat trains until 1914, and was much used for business meetings. The Metropole is usually taken as the Hotel Metropole on the Brighton seafront, and the weekend as a proposition, often read as a homosexual one.

Eliot's note to line 218 makes him the "one-eyed merchant, seller of currants" of Madame Sosostris's pack, who "melts into the Phoenician Sailor"; the one eye has been explained by the profile faces of playing cards. Weston (<i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, ch. XII) says the mystery cults behind the Grail reached the west of the Roman Empire first through Syrian merchants. Smyrna burned in September 1922, between the poem's completion and its publication.

- "Eliot's note apologises": "concedes" is what the note does.

Missing: nothing.

Proposed body:

## tiresias

Verdict: needs correction.

Errors:
- "the drafts show that the design came late": the drafts contain no notes and cannot show when a design came. What is known: Tiresias is in the typescript of the typist scene, typed on Rainey's dating in November 1921 (p. 29); the notes were written in 1922 for the Boni & Liveright book (December 1922), the first printing to carry them; in "The Frontiers of Criticism" (1956) Eliot said the notes were expanded to provide "a few more pages of printed matter" and called the result "bogus scholarship" (wording confirmed via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Frontiers_of_Criticism and kcctreasures.com). Whether the note states a design or supplies one afterwards is a critical dispute (Kenner sceptical, from memory of The Invisible Poet; unverified).
- Pound's marginalium is quoted entire; the rights rule allows a phrase or two. It stands beside the draft's "Across her brain one half-formed thought may pass" (1922: "allows ... to pass"), objecting to the "may" (facsimile, typist scene, from memory; Vendler, Yale Review, transcribes "Make up yr. mind / you Tiresias / if you know / know damn well / or else / you / don't"). Quote "make up yr. mind you Tiresias" and say what it was beside.
- Ovid is summarised correctly (Met. III.316–338): seven autumns as a woman, the change back in the eighth; Jupiter's claim that the woman's pleasure is greater; Juno's blinding; Jupiter's foresight since no god may undo another's act. "(women do)" makes Jupiter's claim the annotator's.

Readings passed off as fact:
- "The note is the closest thing the poem has to a stated design": an evaluation; acceptable if owned or softened.
- "Take it seriously and the poem has a single consciousness; take it as one more voice and it doesn't.": second person, epigram; replace with the two positions, named as positions.

Missing: that the note's claim is Eliot's and what he later said of the notes; that Tiresias was in the typescript.

Cut: "the drafts show that the design came late"; the "Take it seriously" sentence; the full marginalium.

Proposed body:
Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> III, which Eliot's note quotes in Latin. Tiresias struck two coupling snakes with his staff and became a woman for seven years; in the eighth year he struck them again and was a man. Jupiter and Juno, disputing whether men or women have the greater pleasure in love, asked him as the one who had been both; he said women, as Jupiter had claimed. Juno blinded him; Jupiter, who could not undo another god's act, gave him foresight. He is the blind prophet of Thebes in Sophocles, and the dead seer Odysseus consults in <i>Odyssey</i> XI: "I who have sat by Thebes below the wall / And walked among the lowest of the dead."

Eliot's note calls him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest", and says "What Tiresias <i>sees</i>, in fact, is the substance of the poem." That is Eliot's claim, made in notes written in 1922 for the book edition; the typist passage, with Tiresias in it, had been typed in November 1921. In 1956 he said the notes had been expanded to fill out the volume and called them "bogus scholarship". Critics divide: some read the poem as what Tiresias sees, others take him as one voice among many and the note as an afterthought. Pound, in the margin of the typescript beside a hedging "may" in the typist scene, wrote "make up yr. mind you Tiresias".
