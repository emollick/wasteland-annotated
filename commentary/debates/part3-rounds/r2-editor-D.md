# sources.txt

## dante-purg5
note:
In Ante-Purgatory, among those who died by violence and repented at the last, a woman speaks seven lines: her name, her birthplace, the place of her death and, without naming him, the husband who put a ring on her and, the line implies, had her killed. The Maremma is the marsh country on the Tuscan coast. Early commentators identified her as Pia de' Tolomei of Siena and the husband as Nello dei Pannocchieschi; nothing certain is known of her. Eliot's note at 293 cites the passage; his first Thames-daughter has the shape of Pia's sentence, birthplace and undoing, and gives no name.

## gotterdammerung
trans (two changes): "whole and noble" for "heil und hehr"; "noble star of the deep" for "hehrer Stern der Tiefe".
note:
The last of the four <i>Ring</i> operas opens its third act with the three Rhine-daughters swimming and singing to the sun for the gold Alberich stole in the first scene of <i>Das Rheingold</i>; Siegfried comes by, and they ask him for the ring. Their wordless refrain, as the vocal scores and librettos print it, is the one Eliot's Thames-daughters sing at 277–278 and 290–291; Eliot's note at 266 sends the reader to the scene.

## shakespeare-tempest
what: Ariel's song, "Those are pearls that were his eyes"; Ferdinand's "the king my father's wreck" and "This music crept by me upon the waters."
note:
Ferdinand, cast ashore, believes his father Alonso drowned in the wreck; Ariel's song tells him so, though Alonso is alive. The poem takes the song's line about the pearls twice (48, 125) and Ferdinand's "This music crept by me upon the waters" once (257), and at 191–192 turns his "Weeping again the king my father's wreck" into a musing on a brother's wreck and a father's death. Eliot's notes at 192 and 257 point to the scene. The song's next lines, the "sea-change / Into something rich and strange", are not quoted.

## ovid-met6
Also: this is the only record without a passage; VI.424–674 is the story, and a public-domain English rendering of the change into birds (VI.667–674) would serve.
note:
Tereus, king of Thrace, bringing his wife Procne's sister Philomela from Athens to visit her, rapes her in a hut deep in the woods and cuts out her tongue; she weaves the story into a cloth and sends it to her sister; Procne kills her son Itys and serves him to his father; Tereus pursues the sisters with a sword, and all three are turned into birds (VI.424–674). Ovid does not say which sister became the nightingale; the Latin poets and the later tradition made her Philomela, and Eliot follows them. The story is painted on the wall in Part II (99–103); the bird's cry, "jug jug" and "Tereu" in the Elizabethan spelling, breaks in at 203–206 around "So rudely forc'd" from line 100; and Eliot's note to the swallow at 429 refers back to "Philomela in Parts II and III".

## ovid-met3
what: Tiresias, who lived seven years as a woman, and how he came to see the future.
note:
<i>Metamorphoses</i> III.324–331; Eliot's note quotes III.320–338 and calls the passage "of great anthropological interest". Jupiter and Juno, disputing which sex has the greater pleasure in love, refer it to Tiresias, who had been both: he struck two coupling snakes with his staff and was a woman for seven years, then struck them again and was a man. He sides with Jupiter; Juno blinds him; Jupiter, who cannot undo what a god has done, gives him knowledge of the future. Eliot's note makes him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest".

## spenser-prothalamion
note:
A "Spousall Verse" (Spenser's subtitle) for the double wedding in 1596 of the Earl of Worcester's daughters, Elizabeth and Katherine Somerset. The poet, disappointed of preferment at court, walks by the Thames to ease his "sullein care" and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans coming down the river, who stand for the brides; the refrain closes each of the ten stanzas. Eliot's note at 176 cites the poem; the refrain returns at 183–184, and the nymphs who "are departed" (175, 179) are usually taken as Spenser's.

## psalm137
Also: Eliot's line follows the Prayer Book psalter, not the King James; the passage could carry Coverdale's "By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept: when we remembered thee, O Sion", or the note say so, as below. The Part III gloss "leman" now quotes the Prayer Book wording.
note:
The psalm of the exiles in Babylon, asked by their captors for "one of the songs of Zion". Eliot's line follows the Prayer Book wording, "By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept", not the King James "rivers" given here. Leman is Lac Léman, Lake Geneva: Eliot was at Lausanne, on the lake, on leave from the bank for treatment, in the last weeks of 1921, when the poem was finished. The old word "leman", a lover or mistress, has been heard in the name.

## marvell-coy-mistress
note:
Marvell's poem argues that time is short and the lady should yield now. Eliot twice takes "But at my back I always hear" (185, 196) and each time gives it a new object: the rattle of the bones, the horns and motors. His essay "Andrew Marvell" (<i>Times Literary Supplement</i>, March 1921) found in Marvell "a tough reasonableness beneath the slight lyric grace".

## day-parliament-of-bees
date: printed 1641; date of writing unknown
note:
Twelve verse "characters" of bees, satirical portraits in which bees stand for kinds of men. In the third, the ambitious Polypragmus describes the palace he will build, with a roof of forests where, at a sudden "noise of horns and hunting", Actaeon will come upon Diana bathing and be torn by his own hounds (Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> III). In Day "the spring" may be the pool where "all shall see her naked skin"; in Eliot's line it is the season. Eliot's note at 197 quotes the four lines. The only edition is the quarto of 1641; an edition of 1607 was reported by early cataloguers but has never been found, so when Day wrote it is not known.

## verlaine-parsifal
passage, line 7: "rentre sous la tente" (not "sa tente"); trans: "returns to the tent".
note:
Verlaine's sonnet on Wagner's last opera, printed in the <i>Revue wagnérienne</i> of 8 January 1886 among a set of sonnets in homage to Wagner, and collected in <i>Amour</i> (1888). Thirteen lines give the story: Parsifal resists the Flower Maidens and Kundry, wins back the spear, heals King Amfortas and becomes king and priest of the Grail; the last line is the sound of the boys' choir singing in the dome of the Grail temple. Eliot's note at 202 cites the poem; he prints the line with a capital O and a comma after "enfants", and sets it straight after the song about Mrs Porter.

## lyly-campaspe
date: play printed 1584; song first printed 1632
note:
In this song from Lyly's court comedy (Act V scene i) the "ravish'd" nightingale cries "jug, jug" and "tereu", the vocative of Tereus, as if calling the name of the man who raped her. The songs in Lyly's plays are not in the quartos and were first printed in Blount's <i>Sixe Court Comedies</i> of 1632, long after his death; whether Lyly wrote them has been disputed since W. W. Greg questioned it in 1905. Eliot's "'Jug Jug' to dirty ears" (103) and the cry at 203–206 use the convention's words; "So rudely forc'd" (205) repeats line 100.

## sappho-104
note:
Two lines pieced together by editors from two quotations: Demetrius, <i>On Style</i> 141, gives the address to the Evening Star with its three "you bring" as an example of graceful repetition, and the Byzantine lexicon <i>Etymologicum Magnum</i> preserves the words "all that shining Dawn scattered". Byron imitated the lines in <i>Don Juan</i> III.107 ("O, Hesperus! thou bringest all good things"). Eliot's note at 221 says his lines "may not appear as exact as Sappho's lines" and that he had in mind the "longshore" or "dory" fisherman coming in at nightfall; "brings the sailor home from sea" also echoes Stevenson's "Requiem", "Home is the sailor, home from sea".

## goldsmith-vicar
Also: the passage's "soothe" and "is—to die" differ from the Gutenberg text's "sooth" and "bosom—is to die"; editions differ, and the gloss "lovely-woman" carries the same text, so settle both together or leave both.
note:
Olivia Primrose, seduced by Squire Thornhill and abandoned, sings this at her mother's request, on the spot where she first met him. Eliot's note at 253 cites the song; he takes its first line, adds "and", and rhymes it into a pentameter quatrain in which the typist, instead of dying, puts on a record. The lines have been read both as contempt for her and as pity.

## froude-elizabeth
passage: In the afternoon we were in a barge, watching the games on the river. She was alone with the Lord Robert and myself on the poop, when they began to talk nonsense, and went so far that Lord Robert at last said, as I was on the spot there was no reason why they should not be married if the queen pleased.
(Froude's words, checked against the 1911 Everyman page; the present passage is Eliot's note, with "(The queen)" for "She" and "the" dropped. The Part III gloss "elizabeth-leicester" now quotes Froude.)
note:
Froude prints, in his own translation, a letter of 30 June 1561 from the Spanish ambassador, Álvaro de la Quadra, to Philip II, reporting an afternoon on the river with the queen and Lord Robert Dudley, later Earl of Leicester. Eliot's note at 279 quotes it, with "(The queen)" for Froude's "She"; his "Vol. I, ch. iv" is the Everyman's Library edition, where the letter is on p. 244. Dudley's wife, Amy Robsart, had been found dead at the foot of a staircase in September 1560, and the marriage never took place.

## augustine-confessions
note:
Book III opens with Augustine's arrival as a student in Carthage, "where there sang all around me in my ears a cauldron of unholy loves" (Pusey); the Latin plays on Carthago and sartago, a frying-pan. Book X.34, on the lust of the eyes, confesses that visible beauty still catches him and that God plucks him out. Eliot's notes at 307 and 309 cite both; the wording of his first quotation is his own, not Pusey's, and his second note says that placing Augustine beside the Buddha, "these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism", "is not an accident". Pusey's translation of 1838 was the one in Everyman's Library.

## buddha-fire-sermon
note:
Preached, in the Mahāvagga's narrative, on Gayā Head to a thousand monks who had been matted-hair ascetics and tenders of the sacred fire, soon after the enlightenment: every sense and its objects are on fire with passion, hatred and infatuation. Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (Harvard Oriental Series, 1896; §73, pp. 351–353), which Eliot's note names, was the standard English anthology of the Pali texts when Eliot studied Sanskrit and Indian philosophy at Harvard in 1911–13, and it is generally assumed he met the sermon there. His note says the sermon "corresponds in importance to the Sermon on the Mount"; it gives Part III its title and, at 308 and 311, its "burning".

## fisher
Lines 173–198: the "I" who sings Spenser's refrain (Eliot's note at 176), fishes in the canal, and muses, in words adapted from Ferdinand, on "the king my father's death" (note at 192). Usually taken as the Fisher King of Weston's book, whom Eliot's notes to the title and to 46 invoke.

## song
The soldiers' song about Mrs Porter (199–201; Eliot's note calls it a ballad "reported to me from Sydney, Australia"); the line from Verlaine's <i>Parsifal</i> (202); the rag (128–130).

## nightingale
Lines 203–206: the nightingale's cry as the Elizabethans spelt it, "jug jug" and "Tereu" (Lyly's song), around "So rudely forc'd" from line 100.

## walker
The "I" of the City passages: sees the crowd flow over London Bridge in the winter dawn and stops Stetson (60–76); is accosted by Mr. Eugenides in the winter noon (207–211); hears the music along the Strand and up Queen Victoria Street, and the mandoline by the bar in Lower Thames Street (257–265). At 257 he speaks Ferdinand's line (Eliot's note: "The Tempest, as above"), which the fisher had adapted at 191–192.

## tiresias
Lines 215–251 and 253–256. Ovid's Theban seer (Eliot's note at 218 quotes the passage): blind, once a woman for seven years, he says he "can see" the typist's evening and has "foresuffered all". The same note calls him "a mere spectator and not indeed a 'character'" and yet "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest".
