# ways.txt

## Follow the river
Decisions: no single journey downriver (both); "gashouse" is the poem's word; the places given in the section's order with their lines.
title: Follow the river
body:
The section's places in the order it gives them: a canal behind a gashouse (189–190), the Cannon Street Hotel (213), the Strand, Queen Victoria Street and Lower Thames Street with St Magnus Martyr (258–265), Greenwich Reach and the Isle of Dogs (275–276), and in the daughters' speeches Highbury, Richmond and Kew, Moorgate and Margate (292–300). The map has the real course of the Thames.

## Spenser first, then the litter
Decisions: the reader would keep this; the scholar corrects "takes away the nymphs" (Eliot keeps them and says they are departed) and the title's joke. Merged, keeping the reader's "read this, then that".
title: Spenser's refrain
body:
Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596) is a poem for a double wedding on the Thames, and its refrain, "Sweete Themmes runne softly, till I end my Song", closes each stanza. Read its first stanza, then lines 173–184. Eliot takes the refrain at 176 and 183 and varies it at 184; Spenser's nymphs gather flowers by the river, Eliot's "are departed", and his river "bears no" bottles, papers or cigarette ends.

## See who speaks
Decisions: the superlatives cut (the scholar counted: Part III has fewer quotation marks than I or II); the Tiresias claim given as what the note says; the unnamed speakers named as the "I" of each place.
title: See who speaks
body:
The Voices lens marks each speaker and each quoted voice: the "I" who fishes by the canal, the Mrs. Porter song, Verlaine's line, the nightingale, Mr. Eugenides (reported, not quoted), Tiresias, the typist, the "I" of Lower Thames Street, the three Thames-daughters, Augustine and the Buddha. Tiresias speaks only in this section; Eliot's note calls him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest".

## The Fire Sermon itself
Decisions: Harvard dropped here (it is in the carthage gloss, as an assumption); what the sermon says given; "burning" as the poem's word for Warren's "on fire" (scholar); "before the section, not after" and the list of four cut; the reader's "It is short" kept.
title: The Fire Sermon itself
body:
Eliot's note sends the reader to the Buddha's Fire Sermon in Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896) and calls it as important as the Sermon on the Mount. It is short. In it the Buddha says of the eye, the ear, the tongue, the body and the mind that each "is on fire", with the fire of passion, of hatred and of infatuation. The section's title is the sermon's, and "burning", five times at 308–311, is the poem's word for the sermon's "on fire".

## Pound's pencil on the typist
Decisions: "describes", not "shows" (both); Fresca named as a woman; the Pound phrase shortened to the rights rule.
title: Pound's pencil on the typist
body:
In the typescript the typist scene was longer, in regular quatrains, and Pound cut it heavily, writing in the margin that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it. The drafts page describes his marks, and the seventy or so lines of couplets in Pope's manner, about a fashionable woman called Fresca, that opened the section until he struck them out.

## The Rhine-daughters, then the Thames-daughters
Decisions: the site's listening room has the scene (the Solti recording, "Act III Scene 1"), so "listen, then read" stays, as the reader wanted for a Listen way; "swimming rhythm" cut; where Eliot takes the refrain added (scholar).
title: The Rhine-daughters, then the Thames-daughters
body:
Act III of Wagner's <i>Götterdämmerung</i> opens with the three Rhine-daughters swimming and singing for their stolen gold; "Weialala leia, wallala leialala" is their refrain, and the Thames-daughters take it at 277–278 and 290–291. Listen to the scene, then read lines 266–306. Eliot's note says his three daughters "speak in turn" from 292 to 306.

## Margate
Decisions: "much of this section" corrected to Eliot's own "some fifty lines"; dates; the shelter as the listed identification (scholar); the reader's ending kept.
title: Margate
body:
"On Margate Sands. / I can connect / Nothing with nothing." Eliot spent three weeks at Margate, from late October to mid-November 1921, on three months' sick leave from Lloyds Bank. In letters from there he said he had drafted "part of part III", "some fifty lines", sitting in a shelter on the front. The Nayland Rock shelter on the seafront, listed in 2009 as the likely place, still stands. The map has it.

## Fire
Decisions: "Watch the river ... and then burn" cut (both); the title and Eliot's note brought in (scholar); the reader's plain observation kept.
title: Fire
body:
The Water lens marks fire in a third colour. In this section it falls on the last four lines only, where "burning" comes five times, from the Buddha's sermon of the title, after Augustine's "To Carthage then I came". Eliot's note says the "collocation" of the two "is not an accident".

The nightingale (here at 203–206, and in Part II), the gulls of Part IV, the cock and the hermit-thrush of Part V, and the swallow at 429. The Birds pathway takes them in order, with recordings where they exist.

## Tiresias in Ovid
Decisions: what the Latin says given (both wanted it); "nineteen lines" kept, since it is a plain count of what the note contains and the scholar verified it; the second-person close cut.
title: Tiresias in Ovid
body:
Eliot's note at 218 quotes nineteen lines of Ovid (<i>Metamorphoses</i> III, 320–338): Tiresias, who had lived seven years as a woman, is asked to settle Jupiter and Juno's dispute over which sex has the greater pleasure in love; Juno blinds him for his answer and Jupiter gives him foresight. The library has the Latin with an English version. Read it, then the typist scene, which Tiresias says he has "foresuffered".

## Whose side is the poem on
Decisions: "smoothes" (the 1922 spelling the site keeps); "argued for a century", "the only mercy" and "once each way" cut; Goldsmith brought in (scholar); the two readings given as readings with what each rests on (reader). The title kept, since it is a plain question and the way is an "ask".
title: Whose side is the poem on
body:
The typist "smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone" (255–256). Eliot's note gives Goldsmith's song, in which the woman who "stoops to folly" has nothing left but to die; the typist does not. The scene has been read as the poem's contempt for her and as its nearest approach to pity; those who read it as pity point to Tiresias's "foresuffered all" (243).

# paths.txt

## first / 215
text: Tiresias, who in Ovid was a woman for seven years, watches the typist and her visitor. Eliot's note at 218 says what Tiresias sees "is the substance of the poem", a claim critics have disputed.

## first / 266
text: The Thames-daughters' song, in short lines, with the refrain of Wagner's Rhine-daughters (Eliot's note gives <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, III, i). Worth reading aloud.

## water / 176
text: "Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song": Spenser's refrain from the <i>Prothalamion</i>, a wedding poem. Here the nymphs "are departed" and the river "bears no" litter.

## water / 182
text: "By the waters of Leman I sat down and wept." Psalm 137's Babylon, with Lake Geneva (Lac Léman) in its place; Eliot finished the poem at Lausanne, on the lake, in the winter of 1921–22.

## water / 189
text: "While I was fishing in the dull canal", behind the gashouse. The fisher is usually taken as Weston's Fisher King; "the king my father's death" is Ferdinand's in <i>The Tempest</i> (Eliot's note).

## water / 266
text: "The river sweats / Oil and tar." The working Thames of Eliot's day, then the queen's barge in 1561, from the ambassador's letter that Eliot's note quotes from Froude.

## women / 199
text: Mrs Porter and her daughter, from a bawdy song of Australian troops that Eliot's note says reached him from Sydney.

## women / 222
text: The typist, who "smoothes her hair with automatic hand."

## women / 279
text: Elizabeth, in a barge with Leicester, "talking nonsense" (the Spanish ambassador's words, in Eliot's note).

## women / 292
text: The three Thames-daughters, who Eliot's note says speak in turn: from Highbury, Moorgate and Margate. The first speaks in the shape of La Pia's line in Dante, "Siena made me, Maremma unmade me" (Eliot's note at 293).

## cities / 182
text: Leman is Lake Geneva. Eliot was at Lausanne, on the lake, from late November 1921 to early January 1922, and finished the poem there.

## cities / 209

## cities / 213

## cities / 245

# drafts.txt

## fresca
Decisions: the 1946 quotation reduced to a phrase (rights rule, both); Swift as the critics' comparison, Pope as Eliot's own account (scholar); Richardson named as a novelist (reader); Rainey's dating of the printed opening added here, where it belongs.
title: The seventy lines about Fresca
body:
In the typescript "The Fire Sermon" opened with some seventy lines of heroic couplets on a fashionable woman, Fresca: her waking, her breakfast in bed, her visit to the "needful stool" with a novel of Richardson's, her letter, her bath, her reading. Eliot said later that it was written in imitation of Pope's <i>Rape of the Lock</i>; critics also hear Swift's dressing-room poems. Pound struck it out. Eliot recalled in 1946 that Pound's objection was that Pope had done this so well it could not be done better, and that one cannot parody Pope without writing better verse than Pope, "and you can't". The printed section begins with the river; on Rainey's dating of the drafts its opening lines, 173–182, were the last written for the poem, in Paris in January 1922.

## typist-draft
Decisions: the scholar's account of what the draft had (the clerk's age, face, hair, manners, boasts; the typist's kimono and print), which agrees with the editor's own memory of the facsimile; "Perhaps be damned" placed against "Perhaps a cheap house agent's clerk"; Pound's Tiresias comment described, not quoted at length, and not placed by line; the London passage stood before the scene, not after; the dots at 248; "the ones everyone quotes" cut. Quotation kept to phrases.
title: Pound and the typist
body:
In the typescript the scene ran in regular quatrains and was longer. The clerk had his age, his spotty face, his greasy hair, his table manners and his boasts; the typist a kimono and a Japanese print; and Tiresias hedged, "Perhaps a cheap house agent's clerk", one of those loiterers who might have been seen anywhere. Pound wrote that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it, "Perhaps be damned" against the hedge, and beside the loiterers told Tiresias to make up his mind whether he knew or not. The clerk's exit, a couplet in which he stopped at a corner to urinate and spit, was marked by Pound and dropped; the dots at 248 fall where it stood. A passage before the scene addressed to London ("London, the swarming life you kill and breed") went too.

## thames-draft
Decisions: title changed ("lengthened and shortened" describes nothing); the Margate identification brought in (scholar); Pound's "OK" and "echt" kept without a placing by line; the first daughter's speech shortened by Eliot (both scholars, from memory; stated without figures); the note "added, like all the notes" (scholar).
title: The Thames-daughters in draft
body:
The river song, the Elizabeth stanza and the three daughters' speeches are the part of the poem generally taken to be the "some fifty lines" Eliot drafted at Margate in November 1921, and they reached print with few marks from Pound; the words he wrote here, "OK" and "echt" (genuine), approve. Eliot's own revision shortened the first daughter's speech. His note identifying three daughters who "speak in turn" from 292 to 306 was added, like all the notes, for the book edition of December 1922.
