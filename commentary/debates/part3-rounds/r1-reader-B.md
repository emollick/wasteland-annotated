## typist

### Verdict
Rewrite.

### Reads as a model wrote it
- "The most argued-over scene in the poem." — superlative nobody could check, as an opening fragment.
- "A typist in a bedsit, her underwear ... ; a 'small house agent's clerk,' ... ; a seduction that is really an assault met with indifference." — one sentence of clauses stacked on semicolons; the last clause is a reading passed off as summary.
- "blesses the clerk's exit with a rhyme" — annotator's wit; I could not find the rhyme it means.
- "the rhymes are doing the judging" — metaphor describing the poem instead of explaining it.
- "Against Eliot's tentative 'perhaps' in one line he wrote 'Perhaps be damned.'" — anecdote for colour; does nothing for the line.
- "What survived is the poem's cruellest writing" — superlative nobody could check.
- "the cruelty is aimed as much at the eighteenth-century manner it imitates as at the two people on the divan" — balanced antithesis; a reading dressed as fact.
- "Whether Tiresias is compassionate or a voyeur is a question the poem asks about you." — second-person flourish; bow-tying last sentence.

### Unclear or unhelpful
- "house agent" is not glossed (an estate agent); a non-British reader needs it.
- The Bradford parenthesis says the wool towns made fortunes but not that a silk hat is a gentleman's top hat, which is the point of the simile.
- Nothing on "Thebes below the wall" or "the lowest of the dead" (245–246), inside this note's range; I wanted both.
- "eighteenth-century manner": what manner? I cannot tell what is meant or whether to believe it.
- "rhymed quatrains, abab": true of 231–246; 247–252 are looser. Fine as a description, but "mostly" would be honest.
- Two Pound quotations where one, or none, would do.

### Keep
- The word glosses: combinations (one-piece underwear), stays (corset), carbuncular (pimpled), dinner from tins, the divan a bed by night.
- Bradford as the Yorkshire wool town that made wartime fortunes.
- That the passage is in rhymed quatrains, abab.
- "foresuffered": Tiresias has been through it before.
- One Pound phrase, if the scholar wants any: "verse not interesting enough as verse to warrant so much of it", to say the draft ran longer and was cut.
- That Tiresias's attitude is disputed, stated as a dispute and not put to me.

### Proposed body
A typist in a rented room. "Combinations" are one-piece underwear and "stays" a corset; her supper comes out of tins; the divan is her bed at night. Her visitor is a clerk in a house agent's (estate agent's) office, "carbuncular" meaning pimpled. Bradford, the Yorkshire wool town, made new millionaires in the war, and a silk hat is a gentleman's top hat. The passage is mostly in rhymed quatrains, abab. "Foresuffered" means Tiresias has been through all this before. Thebes is the city where he prophesied, and he is among the dead in Hades when Odysseus consults him (<i>Odyssey</i> XI). The draft ran longer; Pound wrote beside it "verse not interesting enough as verse to warrant so much of it," and Eliot cut it. Whether Tiresias's watching is pity or voyeurism has been read both ways.

## lovely-woman

### Verdict
Trim.

### Reads as a model wrote it
- "The gramophone is the poem's newest object, and 'automatic' is its most modern word" — two superlatives nobody could check.
- ": the eighteenth-century ballad meter runs on into a room where nobody feels anything and a machine does the singing" — colon-led metaphor describing the poem; "a machine does the singing" is the annotator's wit.
- "Whether you hear that as contempt for her or relief for her decides how you read the poem." — second-person flourish; bow-tying last sentence.
- Title "Goldsmith's song, and a gramophone" — the tacked-on ", and a [thing]" flourish; the same tic in "The Queen and Lord Robert, in a barge" and "Augustine and the Buddha, burning".

### Unclear or unhelpful

- The note does not say the plain thing the quote shows: line 253 is Goldsmith's first line taken whole, and the stanza then goes Eliot's own way.
- "In Goldsmith the fallen woman's only art is to die. In Eliot she ..." is a balanced antithesis, but it is the point of the echo; keep it in plainer words.

### Keep
- Olivia's song, The Vicar of Wakefield (1766), chapter 24, sung after her seduction.
- The contrast: in Goldsmith she can only die; here she puts on a record.
- That the tone is disputed, said as a dispute.

### Proposed body
Plainer title — Goldsmith's song
Olivia's song in <i>The Vicar of Wakefield</i> (1766), chapter 24, sung after her seduction. Eliot takes Goldsmith's first line whole and finishes the stanza his own way. In Goldsmith the betrayed woman has nothing left but to die; the typist smooths her hair and puts a record on. The contrast has been read as contempt for her and as sympathy with her.

## strand

### Verdict
Rewrite.

- "The mandoline is real; so is the pub. And so is St Magnus the Martyr" — rhythmic triple; "real" is a claim nobody could check.

### Unclear or unhelpful
- "Ferdinand's line from The Tempest again": "again" assumes I know the same speech is behind 191–192; nothing says so.

- "which runs from Blackfriars to the Bank": I needed the direction only (east into the City, down to the river).
- "The mandoline is real; so is the pub": on what evidence? A reading dressed as fact.

### Keep
- The Tempest, I, ii, Ferdinand's own line; "as above" points to Eliot's note on 192.

- St Magnus the Martyr: Wren, Lower Thames Street, foot of the old London Bridge, rebuilt 1671–87, white and gold, Ionic columns.

### Proposed body
Ferdinand's line in <i>The Tempest</i>, I, ii, as he follows Ariel's music; the same speech is behind lines 191–192, which is what Eliot's "as above" refers to. The Strand, Queen Victoria Street and Lower Thames Street run east into the City and down to the river. Billingsgate fish market stood on Lower Thames Street, hence the "fishmen." St Magnus the Martyr is Wren's church on the same street, at the foot of the old London Bridge, rebuilt 1671–87; "Ionian" is the Ionic order of its columns, and the interior is white and gold. In 1920 a commission under Lord Phillimore proposed demolishing nineteen City churches, St Magnus among them. The London County Council's report against the scheme is the pamphlet Eliot's note cites. The church is still there.

## thames-daughters

### Verdict
Trim.

### Reads as a model wrote it
- "First the river as it was in 1921: sweating oil and tar, worked by sailing barges with red sails drifting down Greenwich Reach past the Isle of Dogs" — colon-led paraphrase of the lines I have just read.
- "The lines shorten to two and three words, a water-rhythm, and the river's own noise takes over." — counting the words; two metaphors describing the poem instead of explaining it.

### Unclear or unhelpful
- "the loop of docks on the river's north bank": is the loop the docks or the river? The Isle of Dogs is the peninsula inside a loop of the river.
- The second sentence restates Eliot's note, which is printed beside it.
- "as it was in 1921": why that year? Say "when the poem was written" or drop it.
- I wanted a word on why the sails are red (Thames sailing barges, rust-red dressed sails) and on the "heavy spar" (the sprit). Both are mine to propose and the scholar's to confirm.

### Keep
- The refrain is the Rhine-daughters' in Götterdämmerung, III, i; they swim and lament their stolen gold.
- Greenwich Reach and the Isle of Dogs placed on the map.

### Proposed body
The refrain is sung by the three Rhine-daughters in Wagner's <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, Act III scene i, as they swim and lament the gold stolen from them at the start of the <i>Ring</i>; Eliot's note makes the three speeches from line 292 their Thames counterparts. The barges are Thames sailing barges, whose sails were dressed red-brown; the "heavy spar" is the sprit that carries the mainsail. Greenwich Reach is the stretch of river at Greenwich, and the Isle of Dogs the peninsula opposite, inside a loop of the river, then given over to docks.

### Verdict

- "Cleopatra's barge again": I had to work out that "again" means line 77 and Eliot's note there. Say so.

### Proposed body
Plainer title — Elizabeth and Dudley
Eliot's note quotes Froude's <i>History of England</i>, which quotes a dispatch to Philip II from Álvaro de la Quadra, the Spanish ambassador, describing the queen on the river with Robert Dudley, whom she later made Earl of Leicester. The marriage never happened; Elizabeth never married. The gilded stern and the red and gold recall Enobarbus's description of Cleopatra's barge in <i>Antony and Cleopatra</i>, which Eliot's note on line 77 cites. The "White towers" have been taken as the Tower of London.

## highbury

### Proposed body
Plainer title — La Pia
<i>Purgatorio</i> V, 133–136. La Pia dei Tolomei of Siena, murdered, it was said, by her husband at his castle in the Maremma, asks Dante to remember her. The first Thames-daughter's sentence is modelled on hers: Siena made me, Maremma unmade me; Highbury bore me, Richmond and Kew undid me. Highbury is a suburb in north London. Richmond and Kew are on the Thames above London, where people went boating. La Pia gives her name; this speaker has none.

## margate

### Verdict
Rewrite.

### Reads as a model wrote it
- Title "Where the poem was written" — a tour-guide heading of the "Where the poem is standing" kind; and part of Part III was drafted there, not the poem.
- "he sat in a public shelter on the front, the Nayland Rock shelter, which still stands, and wrote, he told Sydney Schiff on 4 November, 'a rough draft of part of part III.'" — clauses stacked on semicolons and commas.
- "is the daughter's confession and the poet's" — a reading dressed as fact; balanced pairing.
- "are what he saw on the beach at the end of the season" — a reading dressed as fact; nobody knows what he saw.
- "The song runs down to 'la la' and stops." — metaphor describing the poem; bow-tying last sentence.

### Unclear or unhelpful
- "the Nayland Rock shelter": is the shelter Eliot's own detail (I believe the same letter says he wrote sitting in a shelter on the front), and is Nayland Rock a later local identification? The note runs them together as one fact, and I cannot tell how much to believe.
- "on three months' sick leave" against "stayed until mid-November": three months of leave, three weeks at Margate; say which is which.
- "at the Albemarle Hotel in Cliftonville": did not help me with the line.
- "My people humble people who expect / Nothing": I wanted to know who "my people" are and got what Eliot saw instead.

### Keep
- Margate, Kent, at the mouth of the Thames estuary.
- Eliot there in October–November 1921 on sick leave from the bank; the letter to Sydney Schiff, 4 November 1921, "a rough draft of part of part III".
- The shelter on the front, if it is Eliot's own detail; that it still stands, in a clause.
- "I can connect / Nothing with nothing" as commonly read for the poet as well, offered as a reading.

### Proposed body
Plainer title — Margate, 1921
Margate is a seaside resort in Kent at the mouth of the Thames estuary. Eliot spent three weeks there in October and November 1921, on sick leave from the bank, and on 4 November wrote to Sydney Schiff that he had done "a rough draft of part of part III," working, he said, in a shelter on the front; the Nayland Rock shelter, which still stands, is the one identified. "I can connect / Nothing with nothing" is often read as the poet's line as well as the third Thames-daughter's.

## carthage

### Verdict
Rewrite.

### Reads as a model wrote it
- "The Latin puns: Carthago, sartago, a frying pan." — fragment on a colon.
- "Then the Buddha's Fire Sermon ..." / "Then Augustine again" — walk-through framing.
- "The lines fall apart as you read them: a sentence, a word four times, a prayer, a prayer with its end torn off, one word alone." — the owner's own example: counting flourish, second person, a figure for the poem.
- "The fire that burns here is lust. The fire at line 428 refines." — the owner's own example: the "X is Y. Z is W." epigram to close.
- Eliot's note quoted back ("these two representatives ... is not an accident") when it is printed beside the line.
- Title "Augustine and the Buddha, burning" — the tacked-on ", burning".

### Unclear or unhelpful
- "given in Eliot's note in his own wording; E. B. Pusey's translation (1838), which is behind it, reads ...": the point (the poem's line and the note's are Eliot's wording, not Pusey's) took me two readings, and the quote field already gives Pusey.
- "threw himself into love affairs and the theatre": the annotator's phrase for what Augustine says himself in the pages that follow.
- "Eliot read it at Harvard": when, and as what? One clause (as a student, if the scholar confirms the years).
- "The fire at line 428 refines": I wanted the cross-reference plainly, what line 428 is and whose fire.

### Keep
- Confessions, Book III, opening; that the poem's wording and the note's are Eliot's own; Pusey in the quote.
- The Carthago/sartago pun, which is Augustine's wit and explains "cauldron".
- The Fire Sermon as the source of "burning" and of the title; the Warren quotation; Warren (1896).
- Book X for "Thou pluckest me out", with the quotation.
- The cross-reference to line 428, as one plain sentence.

### Proposed body
Plainer title — Augustine and the Buddha
The opening of Book III of Augustine's <i>Confessions</i> (c. 400). The line, like the version in Eliot's note, is Eliot's own wording; Pusey's translation (1838) is given above. Augustine puns on Carthago and sartago, a frying pan, which is Pusey's "cauldron." He had come to Carthage as a student, and Book III goes on to his love affairs and his passion for the theatre. "Burning" is from the Buddha's Fire Sermon, which gives this part of the poem its title, in Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896), which Eliot read as a student at Harvard: "All things, O priests, are on fire . . . The eye, O priests, is on fire; forms are on fire . . . with the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation." "Thou pluckest me out" is Augustine again, Book X: "I . . . entangle my steps with these outward beauties; but Thou pluckest me out, O Lord, Thou pluckest me out." At line 428 the fire is Dante's, the fire of purgatory that refines.
