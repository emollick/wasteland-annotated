# Round 2, editor's rewrites, batch B (glosses typist to carthage)

How these were made: the scholar's corrections of fact are taken (the scholar has the last word on substance), the reader's cuts and wording are taken (the reader has the last word on wording). Where the two wanted different things the decision is noted. Fixed fields untouched; only `title`, `body` and, where marked, `quote` change.

## typist
Decisions: the stays moved from the window to the divan (scholar; the poem's own placing); the word glosses the reader asked for; the quatrain claim corrected to what the text does; Eliot's note at 218 pointed to and kept distinct from what the lines say; one Pound phrase, not two; the "may pass / allows" detail left out of the gloss, since the two scholars place Pound's comment differently and neither has the facsimile; all superlatives, "the rhymes are doing the judging", "blesses ... with a rhyme" and the second-person close cut. The two readings of Tiresias given as readings.

title: The typist and the clerk
body:
The typist's room is a bedsit: food from tins, the divan her bed at night, her "combinations" (a one-piece undergarment) drying out of the window, her "stays" (a corset) piled on the divan with the stockings and camisoles. Her visitor is a "small house agent's clerk", an estate agent's clerk, pimply ("carbuncular"), with the assurance of new money: Bradford, the Yorkshire wool town, stood for fortunes made in the war, and a silk hat is a gentleman's top hat. Eliot's note at line 218 says that what Tiresias sees "is the substance of the poem"; here Tiresias says he has "foresuffered all" and foretells the rest, and the lines themselves say "assaults", "no defence" and "indifference".

In the typescript the scene ran in regular quatrains rhyming abab, and Pound cut several stanzas, writing beside it that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it. What survives keeps the quatrains from 231 to 246 and loosens them before and after. Critics have read Tiresias's watching as compassion and as voyeurism.

## lovely-woman
Decisions: the scholar's correction of the metre claim (Goldsmith four beats, Eliot five; the first line taken whole with "and" added); the chapter's setting in one clause; the superlatives and the "machine does the singing" figure cut; the two readings given as readings. The quote field is left as it is: Gutenberg's text reads "sooth" and "bosom—is to die", the site "soothe" and "is—to die"; editions differ, and the library record (another thread's) has the same wording as the gloss, so the two should be settled together (noted in the shared proposals).

title: Goldsmith's song
body:
Olivia Primrose's song in <i>The Vicar of Wakefield</i> (1766), chapter 24, sung to her family some days after her father has brought her home from the squire who seduced her. Eliot takes Goldsmith's first line and adds "and" to carry it into his own longer line; Goldsmith's lines have four beats, Eliot's five. In the song the only art left to the fallen woman is to die. Eliot's typist "smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone." The scene has been read as contempt for her and as a kind of mercy: she is not required to die.

## strand
Decisions: it is the music that travels along the Strand, not a walker (scholar); St Magnus's dates from the pamphlet Eliot cites; the Phillimore commission and the LCC reply as the scholar found them, with the pamphlet's verdict on the interior, which Eliot's note answers; "Ionian" tied to the Ionic columns (reader); Billingsgate's closing date; the redecoration of 1924 in a clause. Eliot's Dial letter of 1921 left out as more than the line needs. "The mandoline is real; so is the pub" and the "street of fish" close cut.

title: From the Strand to Lower Thames Street
body:
Ferdinand's line from <i>The Tempest</i> again (Eliot's note: "as above"), and then the music's course through the City: along the Strand, up Queen Victoria Street, which runs from Blackfriars to the Bank, and down to Lower Thames Street, where Billingsgate fish market stood until 1982 and the "fishmen" drink at noon. St Magnus the Martyr is Wren's church at the north end of the old London Bridge, rebuilt after the Great Fire and finished in 1676, with the steeple added in 1705; "Ionian" is usually taken to mean the Ionic columns that divide its nave from the aisles. In April 1920 a commission appointed by the Bishop of London and chaired by Lord Phillimore recommended removing nineteen City churches, St Magnus among them. The London County Council's reply is the pamphlet Eliot's note cites, <i>The Proposed Demolition of Nineteen City Churches</i> (October 1920): it calls St Magnus "one of the most beautiful of all Wren's works" but finds the interior "disappointing", which Eliot's note answers with "one of the finest among Wren's interiors". The church stands; its interior was redecorated in 1924.

## thames-daughters
Decisions: Wagner as both had it; Eliot's note kept in one sentence because the gloss hangs on the line the note is about; the river of "Eliot's day" rather than "1921" (scholar); the Isle of Dogs and Greenwich Reach placed exactly (scholar); the reader's two facts, the red-brown dressed sails and the sprit as the "heavy spar", added for the scholar to confirm; "a water-rhythm" and "the river's own noise" cut.

title: The Rhine-daughters on the Thames
body:
"Weialala leia / Wallala leialala" is the refrain of the three Rhine-daughters at the opening of Act III of Wagner's <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, as they swim and sing of the gold stolen from them at the start of the <i>Ring</i>. Eliot's note says the Song of the Thames-daughters begins here and that from line 292 to 306 they speak in turn. Lines 266–276 give the working river of Eliot's day: oil and tar, and sailing barges, their sails dressed red-brown and the "heavy spar" the sprit that carries the mainsail, drifting with the tide down Greenwich Reach, the stretch opposite Greenwich, past the Isle of Dogs, the peninsula of docks on the north bank.

## elizabeth-leicester
Decisions: the letter dated and its occasion given (scholar, from Froude and the Simancas calendar); the quote field restored to Froude's words, which the 1911 Everyman page confirms ("She was alone with the Lord Robert and myself on the poop"; the parenthesis "(The queen)" is Eliot's); Amy Robsart's death in a clause; the barge's furniture marked as Eliot's, not the letter's; "White towers" hedged; Cleopatra's barge given as a critics' comparison tied to line 77; the "closes the sixteenth century" sentence cut; the title plainer.

title: Elizabeth and Leicester on the river
quote: In the afternoon we were in a barge, watching the games on the river. She was alone with the Lord Robert and myself on the poop, when they began to talk nonsense, and went so far that Lord Robert at last said, as I was on the spot there was no reason why they should not be married if the queen pleased.
body:
Eliot's note quotes Froude's <i>History of England</i> (the volume on Elizabeth, chapter iv), which translates a letter of 30 June 1561 from Álvaro de la Quadra, Philip II's ambassador in London, reporting a water party at which Elizabeth and Robert Dudley, made Earl of Leicester three years later, joked in his hearing about marrying. Dudley's wife had died the year before in a fall that left him under suspicion, and the marriage never took place. The gilded stern, the red and gold, the south-west wind and the peal of bells are not in the letter; the "White towers" are not identified, and are usually taken as the Tower of London. Critics have set the passage beside the "burnished throne" of line 77, Shakespeare's Cleopatra in her barge.

## highbury
Decisions: La Pia as the scholar had her (the early commentators' identification, nothing certain known); the two sentences set side by side (reader); Highbury and the river placed plainly; "Trams and dusty trees" marked as the daughter's own first line; the counting close cut; the draft's longer version moved to the drafts record.

title: La Pia's line
body:
<i>Purgatorio</i> V, 133–136. Among the late-repentant who died by violence, a woman gives Dante her name, her birthplace and the place of her death in one sentence, and points at her husband without naming him. The early commentators made her Pia de' Tolomei of Siena, killed on her husband's orders at his castle in the Maremma; nothing certain is known of her. The first Thames-daughter's speech (lines 292–295) keeps the shape of Dante's line, born in one place and undone in another: Highbury, in north London ("Trams and dusty trees" is her own first line); Richmond and Kew, on the river above London, where Londoners went boating. She gives no name.

## moorgate
Decisions: Moorgate placed (scholar's gate in the wall, reader's business district); "the event" and "a new start" glossed; everything else, which was the annotator's response to the lines, cut.

title: The second daughter
body:
The second of the three Thames-daughters of Eliot's note speaks from Moorgate, a street and district on the north edge of the City of London, the business district, named from a gate in the old wall. "The event" is the seduction; "a new start" is his phrase, reported without comment.

## margate
Decisions: title changed ("Where the poem was written" is the kind of heading the owner objected to, and untrue as stated); dates and the shelter as the scholar found them (the letter says "a shelter on the front"; Nayland Rock is the listed identification); the Schiff letter kept; which lines the Margate draft was, as usually identified; "Nothing with nothing" as Lyndall Gordon's reading; the beach sentence and "runs down to 'la la'" cut. The scholar's memory of the draft's "dirty hand" is left out as unverified.

title: Margate Sands
body:
Margate, on the north Kent coast where the Thames estuary opens to the sea, is where Eliot went in the autumn of 1921, on three months' leave from Lloyds Bank after a breakdown; he stayed about three weeks, from late October to mid-November, at the Albemarle Hotel, Cliftonville. On 4 November he told Sydney Schiff he had done "a rough draft of part of part III", written sitting in a shelter on the front; the Nayland Rock shelter on the promenade, listed in 2009 as the likely one, still stands. The part is usually identified as the pencil drafts of lines 266–311, from "The river sweats" to "burning". Lyndall Gordon and others have read "I can connect / Nothing with nothing" as Eliot's own confession as well as the third daughter's.

## carthage
Decisions: Pusey's sentence is in the quote field, so the body does not repeat it; the pun kept as Augustine's; the Harvard claim reduced to what is known (Lanman, 1911–13) and what is assumed; the Warren quotation kept, since a reader wants the words; Book X kept; the Dante fire at 428 given as a critics' connection; the counting sentence and the "lust / refines" close cut; title plainer.

title: Augustine and the Buddha
body:
The opening of Book III of Augustine's <i>Confessions</i> (c. 400), in Pusey's translation (1838), given above; the line, like Eliot's note, rewords it. The Latin has a pun, Carthago and sartago, a frying pan. Augustine had come to Carthage at about seventeen to study rhetoric, and Book III begins with his love affairs and the theatre. "Burning" is from the Buddha's Fire Sermon, which gives the section its title: "All things, O priests, are on fire . . . The eye, O priests, is on fire; forms are on fire . . . with the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation." Eliot's note sends readers to Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896), in the Harvard series edited by Charles Lanman, under whom Eliot studied Sanskrit and Pali in 1911–13; it is generally assumed he met the sermon there. "O Lord Thou pluckest me out" is Augustine again, Book X: "I . . . entangle my steps with these outward beauties; but Thou pluckest me out, O Lord, Thou pluckest me out." Eliot's note says that placing "these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism" together "as the culmination of this part of the poem, is not an accident." Critics have connected this burning with the refining fire of <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, quoted at line 428.
