# Round 3, editor's final texts, batch C (ways.txt, paths.txt, drafts.txt)

How these were made: the round-2 texts with the scholar's four corrections (Margate letter, bird order, the Ovid library record, "began to talk nonsense") and the reader's rewordings, all taken. Alterations to the reader's wording are named under Decisions. Items not listed are unchanged (both sides passed them in round 2).

# ways.txt

## Follow the river
Decisions: the reader's text, without the bracketed line numbers.
title: Follow the river
body:
The section names, in order, a canal behind a gashouse, the Cannon Street Hotel, the Strand, Queen Victoria Street and Lower Thames Street with St Magnus Martyr, Greenwich Reach and the Isle of Dogs, and in the daughters' speeches Highbury, Richmond and Kew, Moorgate and Margate. The map has the real course of the Thames, with each of these on it.

## Spenser's refrain
Decisions: accepted by both; unchanged.
title: Spenser's refrain
body:
Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596) is a poem for a double wedding on the Thames, and its refrain, "Sweete Themmes runne softly, till I end my Song", closes each stanza. Read its first stanza, then lines 173–184. Eliot takes the refrain at 176 and 183 and varies it at 184; Spenser's nymphs gather flowers by the river, Eliot's "are departed", and his river "bears no" bottles, papers or cigarette ends.

## See who speaks
Decisions: accepted by both; unchanged.
title: See who speaks
body:
The Voices lens marks each speaker and each quoted voice: the "I" who fishes by the canal, the Mrs. Porter song, Verlaine's line, the nightingale, Mr. Eugenides (reported, not quoted), Tiresias, the typist, the "I" of Lower Thames Street, the three Thames-daughters, Augustine and the Buddha. Tiresias speaks only in this section; Eliot's note calls him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest".

## The Fire Sermon itself
Decisions: accepted by both; unchanged.
title: The Fire Sermon itself
body:
Eliot's note sends the reader to the Buddha's Fire Sermon in Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896) and calls it as important as the Sermon on the Mount. It is short. In it the Buddha says of the eye, the ear, the tongue, the body and the mind that each "is on fire", with the fire of passion, of hatred and of infatuation. The section's title is the sermon's, and "burning", five times at 308–311, is the poem's word for the sermon's "on fire".

## Pound's pencil on the typist
Decisions: accepted by both; unchanged.
title: Pound's pencil on the typist
body:
In the typescript the typist scene was longer, in regular quatrains, and Pound cut it heavily, writing in the margin that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it. The drafts page describes his marks, and the seventy or so lines of couplets in Pope's manner, about a fashionable woman called Fresca, that opened the section until he struck them out.

## The Rhine-daughters, then the Thames-daughters
Decisions: the reader's "singing of".
title: The Rhine-daughters, then the Thames-daughters
body:
Act III of Wagner's <i>Götterdämmerung</i> opens with the three Rhine-daughters swimming and singing of their stolen gold; "Weialala leia, wallala leialala" is their refrain, and the Thames-daughters take it at 277–278 and 290–291. Listen to the scene, then read lines 266–306. Eliot's note says his three daughters "speak in turn" from 292 to 306.

## Margate
Decisions: the reader's third sentence, which quotes only "some fifty lines" and so also meets the scholar's objection to quoting the Schiff letter's wording here. Round 3: the scholar corrected "letters" to one letter, to Sydney Schiff, 4 November 1921: the Guardian of 11 July 2009, quoting Valerie Eliot, gives the shelter and "some fifty lines" in one continuous quotation from it, and the batch B scholar recalls the same letter in the facsimile's introduction. The editor's memory that "some fifty lines" was in the letter to Aldington is withdrawn.
title: Margate
body:
"On Margate Sands. / I can connect / Nothing with nothing." Eliot spent three weeks at Margate, from late October to mid-November 1921, on three months' sick leave from Lloyds Bank. In a letter from there, to Sydney Schiff, he said he had drafted "some fifty lines" of this part, sitting in a shelter on the front. The Nayland Rock shelter on the seafront, listed in 2009 as the likely place, still stands. The map has it.

## Fire
Decisions: the reader's text.
title: Fire
body:
The Water lens marks fire in a third colour. In this section it falls on the last four lines only, where "burning", from the Buddha's sermon of the title, follows Augustine's "To Carthage then I came". Eliot's note says the placing of these two together "is not an accident".

## Every bird

The Birds pathway stops at each bird in the poem in order: the nightingale (here at 203–206, and in Part II), the gulls of Part IV, the hermit-thrush and the cock of Part V, and the swallow at 429, with recordings where they exist.

## Tiresias in Ovid
Decisions: the reader's split; the scholar's correction of what the library holds (324–331 only), in the reader's round-3 wording.
title: Tiresias in Ovid
body:
Eliot's note at 218 quotes nineteen lines of Ovid (<i>Metamorphoses</i> III, 320–338). Tiresias, who had lived seven years as a woman, is asked to settle Jupiter and Juno's dispute over which sex has the greater pleasure in love. Juno blinds him for his answer and Jupiter gives him foresight. The library gives lines 324–331, on the serpents he struck and the change of sex that followed, in Latin and English. Read them, then the typist scene, which Tiresias says he has "foresuffered".

## Whose side is the poem on
Decisions: the reader's text.
title: Whose side is the poem on
body:
The typist "smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone" (255–256). Eliot's note gives Goldsmith's song, in which the woman who "stoops to folly" has nothing left but to die; Eliot's typist, instead, puts a record on. Critics have read the scene as contempt for her, and as the nearest the poem comes to pity; those who read it as pity point to Tiresias's "foresuffered all" (243).

# drafts.txt

## fresca
Decisions: the reader's two changes ("compare"; the last sentence split).
title: The seventy lines about Fresca
body:
In the typescript "The Fire Sermon" opened with some seventy lines of heroic couplets on a fashionable woman, Fresca: her waking, her breakfast in bed, her visit to the "needful stool" with a novel of Richardson's, her letter, her bath, her reading. Eliot said later that it was written in imitation of Pope's <i>Rape of the Lock</i>; critics also compare Swift's dressing-room poems. Pound struck it out. Eliot recalled in 1946 that Pound's objection was that Pope had done this so well it could not be done better, and that one cannot parody Pope without writing better verse than Pope, "and you can't". The printed section begins with the river. On Rainey's dating of the drafts, its opening lines, 173–182, were the last lines written for the poem, in Paris in January 1922.

## typist-draft
Decisions: the reader's text; the scholar confirmed every detail in it. Round 3: where Pound's "make up yr. mind" stands is given as the batch A scholar placed it in round 3 (beside the typist's "may pass" line, facsimile p. 47, borne out by the printed change to "allows"), not beside the loiterers as this batch's scholar recalled; recorded as a difference between the two.
title: Pound and the typist
body:
In the typescript the scene ran in regular quatrains and was longer. The draft gave the clerk his age, a spotty face, greasy hair, table manners and boasts, and gave the typist a kimono and a Japanese print. Tiresias hedged with "Perhaps a cheap house agent's clerk", one of those loiterers who might have been seen anywhere. Pound wrote that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it. Against the hedge he wrote "Perhaps be damned", and beside the typist's "one half-formed thought may pass" (printed at 251 as "allows one half-formed thought to pass") he told Tiresias to make up his mind whether he knew or not. The clerk's exit, a couplet in which he stopped at a corner to urinate and spit, was marked by Pound and dropped; the dots at 248 fall where it stood. A passage that stood before the scene, addressed to London ("London, the swarming life you kill and breed"), went too.

## thames-draft
Decisions: the reader's text; "shortened" kept on the scholar's word (three memories of the facsimile agree).
title: The Thames-daughters in draft
body:
The river song, the Elizabeth and Leicester lines and the three daughters' speeches are generally taken to be the "some fifty lines" Eliot drafted at Margate in November 1921. They reached print with few marks from Pound, and the words he did write here, "OK" and "echt" (genuine), are approving. Eliot's own revision shortened the first daughter's speech. His note, which identifies three daughters who "speak in turn" from 292 to 306, was added, like all the notes, for the book edition of December 1922.
