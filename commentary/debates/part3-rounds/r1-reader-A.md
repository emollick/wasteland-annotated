# Round 1, reader, batch A (sweet-thames to tiresias)

## sweet-thames

### Verdict
Rewrite.

### Reads as a model wrote it
- "Spenser's river is a procession; Eliot's is the morning after." Balanced antithesis (one of the owner's own examples).
- "a season you can feel on your skin." Second-person flourish (also one of the owner's examples).
- "it is the only part of the poem with a season" Superlative nobody could check, and I do not believe it: Part I has April, lilacs, winter and a summer shower.
- "October:" Fragment with a colon. The poem says leaves are falling; it does not say October.

### Unclear or unhelpful
- The two swans floating down to Essex House do nothing for line 176.
- "Part of the section was drafted at Margate in the autumn of 1921" belongs at line 300 ("On Margate Sands"), not here.
- "leaving no bottles ... because the season is over" tells me what lines 177 to 179 already say.

### Keep
- That the line is the refrain of the Prothalamion (1596), a wedding poem ("spousal verse" is Spenser's own phrase and can stay).
- That Spenser's nymphs gather flowers on the Thames bank, so that "The nymphs are departed" means those nymphs.
- That "their friends, the loitering heirs of city directors" are the young men of the summer. This is the one thing in the note I could not have worked out alone.

### Proposed body
Title unchanged.

The refrain of Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596), a "spousal verse" for a double wedding, in which nymphs gather flowers on the Thames bank on a summer day. Eliot's nymphs and "their friends, the loitering heirs of city directors," are the girls and young men of a summer now over, and the litter that was its "testimony" has gone with them.

## leman

### Verdict
Rewrite.

### Reads as a model wrote it
- "The line is the poem's most private moment and its most public:" Balanced antithesis plus superlative (one of the owner's examples).
- "a man on sick leave from a bank, weeping by a Swiss lake, in the words of a people carried off to Babylon." Rhythmic list of three tying a bow, and a reading (that the weeper is Eliot) put as a fact.

### Unclear or unhelpful
- "with one word changed": the quote printed beside it shows three changes (rivers/waters, Babylon/Leman, we/I). I cannot tell whether the claim or the quote is wrong. The Prayer Book wording ("By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept") looks nearer to Eliot's line; the scholar should decide which to print.
- Dr Roger Vittoz and "writing Parts IV and V" are facts about Lausanne, not about this line.

### Keep
- Psalm 137, the exiles' psalm.
- Leman is Lake Geneva, and Eliot was at Lausanne on its shore in November and December 1921, on sick leave from the bank. Without this the line is just an odd place name.
- "Leman" is an old English word for a lover or mistress. Short, checkable, and it changes how I hear the line.

### Proposed body
Plainer title: Psalm 137 at Lake Geneva

Psalm 137, the psalm of the exiles in Babylon, with Babylon changed to Leman. Lac Léman is Lake Geneva; Eliot spent November and December 1921 at Lausanne on its shore, on sick leave from Lloyds Bank, and worked on the poem there. "Leman" is also an old English word for a lover or mistress.

## at-my-back

### Verdict
Rewrite.

### Reads as a model wrote it
- "Marvell's poem is a seduction: hurry, because we die." The "X is a Y:" opening with a brisk fragment after the colon.
- "skip the seduction and keep the dying." Balanced antithesis as a bow (one of the owner's examples).
- "comes round again and turns into Sweeney's motor horns; here, first time through," Tour-guide narration; the sentence runs on through two semicolons and I read it twice to find out where Eliot's note actually sits.

### Unclear or unhelpful

- "turns into Sweeney's motor horns" assumes I already know who Sweeney is; he is glossed at 198, so a plain cross-reference would do.

### Keep
- Marvell, "To His Coy Mistress," 1681, and in one plain clause what the poem is (a man urging a woman to yield because time is short); the quote does the rest.
- That Eliot uses the line twice, here and at 196, and that his note is at the second.
- The 1922 numbering slip, in a few words, because the site prints Eliot's note with the wrong number beside it and I would otherwise think the site had mis-set it.
- The skull's grin.

### Proposed body
Title unchanged.

Marvell, "To His Coy Mistress" (published 1681), which urges a woman to yield because time is short. Eliot uses the line twice. Here what is heard at the speaker's back is the rattle of bones and a chuckle "spread from ear to ear," a skull's grin; at line 196 it is "the sound of horns and motors." Eliot's note is placed at the second use (numbered 197 in the 1922 notes, one off).

## fishing-canal

### Verdict
Rewrite.

### Reads as a model wrote it
- "which is not where either of them belongs." Annotator's wit tying off the sentence.
- "Then the rat again (line 115 sent you here)," Tour-guide second person; nothing sent me anywhere.
- "which are the war dead, or the swimmers of last summer, or both." Rhythmic "or ... or both" ending; a reading dressed as fact.
- "Eliot's fisherman is both Ferdinand ... and Weston's Fisher King" Reading put as fact. Ferdinand is in the line; the Fisher King is in Weston and in Eliot's prefatory note.

### Unclear or unhelpful
- Whose reading is "the swimmers of last summer"? I cannot tell whether to believe it. The war dead I have met before in this poem (line 115); the swimmers are new and unsourced.
- "mourning a drowned king": Ferdinand's father is not drowned, he only thinks so. Costs nothing to say so.
- The printed quote includes "This music crept by me upon the waters," which is line 257, and the note does not say so. I wanted that.

### Keep
- Ferdinand, Tempest I ii, on the shore weeping for his father, just before Ariel's "Full fathom five" (which I have met at lines 48 and 125).
- "The poem adds a brother to Shakespeare's father." Plain and exact.
- The Fisher King, as a reading and with a one-clause account of who he is (wounded king, barren land), because Eliot's note on sources points to Weston and the reader needs the connection made once.
- The cross-reference to line 115 and the war dead as the usual reading of the white bodies.

### Proposed body
Title unchanged.

Ferdinand in <i>The Tempest</i>, Act I scene ii, sitting on the shore weeping for the father he believes drowned, just before Ariel sings "Full fathom five"; the same speech gives line 257. Eliot adds a brother: "the king my brother's wreck / And on the king my father's death before him." Because Eliot's notes name Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> as the poem's source, the man fishing is usually also taken as the Fisher King of the Grail legend, whose wound leaves his land barren; here he fishes a canal behind a gasworks in winter. The rat and the bones recall line 115, "rats' alley / Where the dead men lost their bones," and the "white bodies naked on the low damp ground" are generally read as the war dead.

## mrs-porter

### Verdict
Trim.

### Reads as a model wrote it
- "Which makes the next line, in French, the most violent cut in the poem." Fragment beginning "Which", superlative nobody could check (one of the owner's examples), and "cut" is film talk.
- "The three-line song is real:" Colon fragment. Real as opposed to what? Say what it was.
- "in versions considerably less printable than this one." Arch.
- "the horns are motor horns, the hunter is Sweeney, ..., the goddess is Mrs Porter, and the bathing is a foot-wash" is a list of four, but it is the mapping I needed, so it stays if said flatly.

### Unclear or unhelpful
- "the ape-necked man of his 1920 poems": "Apeneck Sweeney" is Eliot's own phrase and could be quoted as such; paraphrasing it loses the point that the wit is his.
- Nothing else; this note is mostly clear.

### Keep
- Day, Parliament of Bees, 1641, quoted in Eliot's note.
- The Actaeon story in two sentences, since Day only alludes to it.
- The mapping of Day's horns, hunter, goddess and bathing onto Eliot's lines.
- The song's history: reported from Sydney (Eliot's note), a Gallipoli soldiers' song, Mrs Porter said to be a Cairo brothel-keeper, the usual versions obscene.

### Proposed body
Plainer title: Actaeon and Mrs Porter

John Day's <i>The Parliament of Bees</i> (printed 1641), quoted in Eliot's note. Actaeon, out hunting, came upon the goddess Diana bathing; she turned him into a stag and his own hounds tore him apart. In Eliot the horns are motor horns, the hunter is Sweeney, "Apeneck Sweeney" of his 1920 poems, and Diana bathing is Mrs Porter and her daughter washing their feet in soda water. The song was a real one. Eliot's note says it "was reported to me from Sydney, Australia"; it was a soldiers' song of the Gallipoli campaign, Mrs Porter is said to have kept a brothel in Cairo, and the usual versions were cruder than this.

### Proposed body
Title unchanged.

The last line of Verlaine's sonnet "Parsifal" (1886), on Wagner's opera: the pure knight resists the Flower-maidens and Kundry, heals the wounded king Amfortas, and adores the Grail while boys' voices sing from the dome of the temple. In Wagner, Parsifal's feet are washed before he goes in to the Grail. Eliot sets the choirboys directly after Mrs Porter and her daughter washing their feet in soda water.

## eugenides

### Verdict
Trim.

### Reads as a model wrote it
- "a banker's slip that the editors of the poem have enjoyed ever since." Arch, and nobody could check it.
- "language Eliot handled daily at Lloyds" "Daily" is colour I cannot check; that he worked with such documents at Lloyds is the fact.
- "In September 1922, between the poem's completion and its publication, Smyrna burned." Dramatic last sentence built for effect; a bow.
- "The one-eyed merchant, in person" (title) "In person" is a wink.

### Unclear or unhelpful
- ""Demotic French" is the Levant's trading language." Demotic means colloquial, of the common people; the gloss slides from the word to a claim about the Levant that I cannot check. Say both plainly if both are wanted.
- "the usual place for City men to meet foreign clients": cannot check "the usual."
- "the terminus for the boat trains to the Continent": cannot check; keep only if the scholar can.
- Smyrna burning does not help me with the line; Eliot wrote it before. If it belongs anywhere it is with the 1920 photograph.
- Two paragraphs is long, but the six lines are full of trade jargon and the length is mostly earned.

### Keep
- Eugenides means "well-born."
- Smyrna (İzmir), the currant port; the pocketful is a sample.
- The expansion of "C.i.f. London: documents at sight," and that Eliot's own note expands it wrongly, because I will read his note and be puzzled. The bare fact is enough.
- The Cannon Street Hotel by the station, a meeting place for City men and foreign clients; the Metropole at Brighton; the weekend as a proposition "generally read as a homosexual one" (this is the right way to put a reading).
- Eliot's note linking him to the one-eyed merchant of the cards and the Phoenician Sailor; the profile guess, hedged as it is; Weston on Syrian merchants and the trade routes, in one clause.

### Proposed body
Plainer title: The one-eyed merchant

Eugenides means "well-born." Smyrna, now İzmir, was the port of Asia Minor that shipped currants to London; the pocketful is a sample. "C.i.f. London: documents at sight" is trade shorthand: cost, insurance and freight paid to London, and the shipping documents handed over when the sight draft is paid. Eliot, who handled such documents at Lloyds Bank, nevertheless expands the abbreviation in his note as "carriage and insurance free," which is not what c.i.f. stands for. "Demotic French" is the colloquial French of Levantine trade. The Cannon Street Hotel stood in front of Cannon Street station, where the Continental boat trains came in, and City men met foreign clients there. A weekend at the Metropole, the large hotel on the Brighton seafront, is a proposition, and it has generally been read as a homosexual one.

Eliot's note identifies him with the one-eyed merchant of Madame Sosostris's cards (one-eyed, presumably, because the card shows a face in profile), who "melts into the Phoenician Sailor." The Phoenicians were the traders of the ancient Mediterranean, and Weston argued that the Grail mysteries travelled Europe's trade routes with Syrian merchants.

### Proposed body
Plainer title: Sappho's evening star

Sappho's fragment to Hesperus, the evening star, behind "the evening hour that strives / Homeward, and brings the sailor home from sea." Eliot's note grants that his lines "may not appear as exact as Sappho's" and says he had in mind the "longshore" or "dory" fisherman who comes in at nightfall, as the fishermen did at Gloucester, Massachusetts, where he spent his boyhood summers. Critics also hear Stevenson's "Requiem" ("Home is the sailor, home from sea"). The violet hour is dusk, the end of the working day, when "the eyes and back / Turn upward from the desk"; the colour returns in Part V as "violet air" and "violet light."

## tiresias

### Verdict
Trim (first paragraph stands with small changes; second paragraph recast).

### Reads as a model wrote it
- "Take it seriously and the poem has a single consciousness; take it as one more voice and it doesn't." The "decide whether" instruction dressed as an antithesis.
- "The note is the closest thing the poem has to a stated design" Annotator's verdict as fact.
- "the drafts show that the design came late: Tiresias was there in the typescript, but the claim for him was written for the notes." Sounds like a discovery; what it says is that the note was written after the poem, which is true of every note.
- "He sided with Jupiter (women do)." Brisk parenthesis; a small cuteness.
- "was impatient with the seer's hedging" "The seer's hedging" refers to draft wording I have not seen; unexplained.

### Unclear or unhelpful
- Pound's marginal comment is quoted as a full sentence; the site's rule says a phrase or two of the marginalia, so describe it and quote the phrase.
- I wanted the three things the standard asks to be kept apart, and here they are mixed: what Eliot's note says, what critics made of it, and what the lines do (Tiresias speaks only here, at 228 to 230, and at 243 to 246).

### Keep
- The Ovid story in full, since Eliot's note gives it in untranslated Latin: the snakes, seven years as a woman, the judgement between Jupiter and Juno, blindness from Juno, foresight from Jupiter who could not undo another god's act.
- Sophocles' Tiresias at Thebes and Homer's in the underworld, tied to lines 245 to 246.
- Eliot's note quoted: "the most important personage in the poem" and "What Tiresias sees, in fact, is the substance of the poem."
- That the note came after the poem and that critics divide on how much weight it bears.
- Pound's "make up yr. mind," as a phrase.

### Proposed body
Title unchanged.

Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> III, which Eliot's note quotes in Latin. Tiresias struck two coupling snakes with his stick and was turned into a woman; seven years later he struck them again and became a man. When Jupiter and Juno argued over which sex has the greater pleasure in love, they called him to judge, as the one who had been both, and he agreed with Jupiter that women do. Juno blinded him; Jupiter, who could not undo what another god had done, gave him foresight instead. He is also the blind prophet of Thebes in Sophocles' <i>Oedipus</i> and the dead seer Odysseus consults in the underworld in the <i>Odyssey</i>: "I who have sat by Thebes below the wall / And walked among the lowest of the dead."

Eliot's note calls him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest," and says "What Tiresias <i>sees</i>, in fact, is the substance of the poem." The notes were written after the poem was finished. Critics have divided over whether to read the poem as Tiresias's single vision, as the note proposes, or to treat him as one voice among many; in the poem itself he speaks only here, at lines 228 to 230, and at 243 to 246. In the margin of the typescript Pound objected to hedging in the draft's wording: Tiresias should "make up yr. mind" whether he knew or not.
