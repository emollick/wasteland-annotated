# Round 1, scholar, batch B (typist to carthage)

## typist

### Verdict
rewrite

### Errors
- "her underwear ("combinations," one-piece, and "stays") drying out of the window". Wrong: only the combinations are out of the window (224–225); the stays are on the divan with the stockings, slippers and camisoles (226–227). Source: the 1922 text, lines 224–227 (poem-part3.txt; https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1321/pg1321.txt).
- "It is written in rhymed quatrains, abab". Overstated. Lines 218–221, 224–227, 231–246 and 253–256 rhyme abab; 222–223 and 228 are unrhymed, 229–230 is a couplet, and 247–252 run kiss / unlit / glass / lover / pass / over, which is no quatrain scheme. The regular quatrains belong to the typescript, and Pound's cuts broke them. Source: the poem text; the 1971 facsimile for the typescript (from memory, unverified; Wikipedia's Waste Land article says the same).
- "verse not interesting enough as verse to warrant so much of it" quotes a whole Pound comment. The site's rule for Pound's marginalia is a phrase or two: "not interesting enough as verse" is enough. The wording is right (facsimile, from memory; confirmed by the Wikipedia article on the poem). "Perhaps be damned" is right and is a phrase (the annotated page is reproduced at https://ecehm.tumblr.com/post/165927846321/).
- "pimpled ("carbuncular")": a carbuncle is a boil; "pimply" is the usual gloss (Southam). Not wrong.
- "the Yorkshire wool towns made fortunes in the war": Bradford's wool trade specifically; Southam's gloss (from memory). Not wrong, but say Bradford.

### Readings passed off as fact
- "The most argued-over scene in the poem." Uncheckable.
- "the rhymes are doing the judging". Annotator's metaphor.
- "What survived is the poem's cruellest writing, and the cruelty is aimed as much at the eighteenth-century manner it imitates as at the two people on the divan." Uncheckable superlative, and the reading (the quatrains as pastiche of the eighteenth-century heroic quatrain) is one critics have offered; it is not a fact, and the manner is as much Eliot's own quatrain poems of 1917–19.
- "blesses the clerk's exit with a rhyme". Decoration.
- "Whether Tiresias is compassionate or a voyeur is a question the poem asks about you." Second person; the two readings can be offered as readings.

### Missing
- Eliot's note at 218, the test case: what Tiresias sees "is the substance of the poem". A reader of this scene needs to be pointed at it, kept distinct from what the lines do.
- What "combinations", "stays" and "house agent" mean.
- Pound's other intervention here, which explains the poem's certainty at 251: the typescript let a thought "may pass" across her brain; Pound objected that Tiresias must make up his mind whether he knows; the poem now says her brain "allows" it. Facsimile, from memory, unverified (the wording of Pound's comment is confirmed by the Wikipedia article on the poem).

### Cut
"The most argued-over scene in the poem." / "and the rhymes are doing the judging" / "and blesses the clerk's exit with a rhyme" / "What survived is the poem's cruellest writing, and the cruelty is aimed ... on the divan." / "Whether Tiresias is compassionate or a voyeur is a question the poem asks about you."

### Proposed body
Title — The typist and the clerk

The typist's room is a bedsit: food from tins, the divan her bed at night, her "combinations" (a one-piece undergarment) drying out of the window, her "stays" (corset) piled on the divan with the stockings and camisoles. Her visitor is a "small house agent's clerk," an estate agent's clerk, pimply ("carbuncular"), with the assurance of new money; Bradford, the Yorkshire wool town, stood for fortunes made in the war. Eliot's note at line 218 says that what Tiresias sees "is the substance of the poem"; here he says he has "foresuffered all" and foretells the rest.

In the typescript the scene ran in regular quatrains rhyming abab. Pound cut several stanzas, writing beside the passage that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it, and "Perhaps be damned" against a line that began with "perhaps." What survives keeps the quatrains from line 231 to 246 and loosens them before and after. The typescript had a thought that "may pass" across her brain; Pound wrote that Tiresias must make up his mind whether he knows, and the poem now says her brain "allows" it. Critics have read Tiresias's watching as compassion and as voyeurism. The lines say "assaults," "no defence," "indifference," and give her the last word: "Well now that's done: and I'm glad it's over."

### Proposed body
Title — Goldsmith's song

Olivia Primrose's song in <i>The Vicar of Wakefield</i> (1766), chapter 24, sung to her family at her mother's asking some days after her father has brought her home from the squire who seduced her; they are sitting where she first met him, and his carriage appears as she finishes. Eliot takes Goldsmith's first line and adds "and" to carry it into his own longer line; Goldsmith's lines have four beats, Eliot's five. In the song the only art left to the fallen woman is to die. Eliot's typist "smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone." The scene has been read as contempt for her and, by others, as a kind of mercy: she is not required to die.

### Proposed body
Title — From the Strand to Lower Thames Street

Ferdinand's line from <i>The Tempest</i> again (Eliot's note: "as above"), and then the music's course through the City: along the Strand, up Queen Victoria Street, which runs from Blackfriars to the Bank, and down to Lower Thames Street, where Billingsgate fish market stood until 1982 and the "fishmen" drink at noon. St Magnus the Martyr is Wren's church at the north end of the old London Bridge, rebuilt after the Great Fire, finished in 1676 with the steeple added in 1705; its nave is divided from the aisles by Ionic columns, which is what "Ionian" is usually taken to mean. In April 1920 a commission appointed by the Bishop of London and chaired by Lord Phillimore recommended removing nineteen City churches, St Magnus among them. The London County Council's reply is the pamphlet Eliot's note cites, <i>The Proposed Demolition of Nineteen City Churches</i> (October 1920): it calls St Magnus "one of the most beautiful of all Wren's works" but finds the interior "disappointing," which Eliot's note answers with "one of the finest among Wren's interiors." He had already written, in <i>The Dial</i> in June 1921, that the threatened churches gave the City "a beauty which its hideous banks and commercial houses have not quite defaced." The church stands; its interior was redecorated by Martin Travers in 1924.

### Proposed body
Title — The Rhine-daughters on the Thames

"Weialala leia / Wallala leialala" is the refrain of the three Rhine-daughters at the opening of Act III of Wagner's <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, as they swim and sing of the gold stolen from them at the start of the <i>Ring</i>. ### Proposed body
Title — The Queen and Lord Robert, in a barge

Critics have set the passage beside the "burnished throne" of line 77, Shakespeare's Cleopatra in her barge. After the refrain the three Thames-daughters of Eliot's note speak in turn, lines 292–305.

Title — La Pia's line

<i>Purgatorio</i> V, 133–136. Among the late-repentant who died by violence, a woman gives Dante her name, her birthplace and the place of her death in one sentence, and points at her husband without naming him. ### Proposed body
Title — Margate Sands

Margate, on the north Kent coast where the Thames estuary opens to the sea, is where Eliot went in the autumn of 1921, on three months' leave from Lloyds Bank after a breakdown. He and Vivien were at the Albemarle Hotel, Cliftonville, by late October, and he stayed about three weeks. On 4 November he told Sydney Schiff he had done "a rough draft of part of part III," written "while sitting in a shelter on the front"; the Nayland Rock shelter on the promenade, listed in 2009 as the likely one, still stands. The part is usually identified as the pencil drafts of lines 266–311, the Thames-daughters' song through to "burning." The third daughter's speech was changed in draft: in its first form she still felt "the pressure of dirty hand," and "my people" were "plain people." Lyndall Gordon and others have read "I can connect / Nothing with nothing" as Eliot's own confession as well as hers.

### Proposed body
Title — Augustine and the Buddha

Augustine, <i>Confessions</i> III.1, in Pusey's translation (1838): "To Carthage I came, where there sang all around me in my ears a cauldron of unholy loves." Eliot's note gives the sentence in his own rewording. The Latin has a pun, Carthago and sartago, a frying pan. Augustine had come to Carthage at about seventeen to study rhetoric, and Book III begins with his love affairs and the theatre. "Burning" is the Buddha's Fire Sermon, which gives the section its title: "All things, O priests, are on fire . . . The eye, O priests, is on fire; forms are on fire . . . with the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation." Eliot's note sends readers to Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896), in the Harvard Oriental Series edited by Charles Lanman, under whom Eliot studied Sanskrit and Pali at Harvard in 1911–13; it is generally assumed he met the sermon there. Then Augustine again, Book X: "I . . . entangle my steps with these outward beauties; but Thou pluckest me out, O Lord, Thou pluckest me out." Eliot's note says that placing "these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism" together "as the culmination of this part of the poem, is not an accident." Critics have connected this burning with the refining fire of <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, quoted at line 428.
