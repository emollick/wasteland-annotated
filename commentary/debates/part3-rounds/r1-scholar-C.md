Proposed title: Spenser's refrain
Proposed text:
Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596) is a poem for a double wedding on the Thames, and its refrain, "Sweete Themmes runne softly, till I end my Song", closes each stanza. Spenser's nymphs gather flowers by the river; Eliot's "are departed", and his river "bears no" bottles, papers or cigarette ends, a list of what is not there.

## The Fire Sermon itself
Verdict: needs correction.
Errors: "the translation Eliot used at Harvard" is an inference, not a documented fact. Eliot's note names "the late Henry Clarke Warren's Buddhism in Translation" (the title is <i>Buddhism in Translations</i>, Harvard Oriental Series III, 1896). Eliot studied Sanskrit under Charles Lanman at Harvard from 1911 and Pali in 1912–13; Lanman edited the series and had been Warren's collaborator, so the book is the obvious text, but I know no document that says Eliot used it there (Crawford, <i>Young Eliot</i>; Kearns, <i>T. S. Eliot and Indic Traditions</i>; from memory, unverified). "the five 'burning's ... are the sermon's own refrain": five is right (four at 308, one at 311; counted), but Warren's repeated phrase is "is on fire" / "are on fire", never "burning" (Warren §73, pp. 351–52, https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.217548; checked). Eliot's note says only that "these words are taken" from the sermon. "everything in it ... is seen 'on fire'" is a reading.
Readings passed off as fact: the last sentence.
Missing: what the sermon says.
Cut: "before the section, not after".
Proposed text:
Eliot's note sends the reader to the Buddha's Fire Sermon in Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896) and calls it as important as the Sermon on the Mount. In Warren the Buddha says of the eye, the ear, the tongue, the body and the mind that each "is on fire", with the fire of passion, hatred and infatuation. Eliot had studied Sanskrit and Pali at Harvard under Charles Lanman, who edited the series the book appeared in, and is generally assumed to have read it there. The section's title is the sermon's; "burning", five times at 308–311, is the poem's word for the sermon's "on fire".

## Margate
Verdict: needs correction.
Errors: "much of this section": the typist scene and the opening were in typescript before Margate. Eliot's own words from Margate were "a rough draft of part of part III" (to Sydney Schiff, 4 November 1921) and "some fifty lines" (letter from Margate, November 1921; both quoted in the Guardian, 11 July 2009, with Valerie Eliot's letter supporting the listing: https://www.theguardian.com/books/2009/jul/12/ts-eliot-margate-shrine; the "fifty lines" sentence is, as I recall, from the letter to Richard Aldington, unverified). "October 1921": he was there from about 22 October to about 12 November, and the letters that report the work are from November (Letters vol. 1, from memory). "a public shelter on the Margate seafront": Eliot wrote "a shelter on the front"; the identification with the Nayland Rock shelter is the council's and the listing's (Grade II, 2009), supported by Valerie Eliot, not Eliot's own. "The shelter is still there": the Nayland Rock shelter stands. "on sick leave from the bank": right (three months' leave from Lloyds).
Readings passed off as fact: which shelter; how much of the section.
Missing: nothing.
Cut: "much of".
Proposed text:
"On Margate Sands. / I can connect / Nothing with nothing." Eliot spent three weeks at Margate, from late October to mid-November 1921, on three months' sick leave from Lloyds Bank. In letters from there he said he had drafted "part of part III", "some fifty lines", sitting in a shelter on the front. The Nayland Rock shelter on the seafront was listed in 2009 as the place; it still stands, and the map has it.

Proposed text:
Eliot's note quotes nineteen lines of Ovid (<i>Metamorphoses</i> III, 320–338). ## fresca
Verdict: needs correction (rights) and hedging.
Errors: the description is right as far as I know: about seventy lines of couplets on two typescript pages (Ricks and McCue's editorial composite; Rainey; from memory), covering her waking, breakfast in bed, the "needful stool" with Richardson, a letter, the bath and her reading. Pope's <i>Rape of the Lock</i> is Eliot's own account (the 1946 essay, and again in the 1959 <i>Paris Review</i> interview, "an imitation Rape of the Lock"); Swift's dressing-room poems are the critics' comparison and should be given as such. "Pound struck it all out": his pencil runs through the passage and it went; fine. The 1946 quotation is about forty-five words of Eliot's "Ezra Pound" (<i>Poetry</i>, September 1946); it matches my memory of the text (the Poetry Foundation archive is behind a challenge page from here, so unverified), but under the site's rule it is a passage, not a phrase, and must be paraphrased with at most a phrase kept.
Readings passed off as fact: Swift.
Missing: optional: some Fresca lines were reused in Vivien Eliot's "Letters of the Moment II" in the <i>Criterion</i>, April 1924 (Rainey; from memory).
Cut: the long quotation.
Proposed text:
In the typescript "The Fire Sermon" opened with some seventy lines of heroic couplets on a fashionable woman, Fresca: her waking, her breakfast in bed, her visit to the "needful stool" with a novel of Richardson, her letter, her bath, her reading. Eliot said later that it was written in imitation of Pope's <i>Rape of the Lock</i>; critics also hear Swift's dressing-room poems. Pound struck it out. Eliot recalled in 1946 that Pound's objection was that Pope had done this so well it could not be done better, and that one cannot parody Pope without writing better verse than Pope, "and you can't". The printed section begins with the river.

## typist-draft
Verdict: needs correction.
Errors: "in quatrains throughout": right; the irregular lines of the 1922 text (215–218, 222–223) are where Pound cut half-lines out of regular quatrains (the draft's first stanza ended on "the horn or ivory gates"; from memory). "(his hair, his evening, his 'one bold stare' was there)": muddled. The draft's clerk had his age (twenty-one), his spotty face, hair "thick with grease, and thick with scurf", his table manners and his boast of an evening with the painter Nevinson; "one bold stare" is in the printed text (232), so it is not a cut detail. The typist also lost a stanza (a "bright kimono", a Japanese print bought in Oxford Street). "a line with 'perhaps' in it": the line is "Perhaps a cheap house agent's clerk", which became 232; say so. "beside Tiresias's foretelling": as I recall, "make up yr. mind ..." stands beside the stanza calling the clerk one of those loiterers "whom we say / We may have seen in any public place", the second hedge, not beside "foretold the rest" (229); unverified against the facsimile. "urinating in the street": the couplet had him stop "at the corner where the stable is" to urinate and spit; Pound's comment there, as I recall, was "probaly over the mark"; the couplet went and the dots at 248 fall where it stood. "A passage after the typist, beginning 'London, the swarming life you kill and breed'": before, not after, as I recall: in the typescript it stands between Mr. Eugenides (214) and "At the violet hour" (215), and Pound's comments on it include "B-ll-S" and "Palmer Cox's brownies" (against "Phantasmal gnomes"); from memory of the facsimile and of Ricks and McCue, unverified. Rights: three quotations of Pound's marginalia; keep the shortest forms.
Readings passed off as fact: none.
Missing: that the dots at 248 mark a cut.
Cut: "the ones everyone quotes".
Proposed text:
In the typescript the scene ran in regular quatrains and was longer. The clerk had his age, his spotty face, his greasy hair, his table manners and his boasts; the typist a kimono and a Japanese print; and Tiresias hedged: "Perhaps a cheap house agent's clerk", one of those "whom we say / We may have seen". Pound wrote that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it, "Perhaps be damned" against the first hedge, and beside the second told Tiresias to make up his mind. The clerk's exit, a couplet in which he stopped at a corner to urinate and spit, was marked by Pound and dropped; the dots at 248 fall where it stood. A passage before the scene addressed to London ("London, the swarming life you kill and breed") went too.

## thames-draft
Verdict: needs correction and hedging.
Errors: "survive much as drafted": true of the river song, the Elizabeth stanza and the second and third daughters, as I recall; the first daughter's speech was reworked by Eliot himself from a longer manuscript version (unverified). "he wrote 'OK' and 'echt' against the short-lined river passage": I recall both words on these pages but cannot place them by line; unverified; keep the words only if the editor can check the facsimile, and drop the location. "Eliot's note ... numbering the daughters' turns, was written later, for the book": the note does not number them; it says "(three)" and that they "speak in turn" from 292 to 306; and all the notes were added for the Boni and Liveright book (December 1922), the Criterion and Dial printings having none, so the sentence implies a distinction that does not exist. Title: "lengthened and shortened" describes nothing.
Readings passed off as fact: the placing of Pound's words.
Missing: that this stretch is the part of the poem generally taken to be the "some fifty lines" Eliot drafted at Margate in November 1921, the one fact a reader of the drafts page most needs here.
Cut: the last sentence as written.
Proposed title: The Thames-daughters in draft
Proposed text:
The river song, the Elizabeth stanza and the three daughters' speeches are the part of the poem generally taken to be the "some fifty lines" Eliot drafted at Margate in November 1921, and they reached print with few marks from Pound; the words he wrote here, "OK" and "echt" (genuine), approve. Eliot's own revision shortened the first daughter's speech. His note identifying three daughters who "speak in turn" from 292 to 306 was added, like all the notes, for the book edition of December 1922.
