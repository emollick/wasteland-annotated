## typist
Object: three sentences in the first paragraph.

"The typist's room is a bedsit: food from tins, the divan her bed at night, her "combinations" (a one-piece undergarment) drying out of the window, her "stays" (a corset) piled on the divan with the stockings and camisoles." — a colon and a list of four; "bedsit" is British. Put instead: "The typist lives in one rented room. Her food comes out of tins, the divan is her bed at night, her "combinations" (a one-piece undergarment) are drying out of the window, and her "stays" (a corset) are piled on the divan with the stockings and camisoles."

"Her visitor is a "small house agent's clerk", an estate agent's clerk, pimply ("carbuncular"), with the assurance of new money: Bradford, the Yorkshire wool town, stood for fortunes made in the war, and a silk hat is a gentleman's top hat." — "with the assurance of new money" muddles the simile: he is "one of the low", and it is his assurance, not his money, that is compared to the hat. Put instead: "Her visitor is a clerk in a house agent's (an estate agent's) office; "carbuncular" means pimply. Bradford, the Yorkshire wool town, stood for fortunes made in the war, and a silk hat is a gentleman's top hat."

"and the lines themselves say "assaults", "no defence" and "indifference"." — the poem quoted back at a reader who has it in front of them. Cut; end the sentence at "foretells the rest".

The second paragraph is accepted as it stands. Thebes and "the lowest of the dead" (245–246) are still unglossed; that is the scholar's call, not mine.

## strand
Object: the opening, one fact, and the pamphlet sentence.

"Ferdinand's line from <i>The Tempest</i> again (Eliot's note: "as above"), and then the music's course through the City: along the Strand, up Queen Victoria Street, which runs from Blackfriars to the Bank, and down to Lower Thames Street, where Billingsgate fish market stood until 1982 and the "fishmen" drink at noon." — a fragment with no verb, the "and then ... :" walk-through, and "again" still unexplained; the Strand is not in the City. Put instead: "Ferdinand's line in <i>The Tempest</i>, I, ii, from the speech already echoed at lines 191–192, which is what Eliot's "as above" refers to. The music goes along the Strand, up Queen Victoria Street, which runs from Blackfriars to the Bank, and down to Lower Thames Street, where Billingsgate fish market stood until 1982, hence the "fishmen"."

"with the steeple added in 1705" — the line is about the interior; the steeple does not help with it. Cut, so the sentence ends "rebuilt after the Great Fire and finished in 1676."

"The London County Council's reply is the pamphlet Eliot's note cites, <i>The Proposed Demolition of Nineteen City Churches</i> (October 1920): it calls St Magnus "one of the most beautiful of all Wren's works" but finds the interior "disappointing", which Eliot's note answers with "one of the finest among Wren's interiors"." — one sentence carrying a title, a date, two quotations and a verdict on Eliot's motive. Put instead: "The London County Council's reply, <i>The Proposed Demolition of Nineteen City Churches</i> (October 1920), is the pamphlet Eliot's note cites. It calls St Magnus "one of the most beautiful of all Wren's works" but finds the interior "disappointing"; Eliot's "to my mind one of the finest among Wren's interiors" takes the other side."

The rest is accepted.

## thames-daughters
Object: the last sentence.

"Lines 266–276 give the working river of Eliot's day: oil and tar, and sailing barges, their sails dressed red-brown and the "heavy spar" the sprit that carries the mainsail, drifting with the tide down Greenwich Reach, the stretch opposite Greenwich, past the Isle of Dogs, the peninsula of docks on the north bank." — appositions inside appositions; "drifting" has lost its barges by the time it arrives; "oil and tar" is the poem quoted back. Put instead: "Lines 266–276 give the working river of Eliot's day. The barges are Thames sailing barges, whose sails were dressed red-brown; the "heavy spar" is the sprit that carries the mainsail. Greenwich Reach is the stretch of river opposite Greenwich, and the Isle of Dogs is the peninsula of docks on its north bank."

## elizabeth-leicester
Object: the first sentence, and one phrase.

"Eliot's note quotes Froude's <i>History of England</i> (the volume on Elizabeth, chapter iv), which translates a letter of 30 June 1561 from Álvaro de la Quadra, Philip II's ambassador in London, reporting a water party at which Elizabeth and Robert Dudley, made Earl of Leicester three years later, joked in his hearing about marrying. Dudley's wife had died the year before in a fall that left him under suspicion, and the marriage never took place." — fifty words and three appositions before the verb. Put instead: "Eliot's note quotes Froude's <i>History of England</i> (the volume on Elizabeth, chapter iv). Froude translates a letter of 30 June 1561 from Álvaro de la Quadra, Philip II's ambassador in London, describing a water party at which Elizabeth and Robert Dudley joked in his hearing about marrying. Dudley was made Earl of Leicester three years later. His wife had died the year before the outing, in a fall that left him under suspicion, and the marriage never took place."

"the "White towers" are not identified, and are usually taken as the Tower of London" — not identified by whom? Put instead: "Eliot does not say what the "White towers" are; they are usually taken as the Tower of London."

The rest, and the new quote, accepted.

## highbury
Object: the parallel is still described, not shown.

"The first Thames-daughter's speech (lines 292–295) keeps the shape of Dante's line, born in one place and undone in another: Highbury, in north London ("Trams and dusty trees" is her own first line); Richmond and Kew, on the river above London, where Londoners went boating." — a colon, a semicolon and a parenthesis in one sentence, and the reader is still left to lay the two lines side by side. Put instead: "The first Thames-daughter's speech (lines 292–295) keeps the shape of Dante's line: Siena made me, Maremma unmade me; Highbury bore me, Richmond and Kew undid me. Highbury is in north London, and "Trams and dusty trees" (line 292) is her own first line. Richmond and Kew are on the river above London, where Londoners went boating."

The rest accepted.

## margate
Object: the first two sentences.

"Margate, on the north Kent coast where the Thames estuary opens to the sea, is where Eliot went in the autumn of 1921, on three months' leave from Lloyds Bank after a breakdown; he stayed about three weeks, from late October to mid-November, at the Albemarle Hotel, Cliftonville." — "Margate ... is where Eliot went" is a frame, not a sentence a person would say; the hotel does not help with the line and should go. Put instead: "Margate is a resort on the north Kent coast, where the Thames estuary opens to the sea. Eliot went there in the autumn of 1921, on three months' leave from Lloyds Bank after a breakdown, and stayed about three weeks, from late October to mid-November."

"On 4 November he told Sydney Schiff he had done "a rough draft of part of part III", written sitting in a shelter on the front; the Nayland Rock shelter on the promenade, listed in 2009 as the likely one, still stands." — "listed" means nothing to a reader outside Britain, and the two halves are two facts. Put instead: "On 4 November he told Sydney Schiff he had done "a rough draft of part of part III", written sitting in a shelter on the front. That part is usually identified as the pencil drafts of lines 266–311, from "The river sweats" to "burning". The Nayland Rock shelter on the promenade, which still stands, was given listed-building protection in 2009 as the likely place." (this absorbs the third sentence; the Gordon sentence follows unchanged).

## carthage
Object: three sentences.

"The Latin has a pun, Carthago and sartago, a frying pan." — the reader is not told this is why Pusey has "cauldron". Put instead: "The Latin has a pun, Carthago and sartago, a frying pan, which is Pusey's "cauldron"."

"Eliot's note sends readers to Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896), in the Harvard series edited by Charles Lanman, under whom Eliot studied Sanskrit and Pali in 1911–13; it is generally assumed he met the sermon there." — a chain of five links, and "there" could be the book, the series, the class or Harvard. Lanman and the series do not help with "burning"; I ask for them to go. Put instead: "Eliot's note sends readers to Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896). Eliot studied Sanskrit and Pali at Harvard in 1911–13, and it is generally assumed that he met the sermon there, in Warren's version." If the scholar needs Lanman as the ground for the assumption, one clause: "under Charles Lanman, who edited the series Warren's book is in".

"Eliot's note says that placing "these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism" together "as the culmination of this part of the poem, is not an accident."" — his note is printed beside line 309, inside this gloss's range; quoting it whole is repetition. Put instead: "Eliot's note at line 309 says the pairing of "eastern and western asceticism" here "is not an accident"."

Also "which gives the section its title" — put "which gives Part III its title". The rest accepted.
