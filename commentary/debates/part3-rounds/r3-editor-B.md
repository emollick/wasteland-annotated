# Round 3, editor's final texts, batch B (glosses typist to carthage)

How these were made: the round-2 texts with the scholar's two corrections (lovely-woman, strand) and the reader's rewordings, all taken. Where the editor altered the reader's wording the alteration is named under Decisions. Fixed fields untouched.

## typist
Decisions: the reader's first and third changes taken whole. For the second, the reader's two sentences left Bradford and the silk hat hanging on nothing, so they are tied to the simile they gloss, by line number, without explaining it.
title: The typist and the clerk
body:
The typist lives in one rented room. Her food comes out of tins, the divan is her bed at night, her "combinations" (a one-piece undergarment) are drying out of the window, and her "stays" (a corset) are piled on the divan with the stockings and camisoles. Her visitor is a clerk in a house agent's (an estate agent's) office; "carbuncular" means pimply. In the simile at 233–234, Bradford is the Yorkshire wool town that stood for fortunes made in the war, and a silk hat is a gentleman's top hat. Eliot's note at line 218 says that what Tiresias sees "is the substance of the poem"; here Tiresias says he has "foresuffered all" and foretells the rest.

In the typescript the scene ran in regular quatrains rhyming abab, and Pound cut several stanzas, writing beside it that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it. What survives keeps the quatrains from 231 to 246 and loosens them before and after. Critics have read Tiresias's watching as compassion and as voyeurism.

## lovely-woman
Decisions: the scholar's first sentence (the Vicar found her at an inn; weeks, not days); the reader's close.
title: Goldsmith's song
body:
Olivia Primrose's song in <i>The Vicar of Wakefield</i> (1766), chapter 24, sung to her family some weeks after her father has found her at an inn, cast off by the squire who seduced her, and brought her home. Eliot takes Goldsmith's first line and adds "and" to carry it into his own longer line; Goldsmith's lines have four beats, Eliot's five. In the song the only art left to the fallen woman is to die; Eliot's typist puts a record on the gramophone. The scene has been read as contempt for her and as a kind of mercy.

## strand
Decisions: the reader's opening, which drops "drink at noon" (so the scholar's "lounge" point is met), the steeple cut, and the pamphlet sentence split; all taken. Round 3: with the steeple gone, "finished in 1676" was no longer true as phrased (the scholar), so "the body of the church complete by 1676".
title: From the Strand to Lower Thames Street
body:
Ferdinand's line in <i>The Tempest</i>, I, ii, from the speech already echoed at lines 191–192, which is what Eliot's "as above" refers to. The music goes along the Strand, up Queen Victoria Street, which runs from Blackfriars to the Bank, and down to Lower Thames Street, where Billingsgate fish market stood until 1982, hence the "fishmen". St Magnus the Martyr is Wren's church at the north end of the old London Bridge, rebuilt after the Great Fire, the body of the church complete by 1676; "Ionian" is usually taken to mean the Ionic columns that divide its nave from the aisles. In April 1920 a commission appointed by the Bishop of London and chaired by Lord Phillimore recommended removing nineteen City churches, St Magnus among them. The London County Council's reply, <i>The Proposed Demolition of Nineteen City Churches</i> (October 1920), is the pamphlet Eliot's note cites. It calls St Magnus "one of the most beautiful of all Wren's works" but finds the interior "disappointing"; Eliot's "to my mind one of the finest among Wren's interiors" takes the other side. The church stands; its interior was redecorated in 1924.

## thames-daughters
Decisions: the reader's last three sentences, with the scholar's confirmation of the sails and the sprit.
title: The Rhine-daughters on the Thames
body:
"Weialala leia / Wallala leialala" is the refrain of the three Rhine-daughters at the opening of Act III of Wagner's <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, as they swim and sing of the gold stolen from them at the start of the <i>Ring</i>. Eliot's note says the Song of the Thames-daughters begins here and that from line 292 to 306 they speak in turn. Lines 266–276 give the working river of Eliot's day. The barges are Thames sailing barges, whose sails were dressed red-brown; the "heavy spar" is the sprit that carries the mainsail. Greenwich Reach is the stretch of river opposite Greenwich, and the Isle of Dogs is the peninsula of docks on its north bank.

## elizabeth-leicester
Decisions: the reader's split of the first sentence and the "White towers" sentence; the scholar's quote confirmed against the Everyman page; "a water party" kept, which the scholar allows.
title: Elizabeth and Leicester on the river
quote: In the afternoon we were in a barge, watching the games on the river. She was alone with the Lord Robert and myself on the poop, when they began to talk nonsense, and went so far that Lord Robert at last said, as I was on the spot there was no reason why they should not be married if the queen pleased.
body:
Eliot's note quotes Froude's <i>History of England</i> (the volume on Elizabeth, chapter iv). Froude translates a letter of 30 June 1561 from Álvaro de la Quadra, Philip II's ambassador in London, describing a water party at which Elizabeth and Robert Dudley joked in his hearing about marrying. Dudley was made Earl of Leicester three years later. His wife had died the year before the outing, in a fall that left him under suspicion, and the marriage never took place. The gilded stern, the red and gold, the south-west wind and the peal of bells are not in the letter. Eliot does not say what the "White towers" are; they are usually taken as the Tower of London. Critics have set the passage beside the "burnished throne" of line 77, Shakespeare's Cleopatra in her barge.

## highbury
Decisions: the reader's version, which lays the two lines side by side.
title: La Pia's line
body:
<i>Purgatorio</i> V, 133–136. Among the late-repentant who died by violence, a woman gives Dante her name, her birthplace and the place of her death in one sentence, and points at her husband without naming him. The early commentators made her Pia de' Tolomei of Siena, killed on her husband's orders at his castle in the Maremma; nothing certain is known of her. The first Thames-daughter's speech (lines 292–295) keeps the shape of Dante's line: Siena made me, Maremma unmade me; Highbury bore me, Richmond and Kew undid me. Highbury is in north London, and "Trams and dusty trees" (line 292) is her own first line. Richmond and Kew are on the river above London, where Londoners went boating. She gives no name.

## moorgate
Decisions: accepted by both; unchanged from round 2.
title: The second daughter
body:
The second of the three Thames-daughters of Eliot's note speaks from Moorgate, a street and district on the north edge of the City of London, the business district, named from a gate in the old wall. "The event" is the seduction; "a new start" is his phrase, reported without comment.

## margate
Decisions: the reader's first two sentences (the hotel cut) and the shelter sentence taken. The Schiff letter's words, "a rough draft of part of part III", kept on the batch B scholar's citation (the Letters, as quoted by the Historic England listing); the batch C scholar doubts the wording, which is recorded as unsettled.
title: Margate Sands
body:
Margate is a resort on the north Kent coast, where the Thames estuary opens to the sea. Eliot went there in the autumn of 1921, on three months' leave from Lloyds Bank after a breakdown, and stayed about three weeks, from late October to mid-November. On 4 November he told Sydney Schiff he had done "a rough draft of part of part III", written sitting in a shelter on the front. That part is usually identified as the pencil drafts of lines 266–311, from "The river sweats" to "burning". The Nayland Rock shelter on the promenade, which still stands, was given listed-building protection in 2009 as the likely place. Lyndall Gordon and others have read "I can connect / Nothing with nothing" as Eliot's own confession as well as the third daughter's.

## carthage
Decisions: the reader's three rewordings taken; the series named as Eliot's note names it (Harvard Oriental Series), which the scholar offered; Eliot's note quoted in the reader's shorter form, with the note's own phrase for the two. Round 3: "in Warren's version" cut (the scholar: the one account of the Harvard course has the class reading the sermon in Pali, so it says more than is known).
title: Augustine and the Buddha
body:
The opening of Book III of Augustine's <i>Confessions</i> (c. 400), in Pusey's translation (1838), given above; the line, like Eliot's note, rewords it. The Latin has a pun, Carthago and sartago, a frying pan, which is Pusey's "cauldron". Augustine had come to Carthage at about seventeen to study rhetoric, and Book III begins with his love affairs and the theatre. "Burning" is from the Buddha's Fire Sermon, which gives Part III its title: "All things, O priests, are on fire . . . The eye, O priests, is on fire; forms are on fire . . . with the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation." Eliot's note sends readers to Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (Harvard Oriental Series, 1896). Eliot studied Sanskrit and Pali at Harvard in 1911–13, and it is generally assumed that he met the sermon there. "O Lord Thou pluckest me out" is Augustine again, Book X: "I . . . entangle my steps with these outward beauties; but Thou pluckest me out, O Lord, Thou pluckest me out." Eliot's note at line 309 says that putting "these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism" together here "is not an accident". Critics have connected this burning with the refining fire of <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, quoted at line 428.
