## The Fire Sermon itself
Verdict: rewrite

"the five 'burning's at the end" — counting to sound clever.
Unclear or unhelpful: "the translation Eliot used at Harvard" — the note names Warren's book; the Harvard part I cannot check and do not need. "the sermon's own refrain" — I cannot tell whether the sermon says "burning" or "on fire"; the scholar should say which.
Keep: that the title and the last lines come from the sermon, that it is short, and that the note names it.
Proposed text: Eliot's note at line 308 names the translation of the Buddha's Fire Sermon he used, in Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i>. It is short. In it the Buddha tells his monks that everything the senses meet is on fire. The section takes its title from it, and the word "burning" at lines 308 and 311.

## Tiresias in Ovid
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: "nineteen lines of Latin" — counting. "a person who has been both people on the divan" — clever, a little.
Unclear or unhelpful: it says nothing of what the Latin is about beyond the change of sex; the quarrel between Jupiter and Juno that Tiresias settles is the part that bears on the typist scene.

## Whose side is the poem on
Verdict: rewrite
Reads as a model wrote it: "Contempt, or the only mercy in the poem?" — the balanced antithesis and a superlative nobody can check. "Readers have argued for a century" — who? "Read lines 249–256 once each way" — the tell the owner named.
Unclear or unhelpful: "smooths" — the 1922 text has "smoothes" (line 255). It never says what the two readings rest on, so I have nothing to read for.
Keep: the question in the title; the quotation, corrected.
Proposed text: In Goldsmith's song, which Eliot's note at line 253 points to, the woman who "stoops to folly" has nothing left but to die. Eliot's typist "smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone." Contempt for her, or pity? Critics have read it both ways; those who read it as pity point to lines 243–246, where Tiresias says he has "foresuffered all."

## fresca
Verdict: trim
Reads as a model wrote it: little. "a deliberate imitation" — "deliberate" is padding.
Unclear or unhelpful: the 1946 quotation is two whole sentences of Eliot after 1929, which the rights rule says to describe or quote a phrase of. "reading Richardson" — a reader may not know he is a novelist.
Keep: the shape of the record: what the passage was, what it imitated, that Pound struck it out, why, how the section now begins.
Proposed text: In typescript the section opened with about seventy lines of heroic couplets on a fashionable woman, Fresca: she wakes, breakfasts in bed, reads a novel of Richardson's on the lavatory, writes a letter. It imitates Pope's <i>Rape of the Lock</i> and Swift's dressing-room poems. Pound struck it all out. Eliot recalled his reason in 1946: Pope had done this so well that it could not be done better, and "you cannot parody Pope unless you can write better verse than Pope." The section now begins with the river.
