# Part III, The Fire Sermon: twelve notes before and after

The text as it stood on the site on 20 September 2026, and the text after the debate. The full record, with every note and every round, is in part3.md beside this file.

## Gloss at line 176, "Sweet Thames, run softly"

**Before**

title: Spenser's wedding river
body: The refrain of Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596), a "spousal verse" for a double wedding in which nymphs gather flowers on the Thames bank and two swans float down to Essex House. Spenser's river is a procession; Eliot's is the morning after. "The nymphs are departed" and so have "their friends, the loitering heirs of city directors," the young men of the summer, leaving no bottles, papers or cigarette ends because the season is over. October: "the last fingers of leaf / Clutch and sink into the wet bank." Part of the section was drafted at Margate in the autumn of 1921, and it is the only part of the poem with a season you can feel on your skin.

**After**

title: Spenser's wedding river
body: The refrain of Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596), a "spousal verse" for a double wedding, in which the poet walks by the Thames and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans that stand for the brides. Eliot repeats the refrain at 183 and varies it at 184. His nymphs and "their friends, the loitering heirs of city directors" are usually taken as the girls and young men of the summer, gone with the season, and the litter of summer nights with them.

## Gloss at line 206, "Tereu"

**Before**

title: The bird names her rapist
body: John Lyly's song from <i>Campaspe</i> (1584), the best-known statement of the Elizabethan convention that the nightingale sings "jug jug" and "tereu," which is the vocative of Tereus: the bird calling the name of the man who cut out her tongue. Four lines, three birds' worth of noise, then the name. "So rudely forc'd" is line 100 with its spelling roughened. The nightingale's song in this poem is never lovely. It is evidence.

**After**

title: Tereu, the nightingale's cry
body: The song printed with Lyly's <i>Campaspe</i> (the play 1584, its songs 1632) gives the Elizabethan convention that the nightingale sings "jug jug" and cries "tereu", the vocative of Tereus, the name of the king who raped her and cut out her tongue. The story is Ovid's (<i>Metamorphoses</i> VI) and is told at lines 99–103. Line 205 repeats line 100, "So rudely forced", in an older spelling, and Eliot's own note at line 100 points to this passage ("Cf. Part III, l. 204").

## Gloss at line 300, "On Margate Sands"

**Before**

title: Where the poem was written
body: The third daughter speaks from Margate, the Kentish seaside resort at the mouth of the Thames estuary. Eliot went there in October 1921, on three months' sick leave from the bank, and stayed until mid-November at the Albemarle Hotel in Cliftonville; he sat in a public shelter on the front, the Nayland Rock shelter, which still stands, and wrote, he told Sydney Schiff on 4 November, "a rough draft of part of part III." "I can connect / Nothing with nothing" is the daughter's confession and the poet's. The "broken fingernails of dirty hands" and "My people humble people who expect / Nothing" are what he saw on the beach at the end of the season. The song runs down to "la la" and stops.

**After**

title: Margate Sands
body: Margate is a resort on the north Kent coast, where the Thames estuary opens to the sea. Eliot went there in the autumn of 1921, on three months' leave from Lloyds Bank after a breakdown, and stayed about three weeks, from late October to mid-November. On 4 November he told Sydney Schiff he had done "a rough draft of part of part III", written sitting in a shelter on the front. That part is usually identified as the pencil drafts of lines 266–311, from "The river sweats" to "burning". The Nayland Rock shelter on the promenade, which still stands, was given listed-building protection in 2009 as the likely place. Lyndall Gordon and others have read "I can connect / Nothing with nothing" as Eliot's own confession as well as the third daughter's.

## Gloss at lines 307–311, "To Carthage then I came"

**Before**

title: Augustine and the Buddha, burning
body: The opening of Book III of Augustine's <i>Confessions</i> (c. 400), given in Eliot's note in his own wording; E. B. Pusey's translation (1838), which is behind it, reads "To Carthage I came, where there sang all around me in my ears a cauldron of unholy loves." The Latin puns: Carthago, sartago, a frying pan. Augustine arrived in the city as a student and threw himself into love affairs and the theatre. Then the Buddha's Fire Sermon, which gives the section its title: "All things, O priests, are on fire . . . The eye, O priests, is on fire; forms are on fire . . . with the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation." Eliot read it at Harvard in Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896). Then Augustine again, Book X: "I . . . entangle my steps with these outward beauties; but Thou pluckest me out, O Lord, Thou pluckest me out."

Eliot's note says that putting "these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism" together "as the culmination of this part of the poem, is not an accident." The lines fall apart as you read them: a sentence, a word four times, a prayer, a prayer with its end torn off, one word alone. The fire that burns here is lust. The fire at line 428 refines.

**After**

title: Augustine and the Buddha
body: The opening of Book III of Augustine's <i>Confessions</i> (c. 400), in Pusey's translation (1838), given above; the line, like Eliot's note, rewords it. The Latin has a pun, Carthago and sartago, a frying pan, which is Pusey's "cauldron". Augustine had come to Carthage at about seventeen to study rhetoric, and Book III begins with his love affairs and the theatre. "Burning" is from the Buddha's Fire Sermon, which gives Part III its title: "All things, O priests, are on fire . . . The eye, O priests, is on fire; forms are on fire . . . with the fire of passion, say I, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of infatuation." Eliot's note sends readers to Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (Harvard Oriental Series, 1896). Eliot studied Sanskrit and Pali at Harvard in 1911–13, and it is generally assumed that he met the sermon there. "O Lord Thou pluckest me out" is Augustine again, Book X: "I . . . entangle my steps with these outward beauties; but Thou pluckest me out, O Lord, Thou pluckest me out." Eliot's note at line 309 says that putting "these two representatives of eastern and western asceticism" together here "is not an accident". Critics have connected this burning with the refining fire of <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, quoted at line 428.

## Gloss at lines 257–265, the Strand to Lower Thames Street

**Before**

title: From the Strand to Lower Thames Street
body: Ferdinand's line from <i>The Tempest</i> again (Eliot's note: "as above"), and then a walk: along the Strand, up Queen Victoria Street, which runs from Blackfriars to the Bank, and down to Lower Thames Street, where Billingsgate fish market stood and the "fishmen" drank at noon. The mandoline is real; so is the pub. And so is St Magnus the Martyr, Wren's church at the foot of the old London Bridge, rebuilt 1671–87, with a white and gold interior of Ionic columns. In 1920 a commission chaired by Lord Phillimore proposed pulling down nineteen City churches, St Magnus among them; the London County Council's report against the scheme, <i>The Proposed Demolition of Nineteen City Churches</i>, is the pamphlet Eliot's note cites, and the church is still there. "Inexplicable splendour" is the first unqualified praise of anything in the poem, and it is given to a building on a street of fish.

**After**

title: From the Strand to Lower Thames Street
body: Ferdinand's line in <i>The Tempest</i>, I, ii, from the speech already echoed at lines 191–192, which is what Eliot's "as above" refers to. The music goes along the Strand, up Queen Victoria Street, which runs from Blackfriars to the Bank, and down to Lower Thames Street, where Billingsgate fish market stood until 1982, hence the "fishmen". St Magnus the Martyr is Wren's church at the north end of the old London Bridge, rebuilt after the Great Fire, the body of the church complete by 1676; "Ionian" is usually taken to mean the Ionic columns that divide its nave from the aisles. In April 1920 a commission appointed by the Bishop of London and chaired by Lord Phillimore recommended removing nineteen City churches, St Magnus among them. The London County Council's reply, <i>The Proposed Demolition of Nineteen City Churches</i> (October 1920), is the pamphlet Eliot's note cites. It calls St Magnus "one of the most beautiful of all Wren's works" but finds the interior "disappointing"; Eliot's "to my mind one of the finest among Wren's interiors" takes the other side. The church stands; its interior was redecorated in 1924.

## Way in: Every bird

**Before**

title: Every bird
body: The nightingale, the gulls, the cock, the hermit-thrush, the swallow. The birds are the poem's musicians, and their songs are all complaints. A short walk with recordings where they exist.

**After**

title: Every bird
body: The Birds pathway stops at each bird in the poem in order: the nightingale (here at 203–206, and in Part II), the gulls of Part IV, the hermit-thrush and the cock of Part V, and the swallow at 429, with recordings where they exist.

## Way in: Fire

**Before**

title: Fire
body: The Water lens has a third colour for fire. In this section it appears at the end only, and all at once. Watch the river run through the whole section and then burn.

**After**

title: Fire
body: The Water lens marks fire in a third colour. In this section it falls on the last four lines only, where "burning", from the Buddha's sermon of the title, follows Augustine's "To Carthage then I came". Eliot's note says the placing of these two together "is not an accident".

## Way in: Whose side is the poem on

**Before**

title: Whose side is the poem on
body: The typist "smooths her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone." Contempt, or the only mercy in the poem? Readers have argued for a century. Read lines 249–256 once each way.

**After**

title: Whose side is the poem on
body: The typist "smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone" (255–256). Eliot's note gives Goldsmith's song, in which the woman who "stoops to folly" has nothing left but to die; Eliot's typist, instead, puts a record on. Critics have read the scene as contempt for her, and as the nearest the poem comes to pity; those who read it as pity point to Tiresias's "foresuffered all" (243).

## Water pathway, stop at line 182

**Before**

text: "By the waters of Leman I sat down and wept." Babylon's river, and Lake Geneva, where the poem was written.

**After**

text: "By the waters of Leman I sat down and wept." Psalm 137 in the Prayer Book wording, "By the waters of Babylon we sat down and wept", with Lake Geneva (Lac Léman) in Babylon's place. Eliot finished the poem at Lausanne, on the lake, in the winter of 1921–22.

## Cities pathway, stop at line 209

**Before**

text: Smyrna, which burned in September 1922.

**After**

text: Smyrna, now İzmir, the chief port of Asia Minor, through which the Levant's dried fruit was shipped; Eliot's note on 211 gives his reading of the line's trade shorthand.

## The poem in Eliot's life, stop at line 300

**Before**

text: Margate, October 1921: the Nayland Rock shelter, fifty lines of Part III.

**After**

text: Eliot spent three weeks at Margate in October and November 1921, on sick leave from the bank, and drafted "some fifty lines" of Part III in a shelter on the front, probably the Nayland Rock shelter, listed in 2009, which still stands. The lines are thought to be the river song and the Thames-daughters' speeches.

## Drafts page: the Thames-daughters

**Before**

title: The Thames-daughters, lengthened and shortened
body: The river song and the three daughters' speeches survive much as drafted. Pound's marks here are approving; he wrote "OK" and "echt" against the short-lined river passage. Eliot's note in the printed edition, numbering the daughters' turns, was written later, for the book.

**After**

title: The Thames-daughters in draft
body: The river song, the Elizabeth and Leicester lines and the three daughters' speeches are generally taken to be the "some fifty lines" Eliot drafted at Margate in November 1921. They reached print with few marks from Pound, and the words he did write here, "OK" and "echt" (genuine), are approving. Eliot's own revision shortened the first daughter's speech. His note, which identifies three daughters who "speak in turn" from 292 to 306, was added, like all the notes, for the book edition of December 1922.
