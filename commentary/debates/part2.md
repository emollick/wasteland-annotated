# Part II, A Game of Chess: the debate record

Lines 77 to 172. Two sides argued over every piece of commentary a reader can open from these lines: a scholar, briefed on the facsimile, Ricks and McCue, Southam, Rainey, Grover Smith, Kenner, Gardner, Gordon and Crawford, asked of each note whether it is true, how it is known, and whether a reading is being passed off as fact; a reader, an attentive non-specialist, asked whether it is clear, whether it helps with the line, whether a person would say it, and whether it is showing off. An editor ran the rounds, rewrote, and decided. Three rounds at most for any note. On substance the scholar had the last word, on wording the reader. What neither settled is written down at the end.

The standard came from Ethan's two examples of what had to go: a tour-guide heading ("Where the poem is standing") and a sentence built as an aphorism with a counted flourish ("The poem's argument is a weather report: rain that is unwelcome, rivers that are dirty, a sea that drowns, and then twenty-nine lines of rock before three words of rain"). What was aimed at instead: the plainness of a scholarly editor writing for readers. Say what the line refers to, what is known and how, what is disputed; when a reading is offered, say whose; then stop.

Sixty-two items were read: ten glosses, the nine ways into the part and their heading, eighteen pathway stops, the drafts record, and, from outside the part's own files, ten library notes, six voice notes, four tarot card notes and three listening-room entries that these lines open onto. The part's own files were edited in place. The rest went to the front-matter thread as proposals (shared-proposals-part2.md).


## What changed

Forty-five of the sixty-two items were rewritten: thirty in the part's own files (all ten glosses, nine of the nine ways in, ten of the eighteen pathway stops, the drafts record) and fifteen as proposals for the site-level files (nine library notes, three voice notes, one tarot note, two listening-room entries). Seventeen stand as they were: the heading "Nine ways into A Game of Chess", eight pathway stops that quote the poem and stop, the Milton note, three voice notes, three tarot notes and the Guinness entry.

**What the scholar corrected.** Vivien Eliot's "WONDERFUL" is pencilled beside the nervous dialogue, not the pub scene, and she supplied two lines of the pub scene (153 and 164), not one; three notes had this wrong. The surgeons in Webster's Devil's Law-Case are bribed before the stabbing, not watching it. The man's replies are not "all quotations": two are, one reworks Webster, the rest are his own. Ovid names the hoopoe himself and leaves the sisters' birds open; both sisters kill Itys. Lil has bad teeth, not none. The rag's chorus runs "Most intelligent, very elegant", which Eliot reverses; the Follies story was told three different ways and now follows Guerrieri's finding of 2017; the dying "O, o, o, o" is Hamlet's in the Folio only and Lear's in the Quarto only. The Licensing Act of 1921 made permanent hours first imposed during the war by government order; nothing supports "the same words". Eve is not a queen; Eliot changes one word of Enobarbus's first line, not of the passage. The phrase attributed to Eliot's 1927 essay on Middleton could not be found there and is gone. Pound's marginalia on Part II are not "the poem's most famous editing"; the blank margin beside "inviolable voice" proves nothing; the James source for "In the Cage" is a critics' suggestion, and the Sibyl one postdates the title; the two struck lines after "What is the wind doing?" cannot be given to Pound's hand. Ophelia has no "next scene"; the next is the Queen's report. "Rats' Alley" is on British trench maps, and whether Eliot knew one is not known. The 1922 book prints "tonight" at line 111, checked by the editor on two scans, which settles a doubt the scholar raised against the site's text.

**What the reader threw out.** Every sentence that described the poem instead of explaining it: "It is a parody, and it is also very beautiful, and it is meant to be both at once"; "Two more queens are folded into the furniture"; "Cleopatra, Dido, Eve: three women about to be undone in their gardens and palaces, and then Philomel"; "He is a man whose inner life is made of other people's lines. That is a diagnosis of the poem as much as of the marriage" (and the same phrase in two other places); "The high and the low do not so much collide as turn out to have been the same thing all along: Shakespeare is what you can hum"; "It is the most direct speech in the poem and the most overheard" (twice); "The section is a timetable"; "The section's subject is an absence, and the quotations are its shape"; "'a knock upon the door' is what people in Jacobean tragedy wait for"; "the poem's clock: time is up, for Lil, for Albert, for everyone in the bar, and for the woman in the chair upstairs"; "Count the women in this section"; "the same voice at two social altitudes"; "The syncopation is the joke, and the joke is what he hums instead of speaking"; "the poem's clock running out"; "he wrote nothing, because it was right"; "the most famous description of a woman in English drama"; "the same picture with the Tudors in it"; "It is never quite a person"; "on her way to the water". The reader also had every name identified where a note first uses it (Enobarbus, Vivien Eliot, Valerie Eliot, Pound, Lyly, Webster, Froude, Tiresias, the Fisher King) and asked for four things the notes lacked, which were added: the grammar of lines 77–96, the pub's words (demobbed, something o' that, get on with it, gammon, the beauty of it hot), lines 132–133, and the closed car at line 136.

**How the rounds went.** Round 1: both read all sixty-two items; the scholar marked each challenged fact as verified now, known from a named book, or unverifiable. Round 2: the editor rewrote forty-eight and both read the rewrites; the scholar answered every "confirm" the editor had asked, and confirmed against the books Southam's words on "jug", Unger's Lavinia reading, the deletion of "swinging" at line 78, the 1960 fair copy for the London Library, and the sequence in Webster. Round 3: the nine notes whose merged wording neither side had seen were read once more; the scholar changed two clauses, the reader nine sentences, and the reader's last line was that the part now reads in one voice. Where the two disagreed, substance went the scholar's way ("stumps" needs Unger and Lavinia or nothing; the gammon gets its plain sense; the Cockney claim about Guinness goes) and wording the reader's (the Follies story short in the gloss and full in the library; "photography" out of the gloss; "prostitute" rather than "solicit").

## Not settled

What the debate leaves open, and where to look. None of these is asserted in the notes as they now stand; each is worded there as a reading, or left out.

1. Whether a standard commentary draws Cleopatra, Dido and Eve together as ruined loves. The note says "commentators commonly read". Check Southam's Guide (notes to 77, 92, 98) and Grover Smith, T. S. Eliot's Poetry and Plays (1956); if one does, name him.
2. Vivien Eliot's pencil, word by word: where "WONDERFUL", "Yes & wonderful wonderful", "Don't see what you had in mind here" and "Splendid last lines" sit on the leaves; whether her line 164 read "dont want to have children"; whether 153 is in her hand on one typescript and Eliot's on the other. The 1971 facsimile, pp. 10–15 and Valerie Eliot's notes pp. 126–127; Ricks and McCue, textual notes to 153 and 164.
3. Whose hand struck "met first" (115) and the two lines after "What is the wind doing?", and whether Pound's marks on this part are pencil or ink. Facsimile pp. 12–13; Ricks and McCue, textual notes to 115 and 120.
4. Pound's "photography" beside the nervous dialogue: what he meant. Dropped from the notes. Facsimile p. 11 or 13; Brooker's chapter in the Cambridge Companion to The Waste Land (2015).
5. Whether "1880" is Pound's mark against "the closed carriage at four". Facsimile p. 13. The note says only that he queried it as dated.
6. The Follies. The notes follow Matthew Guerrieri, "That old classical drag" (2017), against B. R. McElderry (American Quarterly, 1957). Check Ricks and McCue on line 128 for anything later, and the Variety advertisement of 19 July 1912 that Guerrieri cites.
7. Whether the reading of "O O O O" as Hamlet's and Lear's has a named source. Ricks and McCue on line 128. The note says "some readers hear".
8. Whether any commentator links "With my hair down" (133) to Ophelia's stage direction, or line 172 to the song "Good Night, Ladies". Ricks and McCue and Rainey on 132–133 and 172. Both are worded as readings.
9. Whether Guinness's pub voice is Cockney. The site said so; the claim is out until someone listens to the Argo recording.
10. Whether any recording of "That Shakespearian Rag" from 1912–13 exists. The site's "no recording has been found" rests on the build thread's searches of the Library of Congress and the Internet Archive on 20 September 2026; the Discography of American Historical Recordings (UCSB) under Stamper, Buck and Ruby would settle it.
11. "Blind mate". The gloss "a mate that passes unnoticed" covers both the old sense (unnoticed by the giver) and Middleton's turn on the loser; the Revels edition of Women Beware Women (ed. Mulryne, 1975), note to II.ii, would give the exact sense.
12. Whether "the perpetual instinct of the man of genius" is anywhere in Eliot's "Thomas Middleton" (1927). Not found; out unless someone finds it.
13. Whether the typescript has "IT'S" with an apostrophe at 141, where 1922 prints "ITS". Facsimile p. 15. The note no longer claims anything about the shout.
14. The date of the Part II typescript, on which "less than three years before these lines were written, in 1921" rests. Rainey, The Annotated Waste Land, chronology; Gordon, Eliot's Early Years, dating appendix.
15. "Rats' Alley" as a mapped trench, if a place is wanted. Chasseaud, Rats Alley: Trench Names of the Western Front (2006; 2017), gazetteer.
