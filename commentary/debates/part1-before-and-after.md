# Part I: every note as it stood and as it now stands

Only the records whose text changed are listed; titles are given where they changed. The debate that produced each change is in part1.md.

## gloss title (line 0)

Before:

Eliot's first note says it plainly: the title, "the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism" came from Jessie L. Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920). Weston's book argues that the Grail romances preserve, under a Christian skin, a much older fertility cult: a land laid waste because its king is wounded or impotent, healed only when a stranger arrives and asks the right question. The waste land is the country, the king, and the reader who has not yet asked anything.

In the typescript the poem had a different working title, written over the first two parts: <i>He Do the Police in Different Voices</i>. It is Betty Higden's praise of the boy Sloppy in Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (1865): "You mightn't think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices." A poem that reads the news of the world aloud in as many voices as it can do. Keep that title in your head; it explains more than the Grail does.

After:

Eliot's first note says that "not only the title, but the plan and a good deal of the incidental symbolism of the poem" were suggested by Jessie L. Weston's <i>From Ritual to Romance</i> (1920). Weston argued that the Grail romances preserve, beneath their Christian story, the rites of a much older fertility cult. In her account the king is wounded or impotent, his land lies waste with him, and it is healed only when a stranger arrives and asks the right question. Eliot's note recommends the book to "any who think such elucidation of the poem worth the trouble."

The typescript had another title. Parts I and II are headed <i>He Do the Police in Different Voices</i>, a phrase from Dickens's <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (1864–65), Book I, chapter 16, where Betty Higden praises her foundling Sloppy: "You mightn't think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices." The heading was dropped before publication and is known from the drafts, published in facsimile in 1971.

## gloss epigraph (line 0)

Title before: The Sibyl in the bottle
Title after: The Sibyl in her jar

Before:

The speaker is Trimalchio, the vulgar millionaire host of the dinner party in Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i> (first century AD), boasting between courses. The Sibyl of Cumae had asked Apollo for as many years of life as there were grains in a handful of sand, and forgot to ask for youth. She shrank until she could be kept in a bottle. Her wish is the poem's first word on the subject of living on after the point of living has gone. Latin, then Greek, then the poem's English: three languages before the first line, and a handful of sand before the "handful of dust" at line 30.

This was not the first epigraph. The typescript carried Kurtz's dying words from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>: "Did he live his life again in every detail of desire, temptation, and surrender during that supreme moment of complete knowledge? He cried in a whisper at some image, at some vision,—he cried out twice, a cry that was no more than a breath—'The horror! the horror!'" Pound wrote in January 1922 that he doubted "if Conrad is weighty enough to stand the citation." Eliot answered that it was "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," and then replaced it anyway.

After:

The speaker is Trimalchio, a freed slave grown enormously rich, whose dinner party fills the longest surviving stretch of Petronius's <i>Satyricon</i> (first century AD). In chapter 48 he is boasting to his guests of what he has seen. The Sibyl's story is in Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV, 130–153: Apollo offered her whatever she wished; she picked up a heap of dust and asked for as many years as it had grains, and forgot to ask for youth. Ovid has her foretell that she will shrink until only her voice is left; the jar is Petronius's detail, and the boys' question and her answer are in Greek in his Latin. Ovid's word for what she held is dust, "pulvis," and line 30 of the poem ends in "a handful of dust."

This epigraph was not the first. The typescript carried the passage on Kurtz's death from Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, as Eliot typed it: "Did he live his life again in every detail of desire, temptation, and surrender during that supreme moment of complete knowledge? He cried in a whisper at some image, at some vision,—he cried out twice, a cry that was no more than a breath—'The horror! the horror!'" Pound wrote on 24 January 1922 that he doubted "if Conrad is weighty enough to stand the citation." Eliot answered that it was "much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative," and gave it up. The <i>Dial</i> printing of November 1922 carried the epigraph but not Eliot's notes; its editors added a footnote that an edition "with annotations by Mr Eliot" was coming from Boni &amp; Liveright. The <i>Criterion</i> printing of October 1922 had no notes either. The book followed in December 1922.

## gloss dedication (line 0)

Before:

<i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, line 117. The poet Guido Guinizelli, doing penance among the lustful in the refining fire, points Dante toward Arnaut Daniel, the Provençal troubadour, and calls him the better smith of his native speech. The same canto supplies the poem's last page: "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina" at line 428 is Arnaut stepping back into the flames.

The dedication is not in the 1922 printings. Eliot wrote it by hand in Pound's copy of the Boni &amp; Liveright edition in January 1923 and first printed it in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>. It credits the cutting, not the writing: Pound worked over the typescript in Paris in January 1922 and by post afterwards, and roughly halved it. His verdict, in a letter dated in his own invented calendar and read as either 24 December 1921 or 24 January 1922: "Complimenti, you bitch. I am wracked by the seven jealousies."

After:

The line is <i>Purgatorio</i> XXVI, 117. The poet Guido Guinizelli, doing penance among the lustful in the refining fire, points Dante toward Arnaut Daniel, the Provençal troubadour, and calls him the better smith of his native speech. The same canto is quoted near the end of the poem: "Poi s'ascose nel foco che gli affina" at line 428 is Arnaut stepping back into the flames. "Il miglior fabbro" was already Pound's phrase: it is the title of his chapter on Arnaut Daniel in <i>The Spirit of Romance</i> (1910).

The dedication is not in the printings of 1922, nor in the Hogarth Press edition of 1923. Eliot wrote it by hand in Pound's copy of the Boni &amp; Liveright edition, in January 1923 by Ricks and McCue's dating, and first printed it in <i>Poems 1909–1925</i>. ## gloss april (line 1)

Title before: Chaucer's April, turned inside out
Title after: Chaucer's April, reversed

Before:

The most famous opening in English poetry says that April is kind: rain ends the drought, sap rises, people want to go on pilgrimages. Eliot's first line answers it. Rain is still there ("stirring / Dull roots with spring rain"), but it is unwelcome, because it stirs "memory and desire" in something that would rather stay numb. Winter was kinder: "forgetful snow," "a little life with dried tubers." Read the first seven lines aloud and notice how five of them end on a present participle left hanging, breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding, so that the verbs never quite arrive at their objects. Growth that will not finish.

Behind Chaucer stand two other Aprils. Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" (1865) mourns Lincoln with lilacs, spring, and a hermit thrush, and that thrush turns up at line 357 of this poem. And Rupert Brooke, dead in April 1915, whose "The Old Vicarage, Grantchester" opens with the lilac in bloom. The line's "us" is a group that never gets named. It might be the leisured Europeans of the next paragraph, or the dead, or you.

After:

Chaucer's opening welcomes April. Its showers end the drought of March, the sap rises, and people set out on pilgrimages. Eliot's first line answers it; every commentary notes the echo. The rain is still there ("stirring / Dull roots with spring rain"), but the month is called cruel because what the rain stirs is "memory and desire". Winter's snow, by contrast, was "forgetful". Five of the first seven lines end on a participle (breeding, mixing, stirring, covering, feeding) whose object is held over to the next line.

Two other lilac poems are usually mentioned. Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd" (1865) mourns Lincoln, who was shot in April; it has lilacs, "ever-returning spring" and a hermit thrush, and Eliot's own note to line 357 names the hermit-thrush. Rupert Brooke's "The Old Vicarage, Grantchester", written in Berlin in May 1912, opens "Just now the lilac is in bloom"; Brooke died in April 1915. The Whitman connection is generally accepted; the Brooke one is a suggestion. "Us" in line 5 is never identified. The same verse paragraph runs on without a break through the Munich lines, which begin at line 8, to line 18.

## gloss boston-cut (line 1)

Before:

In the typescript, "April is the cruellest month" was not the first line. It was preceded by fifty-four lines of a night on the town in Boston, in the voice of a young man out with his friends: a bar, a burlesque show, a drunken run from a policeman, a sunrise. The whole passage was struck through with a single pencil line, apparently by Eliot himself before Pound saw it. What survived is the second beginning, a poem that starts already exhausted. The manuscript is at the New York Public Library's Berg Collection and is reproduced in the 1971 facsimile edited by Valerie Eliot.

After:

In the typescript, "April is the cruellest month" is not the first line. Fifty-four lines come before it. They tell of a night out in Boston, in the voice of one of a party of young men: drinks at "Tom's place," a show, then a call alone at "Myrtle's place" at two in the morning, where he is told he is too drunk; a policeman picks him up for "committing a nuisance," a man he knows gets him off, and he leaves their cab at sunrise to walk home. The passage is struck through in pencil. Valerie Eliot's facsimile attributes the stroke to Eliot, and Pound's annotations begin only after it, which is why the cut is credited to Eliot rather than Pound. The typescript is in the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library, among John Quinn's papers, and is reproduced in the facsimile edition of 1971.

## gloss starnbergersee (line 8)

Title before: Munich, August 1911
Title after: The lake and the Hofgarten

Before:

The Starnberger See is the long lake south of Munich; the Hofgarten is the court garden in the city centre, with an arcaded walk on two sides, which is the "colonnade" the speakers shelter in. Eliot spent August 1911 in Munich, at the end of his year in Paris, and finished "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" there. The lake is also where King Ludwig II of Bavaria, Wagner's patron and the builder of Neuschwanstein, was found drowned in June 1886, a death by water for the king who paid for <i>Tristan und Isolde</i>. The poem never says so. It only stops for coffee.

After:

The Starnberger See (one word in the poem) is the long lake south-west of Munich; the Hofgarten is the court garden beside the Residenz in the city centre, with arcades along two sides, which are probably the "colonnade". In the typescript the lake was the Königssee, in the far south-east of Bavaria; Eliot changed it (the typescript is reproduced in the 1971 facsimile). He was in Munich in July and August 1911, at the end of his year in Paris, and finished "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" there. King Ludwig II of Bavaria, Wagner's patron and the builder of Neuschwanstein, was found dead in the lake on 13 June 1886; the verdict was suicide by drowning, though the autopsy found no water in his lungs. Commentators, Southam among them, have set that death beside the poem's "death by water"; the poem itself says nothing of it. The lake is also in Countess Marie Larisch's memoir (see the note at line 15): her aunt the Empress Elisabeth spent her childhood summers at Possenhofen on its shore.

## gloss marie (line 15)

Before:

Valerie Eliot, in the facsimile edition, reported that her husband had met Countess Marie Larisch (1858–1940), a niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria, and that the sledding story and the tone of these lines came from her conversation, not from any book. Larisch had been the go-between in the affair of her cousin Crown Prince Rudolf and Mary Vetsera, which ended with both dead at Mayerling in 1889; her memoir <i>My Past</i> (1913) made her notorious. "The archduke's, my cousin's" fits Rudolf. What the poem takes from her is a voice: a displaced aristocrat of a vanished empire, at once frightened and free, reading much of the night. "In the mountains, there you feel free" is the poem's first quotation of someone who is not sure they mean it.

After:

Lines 8–18 draw on Countess Marie Larisch (1858–1940), a niece of the Empress Elisabeth of Austria. G. L. K. Morris pointed out in 1954 ("Marie, Marie, Hold On Tight," <i>Partisan Review</i>) that her memoir <i>My Past</i> (1913) has the archduke who was her cousin, the Starnbergersee, the mountains where one feels free, the reading late at night and the winters in the south. Valerie Eliot's note in the 1971 facsimile adds that Eliot had met her, though when and where is not known, and that the sledding, which is not in the book, came word for word from her conversation. Larisch had carried messages between her cousin Crown Prince Rudolf and Mary Vetsera before the two were found dead at Mayerling in January 1889; she was banished from court, and the memoir revived the scandal. "The archduke's, my cousin's" fits Rudolf.

## gloss litauen (line 12)

Title before: A café sentence
Title after: German, not Russian

Before:

Overheard, unattributed, in a language the reader may not have. After 1918 the old multinational empires had become new nation states, and a woman from the Baltic gentry could be Russian by passport, Lithuanian by birthplace and German by blood, and need to say so quickly. The poem's first foreign sentence is about identity, and it is defensive.

After:

The line is in German. "Russin" is feminine, so the speaker is a woman. Whether she is the Marie of the lines that follow, a companion, or someone overheard, the poem does not say; the site's Voices lens gives the line to a separate voice. Lithuania was part of the Russian Empire until 1918 and a republic after it; either way a German speaker from there might expect to be taken for Russian and want to correct it. It is the first line of the poem proper not in English.

## gloss son-of-man (line 20)

Title before: The prophet's address
Title after: "Son of man" in Ezekiel

Before:

Eliot's note points to Ezekiel 2:1, where God addresses the prophet as "son of man" before sending him to a rebellious people. The next chapter has the prophet eat a scroll; chapter 37 has him prophesy over a valley of dry bones until they stand up as an army ("Dry bones can harm no one," says line 391, with the same book in mind). "A heap of broken images" is Ezekiel too: the Lord promises to break the idols of Israel (6:4–6). The voice that speaks here is not the café voice of Marie. It is the one Eliot would use again in "Ash-Wednesday" and the choruses of <i>The Rock</i>, the desert voice, and it has been given a stony landscape, a "red rock," and a question with no answer: what grows here?

Eliot's second note, on line 23, sends you to Ecclesiastes 12:5, the chapter on old age where "the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail: because man goeth to his long home, and the mourners go about the streets." Cricket, grasshopper: the same small dry noise.

After:

Eliot's note points to Ezekiel 2:1, where God addresses the prophet as "son of man" and sends him to "a rebellious nation" (2:3). The address runs through the book; in chapter 37 the prophet is set down in a valley of dry bones and told to prophesy until they stand up, and "Dry bones can harm no one" (line 391) is usually taken to recall that chapter. "A heap of broken images" is usually traced to Ezekiel 6:4–6, where the idols and images of Israel are to be broken. The voice here is unlike anything in the first eighteen lines; readers have compared it to "Ash-Wednesday" (1930) and the choruses of <i>The Rock</i> (1934).

Eliot's note on line 23 points to Ecclesiastes 12:5, in the chapter on old age: "the grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail: because man goeth to his long home, and the mourners go about the streets." The grasshopper there is a weight, not a sound: in age even so light a thing is a burden. Eliot's cricket gives "no relief."

## gloss red-rock (line 25)

Before:

"Come in under the shadow of this red rock" is lifted, almost word for word, from a poem Eliot wrote in 1915 and withdrew from publication, "The Death of Saint Narcissus," where the invitation is to come in under the shadow of a gray rock and be shown something different from two shadows, one sprawling over the sand at daybreak, the other leaping behind a fire against a red rock. The saint's grey rock has turned red. The two shadows, morning and evening, are a sundial reading of a whole life, and the line that follows, "I will show you fear in a handful of dust," carries Isaiah's "shadow of a great rock in a weary land" (32:2), the Sibyl's handful of sand, and Tennyson's <i>Maud</i>: "Dead, long dead, / Long dead! / And my heart is a handful of dust."

After:

"Come in under the shadow of this red rock" comes almost unchanged from "The Death of Saint Narcissus," a poem Eliot wrote about 1915 and withdrew from <i>Poetry</i> (Chicago) after it had been set in type; it was first printed in 1950 and is reproduced in the 1971 facsimile. There the rock of shelter is grey, and the speaker promises to show something different from two shadows, one sprawling over the sand at daybreak, the other leaping behind a fire against a red rock. Line 27 is carried over word for word. For the shelter of lines 25–26 commentators cite Isaiah 32:2, "the shadow of a great rock in a weary land." The two shadows of lines 28–29, behind you in the morning and rising to meet you at evening, have been read as a life from birth to death. For "a handful of dust" editors cite Tennyson's <i>Maud</i> (Part II, section V): "Dead, long dead, / Long dead! / And my heart is a handful of dust." The Sibyl of the epigraph, in Ovid, asked for as many years as there were grains in a heap of dust.

## gloss frisch-weht (line 31)

Before:

<i>Tristan und Isolde</i> opens with this song, sung by a young sailor high in the rigging, unseen, as Tristan's ship carries Isolde from Ireland to Cornwall to marry a king she does not love. Isolde hears it as mockery. The wind blows toward the sailor's home and away from hers. Eliot's note gives the reference and nothing else; the four short lines sit in the poem like a snatch of song heard through a wall, and their subject, love that has not happened yet and will end in death, opens the hyacinth garden that follows. The reply comes at line 42.

After:

These are the first words sung in Wagner's <i>Tristan und Isolde</i>. A young sailor sings them from high in the rigging, heard but not seen, as Tristan's ship carries Isolde from Ireland to Cornwall, where she is to marry King Marke, Tristan's uncle. The four lines before the ones Eliot quotes give the ship's course ("westward roams the gaze; eastward runs the ship"), so the wind that blows "toward home" blows toward the sailor's Cornwall and away from Isolde's Ireland. She takes the song as mockery; her first words in the opera are "Who dares to mock me?" Eliot's note gives the act and the verse numbers, 5 to 8, and nothing else. His second quotation from the opera, at line 42, comes from its last act.

## gloss hyacinth-girl (line 36)

Before:

Hyacinthus was the boy Apollo loved and killed by accident with a discus; the flower grew from his blood, and in Ovid its petals carry the letters of a cry of grief (<i>Metamorphoses</i> X). In the Greek world the hyacinth had its own festival, the Hyacinthia at Sparta, and Hyacinth is, in Frazer's reading, another dying and reviving god. All of that is available, and none of it explains the passage, which is one of the few places in the poem where a speaker admits to having felt something. "I could not / Speak, and my eyes failed, I was neither / Living nor dead, and I knew nothing, / Looking into the heart of light, the silence." The heart of light is Conrad's heart of darkness turned inside out, and it is also the mystical blank that Eliot's later poems circle.

Two lives have been read into the garden. Lyndall Gordon's biographies attach it to Emily Hale, the Bostonian Eliot loved before he sailed for Europe in 1914; his letters to her, unsealed at Princeton in 2020, show how long that attachment lasted. Others hear Jean Verdenal, the friend of Eliot's Paris year, killed at the Dardanelles in 1915, to whom <i>Prufrock and Other Observations</i> is dedicated. In 1934 Eliot remembered "a friend coming across the Luxembourg Gardens in the late afternoon, waving a branch of lilac, a friend who was later (so far as I could find out) to be mixed with the mud of Gallipoli." Lilacs, line 2. The poem does not choose.

After:

Hyacinthus was the boy Apollo loved and killed by accident with a discus. The flower sprang from his blood, and in Ovid its petals carry the letters AI AI, a cry of grief (<i>Metamorphoses</i> X, 162–219). Sparta kept a three-day festival for him at Amyclae, the Hyacinthia, which began with a day of mourning and ended in rejoicing. In <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, the volumes Eliot's headnote names, Frazer gives him a short chapter at the end of the Attis book. He writes there that Hyacinth "has been interpreted as the vegetation which blooms in spring and withers". None of this is needed to read the lines. The commentaries give it because Eliot's headnote promises readers of Frazer "certain references to vegetation ceremonies", and the hyacinth is usually counted as one. Annotators set "neither / Living nor dead" beside Dante's words at the sight of Satan, <i>Inferno</i> XXXIV 25: "Io non mori' e non rimasi vivo" (I did not die and did not stay alive). "The heart of light" has been read as an inversion of the title of Conrad's <i>Heart of Darkness</i>, the book the typescript's epigraph came from (see the note on the epigraph). Eliot used the phrase again in the first section of <i>Burnt Norton</i> (1936).

Two people in Eliot's life have been proposed for the figure in the garden; the text names neither. Lyndall Gordon, from <i>Eliot's Early Years</i> (1977) to <i>The Hyacinth Girl</i> (2022), attaches it to Emily Hale of Boston, to whom Eliot declared his love before sailing for Europe in 1914; his 1,131 letters to her, opened at Princeton in January 2020, show how long the attachment lasted. John Peter's 1952 essay read the poem as an elegy for a dead friend, and his 1969 postscript named the friend as Jean Verdenal; James E. Miller (1977) made the same identification. Verdenal was the friend of Eliot's Paris year, killed at the Dardanelles in May 1915, to whom <i>Prufrock and Other Observations</i> (1917) is dedicated. In the <i>Criterion</i> for April 1934 Eliot recalled that friend crossing the Luxembourg Gardens "waving a branch of lilac", and his death in "the mud of Gallipoli". Compare the lilacs of line 2.

## gloss oed-und-leer (line 42)

Title before: Empty and desolate the sea
Title after: The shepherd's answer

Before:

Act III of <i>Tristan</i>. Tristan lies dying in Brittany, waiting for Isolde's ship. A shepherd keeps watch and is to play a merry tune the moment a sail appears. Asked whether he sees anything, he answers with these four words. Between line 31 (the ship setting out, love ahead) and line 42 (the ship not coming, love behind), the hyacinth garden happens. Eliot's note, "Id. III, verse 24," is as brief as the shepherd.

After:

The words are from Act III of <i>Tristan und Isolde</i>. Tristan lies wounded at Kareol, his castle in Brittany, and his servant Kurwenal has sent to Cornwall for Isolde, the only one who can heal him. A shepherd keeps watch and is to play a merry tune the moment a sail appears; told to look, he looks out to sea and answers with these four words. Eliot's note, "Id. III, verse 24," points back to the note on line 31 ("Id." is <i>idem</i>, the same work), and the count is exact: the line is the twenty-fourth sung in the act. <i>Öd</i> is the German for desolate or waste, as in <i>Ödland</i>, waste land. This quotation and the one at line 31 stand on either side of the hyacinth garden. That one comes from the opening of the opera, before Tristan and Isolde drink the love potion; this one from its last act, when they are apart and Tristan is dying.

## gloss sosostris (line 43)

Before:

The name is borrowed and bent. In Aldous Huxley's novel <i>Crome Yellow</i>, published in November 1921 while Eliot was drafting, the cynic Mr. Scogan dresses up at a village fair as "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana" to tell fortunes. Sesostris was a legendary Egyptian pharaoh. Eliot feminises the fake and gives her a head cold. "Clairvoyante," with the French feminine ending, is the trade term; "the wisest woman in Europe" is the trade puff. She is the poem's most fully drawn comic character, and she is also its first prophet, since everything she deals turns out to be true.

"One must be so careful these days": fortune-telling for money was an offence under the Vagrancy Act of 1824, and prosecutions of palmists and mediums were routine in the London of the 1920s. She is not being philosophical. She is afraid of the police.

After:

The name is usually traced to Aldous Huxley's <i>Crome Yellow</i>, published in November 1921, where Mr. Scogan, dressed as a gipsy woman, reads palms at the charity fair in the grounds of Crome under a placard announcing "Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana". Sesostris was a legendary Egyptian king. Eliot, asked by Grover Smith in 1952, said he was "almost certain" he had taken the name from the novel without knowing it. Lawrence Rainey has since dated the typescript of Parts I and II to May 1921, before Huxley wrote the book, so the debt is in doubt. The spelling and the head cold are Eliot's own. "Clairvoyante," with the French feminine ending, is the trade term; "the wisest woman in Europe" is the trade puff. Eliot's note ties her cards to what follows: the Sailor, the Merchant and the crowds "appear later", and "Death by Water is executed in Part IV".

Her parting line, "One must be so careful these days" (line 59), is usually read as a fortune-teller's caution about the law. Section 4 of the Vagrancy Act 1824 made "every person pretending or professing to tell fortunes" a rogue and vagabond, and palmists and mediums were still being prosecuted under it in the 1920s.

## gloss tarot (line 46)

Title before: The cards, one by one
Title after: The cards she deals

Before:

Eliot's own note confesses that he did not know the Tarot well and "departed from it to suit my own convenience." What she deals:

<b>The drowned Phoenician Sailor.</b> Not a Tarot card. He is Phlebas, who gets Part IV to himself, and the merchant of Part III, and Ferdinand in <i>The Tempest</i>, whose father is the one lying "full fathom five" with pearls for eyes.

<b>Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks.</b> "Beautiful lady" in Italian, also the poisonous nightshade, also the eye-drops women used to dilate their pupils. Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i> stands behind the title; the society woman of "A Game of Chess" stands in front of it. "The lady of situations" sounds like a phrase from the gossip columns.

<b>The man with three staves.</b> The Three of Wands, a real card: a figure on a headland with his back to us, watching ships. Eliot says he associated it, "quite arbitrarily," with the Fisher King.

<b>The Wheel.</b> The Wheel of Fortune, card X. "O you who turn the wheel," line 320.

<b>The one-eyed merchant.</b> Not a card; Mr. Eugenides of Smyrna, seen in profile at line 209, with his blank card of something carried on his back.

<b>The Hanged Man.</b> Card XII: a young man hanging serenely by one foot from a living tree, a halo round his head. Eliot links him to Frazer's hanged god, the sacrificed king whose death brings the rain, and to the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. Madame Sosostris cannot find him. No sacrifice, no resurrection, is in this reading. "Fear death by water" is the reading's one instruction, and it is the one thing the Grail legend says you should not fear: water is what the waste land needs.

The cards shown here are Pamela Colman Smith's designs for the pack published in London by William Rider &amp; Son in 1909, the Tarot that an English buyer of the 1910s would have found in a shop.

After:

Eliot's note says he was "not familiar with the exact constitution of the Tarot pack" and had "obviously departed" from it "to suit my own convenience". Weston, in her chapter on the Grail symbols, suggests that the Tarot's original use "would seem to have been, not to foretell the Future in general, but to predict the rise and fall of the waters which brought fertility to the land". The cards, in the order Madame Sosostris names them, are these.

<b>The drowned Phoenician Sailor.</b> There is no such card in the pack. Eliot's note says the Sailor and the Merchant "appear later". The Sailor is Phlebas the Phoenician of Part IV, "Death by Water", already "a fortnight dead" when the section opens. His note on line 218 adds that the one-eyed merchant "melts into the Phoenician Sailor", who "is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples" in <i>The Tempest</i>. Line 48, the line in brackets, is from a song telling Ferdinand that his father has drowned; see the note there.

<b>Belladonna, the Lady of the Rocks.</b> Belladonna is Italian for "beautiful lady". It is also the name of the deadly nightshade, and of the drops made from it that women used to widen their pupils. "The Lady of the Rocks" is usually taken to echo Leonardo's <i>Virgin of the Rocks</i>.

<b>The man with three staves.</b> The Three of Wands, which Eliot calls "an authentic member of the Tarot pack". In the Rider pack a man stands at a cliff's edge with his back to us, watching ships. Eliot "quite arbitrarily" associated him with the Fisher King.

<b>The Wheel.</b> The Wheel of Fortune, card X. Compare "O you who turn the wheel" at line 320.

<b>The one-eyed merchant.</b> There is no such card in the pack. Eliot's note says the Merchant appears later; he is Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant, at line 209. Cleanth Brooks (1939) took "one-eyed", on the card, to mean no more than that the face is shown in profile, and the same word, once Eliot applies it to Mr. Eugenides, to mean a defect, like Madame Sosostris's cold.

<b>The Hanged Man.</b> Card XII. In the Rider pack a young man hangs by one foot from a T-shaped gallows of living wood, a halo round his head, his face calm. Eliot's note associates him with "the Hanged God of Frazer" and with the hooded figure on the road to Emmaus in Part V. "The Hanged God" is a chapter in <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, on Attis, Marsyas and Odin, gods hanged on trees. Madame Sosostris does not find him. Brooks, who read her whole fortune as coming true in the poem, ties the card she cannot find to the hooded figure of Part V: "That he is hooded accounts for Madame Sosostris' inability to see him." Her one piece of advice, "Fear death by water", is answered by Eliot's note: "Death by Water is executed in Part IV."

The cards shown here are Pamela Colman Smith's designs for the pack published in London by William Rider &amp; Son in December 1909, the English pack of Eliot's time.

## gloss london-bridge-dead (line 63)

Before:

<i>Inferno</i> III, lines 55–57. Just inside the gate, before the river, Dante sees the neutrals, the souls who lived "without infamy and without praise," and is astonished at how many there are. After:

<i>Inferno</i> III, 55–57. Just inside the gate of Hell, before the river Acheron, Dante sees the souls of those who lived "without infamy and without praise" (III, 36), refused by Heaven and by Hell alike, and is astonished at how many there are. Line 64, "Sighs, short and infrequent, were exhaled," comes from the next canto, Limbo, where the unbaptised and the virtuous pagans live, in Dante's words, "without hope, in desire", and there is no weeping, only sighs that make the eternal air tremble (IV, 25–42). In the same talk he called this crowd "my city clerks trooping over London Bridge from the railway station to their offices". He was one of them. ## gloss stetson (line 69)

Before:

Mylae is the sea battle of 260 BC in which Rome first beat Carthage at sea, early in the First Punic War, a trade war between two commercial empires. The friend met in the City of London, the financial centre of another commercial empire four years after another war, was "in the ships" there. The war dead and the office crowd are the same people, and all wars are the one war. Stetson has often been taken for Pound, who wore wide-brimmed hats; nothing in the poem needs him to be anyone in particular. The name is a hat, an American hat, on a bridge in London.

After:

Mylae was the battle of 260 BC, early in the First Punic War, in which Rome beat Carthage at sea for the first time; the identification is F. O. Matthiessen's (1935). Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the Punic War as a trade war, and so as a parallel to the war of 1914–18: in his words, "all the wars are one war". The speaker greets a friend in the City of London, a few years after the Armistice, as someone who was "in the ships" with him at Mylae. The meeting is in King William Street, where the crowd has just arrived, not on the bridge.

Stetson is an American name and the name of an American hat. He has often been taken for Pound, who wore a wide-brimmed hat; Southam's <i>Guide</i> records the identification. Valerie Eliot, answering it in a letter to the <i>Times Literary Supplement</i> (11 May 1973), wrote that Eliot was not referring to anyone in particular but "simply meant any superior bank clerk", a man in a bowler hat, black jacket and striped trousers. Southam's <i>Guide</i> also records a suggestion that links the name to the felt slouch hat, a kind of stetson, worn by the Australian and New Zealand troops of 1914–18; Stetson himself has been linked by Robert Crawford (1987) and Donald Childs (1988) to Kipling's bank clerk Charlie Mears, in "The Finest Story in the World", who remembers rowing in an ancient galley. Nothing in the poem requires him to be any of these.

## gloss corpse (line 71)

Before:

Frazer's <i>The Golden Bough</i>, in the volumes Eliot's headnote singles out (<i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>), describes the "gardens of Adonis": pots of quick-sprouting seed carried in the god's funeral procession and thrown into the sea, and the "Osiris beds," effigies of the god filled with earth and corn and buried so that the sprouting grain showed his resurrection. The corpse in Stetson's suburban garden is that ritual gone domestic and gone wrong. Will it bloom? Has the frost got at it? The question is asked in the voice of a man talking about bulbs, and it is the question of the whole poem: can anything come back?

After:

In <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i>, the two volumes of <i>The Golden Bough</i> that Eliot's headnote names as "Atthis Adonis Osiris", Frazer describes two rites of gods who die and return. The "gardens of Adonis" were pots of earth sown with quick-sprouting wheat and barley, tended for eight days, then carried out with the images of the dead god and thrown into the sea or into springs. Effigies of Osiris made of earth and corn were buried at the Egyptian festival of sowing and taken up when the grain had sprouted. Corn-stuffed figures of the god have also been found in tombs. The sprouting of the grain, Frazer says, signified his resurrection. Eliot's headnote promises that readers of Frazer will "immediately recognise in the poem certain references to vegetation ceremonies". This is the plainest of them. The questions are the ones a man asks a neighbour about his bulbs.

## gloss dog (line 74)

Title before: Webster's dirge, with one word changed
Title after: Webster's dirge, altered

Before:

Cornelia's mad song over her murdered son in <i>The White Devil</i> (1612), Act V scene iv. Eliot swaps the wolf for a dog and "foe" for "friend," and capitalises the Dog. The domestic pet, man's friend, becomes the thing that will not let the buried god stay buried long enough to sprout. Readers who want more have found the Dog Star, Sirius, whose rising announced the flooding of the Nile and the return of Osiris. The change of one word is the kind of thing Eliot meant when he wrote, in "Philip Massinger" (1920), that "immature poets imitate; mature poets steal."

After:

Cornelia's song over her murdered son Marcello in Webster's <i>The White Devil</i> (1612), Act V scene iv. The stage direction says she sings it "in several forms of distraction". Eliot alters the couplet in six places. Webster's "But keep the wolf far thence" becomes "Oh keep the Dog far hence", the Dog with a capital; "foe to men" becomes "friend to men"; "For" becomes "Or", and "them" becomes "it". In Webster the wolf digs up the unburied dead; in Eliot the danger to the corpse in the garden is the dog, man's friend. Cleanth Brooks (1939) read the capitalised Dog as humanitarianism, the modern concern for man that digs up what was buried and so prevents its return; some annotators see the Dog Star, Sirius, whose rising marked the flooding of the Nile in the Egyptian calendar Frazer describes. Eliot's own account of such borrowing is in "Philip Massinger" (1920): "Immature poets imitate; mature poets steal; bad poets deface what they take, and good poets make it into something better, or at least something different."

The typescript of Part I opens with fifty-four lines in a loose, chatty verse about a night out with the boys in Boston: drinks at "Tom's place," a burlesque show, a girl, a run from a policeman, a sunrise walk home. In the typescript, Part I opens with fifty-four lines about a night out in Boston, told by one of a party of young men in loose, chatty verse: drinks at "Tom's place"; a show; a call at Myrtle's, where he is refused but given a bed, a bath and breakfast; a brush with a policeman on the way out, which a man he knows smooths over; and a walk home at sunrise.
