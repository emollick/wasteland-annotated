Each entry gives the file and record, the present text (as of 18:51 UTC), the proposed text and the reason, with the source for any fact.

## 2. The library (site/sources.txt)

The front-matter thread's rewrite made nearly every correction this debate's scholars had asked for, and the reader found nothing left in it that reads as a model's flourish. What follows is what its text still lacks or gets wrong (the scholars, round two, each point checked against the source named) and the sentences the reader still stumbled on (round two, with the exact wording asked for). In the file's order. Quotation marks are curly, as in the file.

### tristan
Present: “…and wrote a poem about it, “Opera,” dated November 1909; Stravinsky, who met him in 1956, thought <i>Tristan</i> “must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life.””
Proposed: “…and wrote a poem about it, “Opera,” dated November 1909. Stravinsky, who met him in December 1956, wrote in “Memories of T. S. Eliot” (<i>Esquire</i>, August 1965) that <i>Tristan</i> “must have been one of the most passionate experiences of his life.””
Reason: the quotation has no source in the note; it is from Stravinsky’s memoir in <i>Esquire</i>, 1 August 1965 (quoted by Jewel Spears Brooker, <i>Time Present</i> 101, 2020), and the meeting was in London in December 1956. The reader lost the thread at the semicolon that joins 1909 to 1956.

### baudelaire-au-lecteur
Present: DATE “1857”. NOTE: “Baudelaire opens his book by listing the sins of mankind, saving the worst for last, boredom, and then turning on whoever is reading. Eliot closes his first section the same way. He said in 1930 that Baudelaire was “the greatest exemplar in modern poetry in any language,” and in 1950 that he had learned from him “the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric.””
Proposed: DATE “1855; the preface poem of the 1857 volume”. NOTE: “Baudelaire opens his book by listing the sins of mankind, keeping the worst, boredom, for last, and then turning on whoever is reading. Eliot closes his first section the same way. In his essay “Baudelaire” (1930) he called him “the greatest exemplar in modern poetry in any language,” and in “What Dante Means to Me” (1950) he said he had learned from him “the possibility of fusion between the sordidly realistic and the phantasmagoric.””
Reason: the poem was first printed on 1 June 1855 in the <i>Revue des deux mondes</i>, at the head of the eighteen poems that first carried the title <i>Les Fleurs du mal</i>; 1857 is the book. A reader who wants the two quotations needs the essays they come from. “Saving the worst for last, boredom” made the reader read the clause twice.

### death-of-st-narcissus
Present: the whole note.
Proposed: “The poem was set in type for <i>Poetry</i> (Chicago) in 1915 and withdrawn in proof; it was first published in 1950. It is about a saint who believes he has been a tree, a fish and a young girl, and who dies pierced with arrows. Two manuscripts, a rough draft and a fair copy, survived among the papers Eliot sent in 1922 to John Quinn, the New York lawyer and collector, with the <i>Waste Land</i> drafts; they are now in the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library and are reproduced in the 1971 facsimile. After these two lines the speaker promises to show the listener something other than the listener’s own shadow, sprawling on the sand at daybreak or leaping behind the fire against a red rock. <i>The Waste Land</i> keeps the second line, with “gray” changed to “red,” and reshapes the first. The poem is still in copyright, so only its first two lines are given here.”
Reason: there are two manuscripts, both reproduced in Valerie Eliot’s facsimile, which is where a reader of this site can see the poem, and the note did not say so. The early poem already has a red rock, behind the fire, in its fifth line, so “‘gray’ changed to ‘red’” concerns only the rock of shelter; as the note stood, a reader would take the red to be <i>The Waste Land</i>’s. The reader asked for a subject in the opening sentence and one tense in “is sure he had been.”

### huxley-crome-yellow
Present: “who dresses up as an old gypsy woman to tell fortunes at the village fête under the name “Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana,” is the accepted source of Madame Sosostris.”
Proposed: “who dresses up as an old gypsy woman to tell fortunes at the charity fair in the park under the name “Sesostris, the Sorceress of Ecbatana,” is the source usually given for Madame Sosostris.” And, after the sentence on the 1952 letter: “Lawrence Rainey (<i>Revisiting The Waste Land</i>, 2005) has doubted it: he dates the typescript of Parts I and II to May 1921 from its paper and typewriter, before Huxley had finished the novel, and suggests the name was Eliot’s own.”
Reason: Huxley’s event is “the Fair,” the annual charity fair in the park at Crome (chapters XXV and XXVII), not a village fête. “The accepted source” hides a real difficulty: Rainey’s dating of the typescript puts it before the novel was finished (August 1921). Eliot’s letter of 1952 says he was “almost certain” of the debt; the note should carry both, as the Part I gloss on line 43 now does.

### dante-inf3
Present: the first sentence (“Inside the gate … stung by wasps”) and the last (“Eliot said in 1950 that …”).
Proposed: “Inside the gate (“Abandon all hope”) but before the river Acheron, Dante finds the souls who were neither good nor bad, “who lived without infamy and without praise.” Heaven has cast them out and Hell will not receive them; they run for ever after a banner, stung by wasps.” … “Eliot said in “What Dante Means to Me” (1950) that Dante’s poetry was still, “after forty years,” “the most persistent and deepest influence” on his own verse.”
Reason: the reader wanted to stop at the Dante quotation and the sentence would not let him; the talk should be named (given at the Italian Institute, London, 4 July 1950; in <i>To Criticize the Critic</i>).

### dante-inf4
Present: “The comparison likens the sighs of the office crowd to those of Limbo”.
Proposed: “The comparison likens the sighs of the crowd on London Bridge to those of Limbo”.
Reason: “office” is not in the poem, and a reader coming from line 64 knows this crowd as the one on the bridge.

### chaucer (optional)
Present: “birds singing through the night”.
Reason: an inference. Chaucer’s birds “maken melodye” and “slepen al the nyght with open ye” (General Prologue 9–10); “birds singing” would be closer.

### tennyson
Present: the note from “The mad speaker” to the end.
Proposed: “The mad speaker of <i>Maud</i>, who imagines himself buried under a city street with the traffic going over him, is the source usually given for “a handful of dust” (line 30), and the passage has been compared with the “rats’ alley” of Part II. The Prince’s song in <i>The Princess</i>, with Swinburne’s “Itylus” (“Swallow, my sister, O sister swallow”) beside it, is the source usually given for “O swallow swallow” at line 429, the English half of a line whose Latin half is from the <i>Pervigilium Veneris</i>. In his essay “In Memoriam” (1936) Eliot wrote that Tennyson “had the finest ear of any English poet since Milton.””
Reason: “Eliot cites neither poem” followed twice by “has been cited for” uses one verb for Eliot’s notes and for the critics, and “cited for” is not how a person says “given as the source of.” The 1936 quotation is from a text after 1929 and should name its essay (“In Memoriam,” the introduction to a Nelson selection of Tennyson, reprinted in <i>Essays Ancient and Modern</i>).

### whitman-lilacs
Present: “and at line 357 wants the hermit-thrush’s “water-dripping song”; his note on that line describes the bird …” and, after the 1928 quotation, nothing before the Musgrove sentence.
Proposed: “and the hermit-thrush’s “water-dripping song” is at line 357; Eliot’s note on that line describes the bird …” and, after the 1928 quotation: “Two years earlier, reviewing a life of Whitman, he had written that “when Whitman speaks of the lilacs or of the mocking-bird, his theories and beliefs drop away like a needless pretext” (“Whitman and Tennyson”, <i>Nation and Athenaeum</i>, 18 December 1926).”
Reason: the poem does not want; the reader stopped to ask who was wanting. And the 1926 review bears directly on the 1928 disclaimer now quoted: four years after the poem Eliot was already writing about Whitman’s lilacs. A 1926 text may be quoted freely.

### webster-white-devil
Present: “rising from a feigned death after Vittoria has fired at him what she took to be a loaded pistol.”
Proposed: “rising from a feigned death after Vittoria and her maid Zanche have fired at him pistols they took to be loaded.”
Reason: two pistols and two shooters. Flamineo hands them over (“Two of these you shall level at my breast”); the stage direction is “They shoot, and run to him, and tread upon him”; feigning death he says “Kill’d with a couple of braches!” (Act V scene vi).

### shakespeare-tempest
Present: “Eliot’s notes cite the scene at lines 192 and 257, and his note on the pearls of line 125 refers them back to lines 37 and 48; his note on Tiresias says the Phoenician Sailor “is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples.””
Proposed: “Eliot’s notes cite the scene at lines 192 and 257, and his note on the pearls of line 125 refers them back to lines 37 and 48. His note on Tiresias says the Phoenician Sailor “is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples.””
Reason: three notes and six line numbers in one sentence. The facts the scholar found wrong in round one are all corrected in the new text.

### frazer
Present: “argued that the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris, and by implication of Christ, tell one story, that of a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by rites in which a king is sacrificed or an effigy is buried or drowned.” And the last sentence, from “Frazer put a reproduction” to “the underworld.”
Proposed: “argued that the myths of Adonis, Attis and Osiris tell one story, that of a god who dies with the harvest and returns with the spring, helped by rites in which a king is sacrificed or an effigy is buried or drowned, and that the Church’s Easter was adapted from the spring festivals of Adonis and Attis.” And: “Frazer put a reproduction of Turner’s painting <i>The Golden Bough</i> (1834) at the front of his first edition and opened his first page with it, describing it as a view of Lake Nemi. It in fact shows Lake Avernus, with the Sibyl of Cumae, the prophetess of the poem’s epigraph, holding the bough that admits Aeneas to the underworld.”
Reason: “by implication” is wrong; Frazer says it outright in the two volumes Eliot names. <i>Adonis Attis Osiris</i> (third edition, 1914), vol. I, by its own table of contents: “the Christian festival of Easter perhaps grafted on a festival of Adonis” (pp. 254–257) and “the festival of Easter apparently adapted to the spring festival of Attis” (pp. 305–310). The last sentence carried the frontispiece, the first page, the mistake, the real lake, the Sibyl, the epigraph and Aeneas in one breath.

### weston
Present: “The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel).”
Proposed: “The chapters the poem draws on most are II (The Task of the Hero), VI (The Symbols, where the Grail hallows become the four suits of the Tarot), IX (The Fisher King) and XIII (The Perilous Chapel).”
Reason: chapter VI is the chapter behind Eliot’s note on line 46. It is where Weston makes the four Grail hallows the suits of the Tarot: “They exist to-day as the four suits of the Tarot.”

### petronius
Present: “asked Apollo for as many years as the grains of sand in her hand and forgot to ask for youth to go with them” and “Eliot put Petronius in its stead before publication.”
Proposed: “asked Apollo for as many years as there were grains in a heap of dust she had scooped up, and forgot to ask for youth to go with them” and “Eliot put Petronius in its place before publication.”
Reason: Ovid’s words are “ego pulveris hausti / ostendens cumulum, quot haberet corpora pulvis” (<i>Metamorphoses</i> XIV, 136–137): dust, not sand, in a heap she had taken up and holds out. The Part I gloss on the epigraph says the same, and the two should agree; Ovid’s word is also the word of line 30. “In its stead” is not something a person says across a table.

### dante-purg26
Present: “Eliot took the title of his 1920 collection <i>Ara Vos Prec</i> from line 145, and line 428 of the poem from line 148, which his note quotes with the three lines before it.” And nothing after the sentence on the dedication about the phrase itself.
Proposed: “Eliot took the title of his 1920 collection <i>Ara Vos Prec</i> from line 145 (the Ovid Press title page misprinted it “Ara Vus Prec”, a form Eliot later blamed on his Italian edition of Dante), and line 428 of the poem from line 148, which his note quotes with the three lines before it.” And, after the sentence on the dedication: “The phrase was already Pound’s own: “Il Miglior Fabbro” is the title of his chapter on Arnaut Daniel in <i>The Spirit of Romance</i> (1910).”
Reason: a reader who meets “Ara Vus Prec” in a catalogue will want the misprint explained (the spine label is correct; Eliot to Donald Gallup, 1936). The dedication returns to Pound a phrase he had used himself, which the Part I gloss on the dedication now says; the library page should agree.

### dickens-omf
Present: “Betty Higden, a poor old woman in the novel, is praising Sloppy, the foundling she has brought up, who reads her the newspaper and does all the voices in the police-court reports.”
Proposed: “Betty Higden, a poor old woman who minds children for a living, is praising Sloppy, a foundling brought up in the workhouse who lives with her and turns her mangle, and who reads her the newspaper and does all the voices in the police-court reports.”
Reason: Sloppy was brought up in the workhouse, not by her: “parents never known; found in the street. He was brought up in the— … the House” (Book I, chapter 16). He lives with her now and works for her, at the mangle.

### conrad-hod
Present: the first sentence and the sentences on the letters, from “The last words of Kurtz” to “that stands there now.”
Proposed: “Kurtz’s last words stood on the title page of the typescript, as the poem’s epigraph. Kurtz is the trader whom the narrator, Marlow, has gone up the river to find. Pound, returning the poem in January 1922, wrote that he doubted whether Conrad was “weighty enough to stand the citation.” Eliot answered that it was “much the most appropriate I can find, and somewhat elucidative,” and Pound withdrew the objection (the exchange is printed in the 1971 facsimile and in the <i>Letters</i>, volume 1); Eliot nevertheless replaced it with the passage from Petronius that stands there now.”
Reason: the passage stood on the typescript’s title page, before Part I, as the epigraph of the whole poem, not “at the head of the typescript of Part I” (the facsimile, p. 3; the site’s own drafts record on the epigraph says the title page). The letters had no date or source. And the reader found the subject of the first sentence twelve words from its verb with two names between.

### eliot-frontiers
Present: “A lecture given at the University of Minnesota on 30 April 1956, to an audience of about fourteen thousand, and printed in <i>On Poetry and Poets</i> (1957).”
Proposed: “Eliot gave the lecture at the University of Minnesota on 30 April 1956, to an audience of about fourteen thousand; it was printed in <i>On Poetry and Poets</i> (1957).”
Reason: a fragment as the opening sentence. Every fact the scholar questioned in round one is corrected in the new text.

## 3. The voice legend (site/voices.json) and the drafts records about the whole poem (site/drafts.txt)

The reader was satisfied with all ten Part I voices as the front-matter thread now has them. The scholar's round two found three things still missing or wrong, and one in the lens's line assignment.

### voice poem
Present: the note ends “… and answers each DA (402–410, 412–417, 419–423).”
Proposed: add a last sentence: “That Marie begins at line 8 rather than at line 1 is this edition’s reading: the “us” of line 5 returns at line 8, and Cleanth Brooks (1939) read lines 1–18 as one voice.”
Reason: the legend now owns its other assignments (the voice in German, the walker) as readings; the break at 7/8 is one too, and the oldest reading runs the other way.

### voice stranger
Present: “Line 12, one sentence: “I am not Russian at all, I come from Lithuania, a real German.” …”
Proposed: “Line 12, one sentence, spoken by a woman (the German is feminine, “Russin”): “I am not Russian at all, I come from Lithuania, a real German.” …”
Reason: the one hard fact about the speaker, and it bears on whether the line can be Marie’s. The Part I gloss on line 12 says the same.

### voice walker
Present: “Mr. Eugenides (207–211)”, and in the spans, [207, 211, "walker"] and [212, 214, "eugenides"].
Proposed: “Mr. Eugenides (207–214)”, and one span, [207, 214, "walker"].
Reason: lines 209–214 are one sentence, with “Mr. Eugenides” as its subject (209), “Asked” as its verb (212) and the walker as its object (“me”); the split at 211/212 puts a subject and its verb in different mouths. Eugenides has no words in the poem: the invitation is reported (“Asked me in demotic French / To luncheon …”), and “in demotic French” is the walker’s description of speech the poem withholds. The lens’s own precedent runs the other way: at lines 15–16 the archduke’s quoted words stay inside Marie’s span. The most that could be defended as a reading is 213–214 heard as the walker echoing the invitation’s terms, and that is weak. These are Part III’s lines, so the Part III thread may want a say; if the eugenides span goes, the “eugenides” voice has no lines left and its record wants looking at.

### draft title-draft
Present: “The words are Betty Higden’s in Dickens’s <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (Book I, chapter 16), praising Sloppy, the foundling who lives with her and turns her mangle, for the way he reads the police news in the paper aloud, doing the voices: “You mightn’t think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices.” The title page Eliot typed for the finished poem reads “The Waste Land,” and his headnote to the notes says …”
Proposed: “The words are Betty Higden’s, in Dickens’s <i>Our Mutual Friend</i> (Book I, chapter 16). She is praising Sloppy, the foundling who lives with her and turns her mangle, for the way he reads the police news aloud from the paper, doing the voices: “You mightn’t think it, but Sloppy is a beautiful reader of a newspaper. He do the Police in different voices.” The title page of the typescript reads “The Waste Land,” and his headnote to the notes says …”
Reason: the title leaf carried the Conrad epigraph that Pound was objecting to on 24 January 1922, while the poem was still being cut, and that Eliot then replaced; it was not typed for the finished poem, and the epigraph record beside this one describes the same leaf as the typescript’s title page. The reader asked for a stop in a sentence of sixty words.

### art/tarot/NOTES.md, the Sibyl card, "answers to"
Present: "Apollo granted her as many years of life as there were grains in a handful of sand, and she had forgotten to ask for youth to go with them."
Proposed: "Apollo granted her as many years of life as there were grains in a heap of dust she had picked up, and she had forgotten to ask for youth to go with them."

### art/tarot/NOTES.md and the drawing crowd-on-london-bridge.svg (for the art thread)

The batch 5 reader noticed the disagreement between the two cards. A matter for the drawing; the note should follow whatever is drawn.

## 5. Line numbers: the README's sentence and the site's notes

Part I's scholar found that the site attaches Eliot's notes 357 and 360 to lines 357 and 360, and his 366 to line 367, and took the README's sentence ("Eliot's 1922 marginal numbers run one behind from line 346") to be wrong. The build thread answered at 19:18 UTC: the sentence is about the printed marginal numbers and stands. On page 42 of the 1922 book "If there were water" is set as the run-over of "From doors of mudcracked houses" and not counted, so the margin's 350 stands beside the poem's 351st line and 433 beside its 434th (checked on the Boni and Liveright page images). Eliot's notes are the inconsistent party: 357 and 360 fit the true count, 366 onward the printed one, which is why the site attaches 366 to line 367; the About page says as much. Nothing on the site changes, and no line reference in Part I's commentary is affected.

## 6. Where Lloyds Bank stood (for anyone with a 1920s street directory)

The Part I notes on lines 63 and 67 and the Eliot pathway stop at line 66 say that the bank's head office in Lombard Street stood "a few doors along from" St Mary Woolnoth. One scholar in this debate wrote that it faced the church across the street; another could vouch only for the block between Lombard Street and Cornhill (71–77 Lombard Street, 15–22 Cornhill, with Eliot's department usually addressed 17 Cornhill). The weaker claim was kept. If the site ever states which side of the street, it wants a source.
