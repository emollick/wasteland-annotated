id: notes-head-text
where: the Notes lens: the card at the head of the poem explaining the notes
text: Eliot’s notes were first printed in the book edition of December 1922; the poem had appeared in <i>The Criterion</i> and <i>The Dial</i> without them. In 1956 Eliot said the poem had been found “inconveniently short” for a book, so he had expanded the notes to fill it out, and he called the result “bogus scholarship.” Each appears here beside its lines, and all of them <a href="#notes">after the poem</a>. Gold dots mark the lines that have one.

id: legend-voices-text
where: the Voices lens: the card at the head of the poem, first paragraph
text: Eliot’s typescripts of Parts I and II were headed <i>He Do the Police in Different Voices</i>, a phrase from Dickens’s <i>Our Mutual Friend</i>. Each change of speaker is marked, and each voice has its own colour, so that you can see where one stops and another starts. Which changes count as a new voice is a matter of interpretation; the divisions marked here are this edition’s reading.

id: legend-tongues-text
where: the Tongues lens: the card at the head of the poem
text: The epigraph is Latin and Greek, and the dedication Italian. The poem itself has German, French, Italian, Latin and Sanskrit, and Eliot’s note on line 428 quotes the Provençal that Dante gives Arnaut Daniel to speak. The cries of birds and the refrains of songs have a colour of their own. Click a coloured phrase for its translation. Eliot’s notes translate only the thunder’s three words and Shantih; the other translations were made for this edition.

id: legend-places-text
where: the Places lens: the card at the head of the poem, first paragraph
text: Place names are marked. Click one for its lines and its spot on the map. The route of Part I, over London Bridge and up King William Street to St Mary Woolnoth, is the way City workers came in from London Bridge station. Eliot’s note to line 68 says the “dead sound” of the church clock at nine was “a phenomenon which I have often noticed”; Lloyds Bank, where he worked from 1917 to 1925, was a short walk from the church. The places along the river in Part III run from Richmond to Margate.

id: tarot-phoenician-sailor-note
where: Turn the cards: the note read when the card for phoenician sailor is turned
text: <p>Not a card of any Tarot pack. Eliot’s note to line 46 says that the Phoenician Sailor and the Merchant “appear later” and that “Death by Water is executed in Part IV”: the Sailor is Phlebas the Phoenician, the drowned man of Part IV (line 312). The note to line 218 adds that the one-eyed merchant “melts into the Phoenician Sailor, and the latter is not wholly distinct from Ferdinand Prince of Naples.” Line 48, “Those are pearls that were his eyes,” is from Ariel’s song in <i>The Tempest</i>, sung to Ferdinand about his father, who is thought drowned.</p>

id: tarot-one-eyed-merchant-note
where: Turn the cards: the note read when the card for one eyed merchant is turned
text: <p>Not a card of the pack. Eliot’s note to line 218 calls him “the one-eyed merchant, seller of currants,” which makes him Mr. Eugenides, the Smyrna merchant of line 209, with his pocket full of currants and his invitation to lunch. Why he is one-eyed is not explained; Cleanth Brooks (1939) took it to mean no more than that the face on the card is shown in profile. What he carries on his back is the blank card, “which I am forbidden to see” (line 54).</p>

id: tarot-blank-note
where: Turn the cards: the note read when the card for blank is turned
text: <p>“This card, / Which is blank, is something he carries on his back, / Which I am forbidden to see” (lines 52–54). Madame Sosostris cannot read it, and the poem never says what it is. In <i>From Ritual to Romance</i>, the book on the Grail legend that Eliot’s notes name as the source of the poem’s title and plan, Jessie Weston says that the mystery cults she traces the legend to were carried across the Roman Empire by Syrian merchants, among others; the merchant’s unseen burden has been read in that light, by the critic Cleanth Brooks in 1939 and by others since.</p>

id: tarot-hanged-man-note
where: Turn the cards: the note read when the card for hanged man is turned
text: <p>Card XII of the major arcana: a man hanging by one foot from a living tree, his face calm, a halo round his head. Eliot’s note says the card “fits my purpose in two ways: because he is associated in my mind with the Hanged God of Frazer, and because I associate him with the hooded figure in the passage of the disciples to Emmaus in Part V.” The Hanged God is a chapter of Frazer’s <i>The Golden Bough</i>, in the volumes <i>Adonis, Attis, Osiris</i> that Eliot’s headnote names, on gods, and men standing for gods, put to death by hanging on a sacred tree. Madame Sosostris does not find him: “I do not find / The Hanged Man” (lines 54–55). Cleanth Brooks (1939) explained her failure by the hood: “That he is hooded accounts for Madame Sosostris’ inability to see him.”</p>

id: tarot-crowd-in-a-ring-note
where: Turn the cards: the note read when the card for crowd in a ring is turned
text: <p>Not a card; it is what she sees next. Eliot’s note says the “crowds of people” appear later: the crowd that flows over London Bridge at line 62, six lines on, which his note to line 63 traces to the souls in Dante’s <i>Inferno</i> (canto III) who run for ever after a banner. The “hooded hordes” of Part V (line 369) have been read as the same crowd.</p>
