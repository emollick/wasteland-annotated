---
kind: map
title: Follow the river
lens: places
body: The section names, in order, a canal behind a gashouse, the Cannon Street Hotel, the Strand, Queen Victoria Street and Lower Thames Street with St Magnus Martyr, Greenwich Reach and the Isle of Dogs, and in the daughters' speeches Highbury, Richmond and Kew, Moorgate and Margate. The map has the real course of the Thames, with each of these on it.
---
kind: source
title: Spenser's refrain
source: spenser-prothalamion
body: Spenser's <i>Prothalamion</i> (1596) is a poem for a double wedding on the Thames, and its refrain, "Sweete Themmes runne softly, till I end my Song", closes each stanza. Read its first stanza, then lines 173–184. Eliot takes the refrain at 176 and 183 and varies it at 184; Spenser's nymphs gather flowers by the river, Eliot's "are departed", and his river "bears no" bottles, papers or cigarette ends.
sources: Spenser, Prothalamion, line 18
---
kind: lens
title: See who speaks
lens: voices
body: The Voices lens marks each speaker and each quoted voice: the "I" who fishes by the canal, the Mrs. Porter song, Verlaine's line, the nightingale, Mr. Eugenides (reported, not quoted), Tiresias, the typist, the "I" of Lower Thames Street, the three Thames-daughters, Augustine and the Buddha. Tiresias speaks only in this section; Eliot's note calls him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest".
---
kind: source
title: The Fire Sermon itself
source: buddha-fire-sermon
body: Eliot's note sends the reader to the Buddha's Fire Sermon in Henry Clarke Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (1896) and calls it as important as the Sermon on the Mount. It is short. In it the Buddha says of the eye, the ear, the tongue, the body and the mind that each "is on fire", with the fire of passion, of hatred and of infatuation. The section's title is the sermon's, and "burning", five times at 308–311, is the poem's word for the sermon's "on fire".
sources: Warren, Buddhism in Translations, § 73, pp. 351–353
---
kind: draft
title: Pound's pencil on the typist
lens: drafts
body: In the typescript the typist scene was longer, in regular quatrains, and Pound cut it heavily, writing in the margin that the verse was "not interesting enough as verse" for so much of it. The drafts page describes his marks, and the seventy or so lines of couplets in Pope's manner, about a fashionable woman called Fresca, that opened the section until he struck them out.
sources: Facsimile (1971), the typescript of Part III
Ricks and McCue, commentary on lines 222–230

kind: listen
title: The Rhine-daughters, then the Thames-daughters
lens: listen
body: Act III of Wagner's <i>Götterdämmerung</i> opens with the three Rhine-daughters swimming and singing of their stolen gold; "Weialala leia, wallala leialala" is their refrain, and the Thames-daughters take it at 277–278 and 290–291. Listen to the scene, then read lines 266–306. Eliot's note says his three daughters "speak in turn" from 292 to 306.
sources: Wagner, Götterdämmerung III.i
---
kind: place
title: Margate
lens: places
body: "On Margate Sands. / I can connect / Nothing with nothing." Eliot spent four weeks at Margate, from 15 October to 12 November 1921, on three months' sick leave from Lloyds Bank. In a letter from there, to Sydney Schiff, he said he had drafted "some fifty lines" of this part, sitting in a shelter on the front. The Nayland Rock shelter on the seafront, listed in 2009 as the likely place, still stands. The map has it.
sources: Letters, vol. 1, Eliot to Sydney Schiff, 4 November 1921
Letters, vol. 1, chronology, October–November 1921
Historic England, list entry 1393490

body: The Water lens marks fire in a third colour. In this section it falls on the last four lines only, where "burning", from the Buddha's sermon of the title, follows Augustine's "To Carthage then I came". Eliot's note says the placing of these two together "is not an accident".

---
kind: source
title: Tiresias in Ovid
source: ovid-met3
body: Eliot's note at 218 quotes nineteen lines of Ovid (<i>Metamorphoses</i> III, 320–338). Tiresias, who had lived seven years as a woman, is asked to settle Jupiter and Juno's dispute over which sex has the greater pleasure in love. Juno blinds him for his answer and Jupiter gives him foresight. The library gives lines 324–331, on the serpents he struck and the change of sex that followed, in Latin and English. Read them, then the typist scene, which Tiresias says he has "foresuffered".
sources: Ovid, Metamorphoses III 320–338
---
kind: ask
title: Whose side is the poem on
body: The typist "smoothes her hair with automatic hand, / And puts a record on the gramophone" (255–256). Eliot's note gives Goldsmith's song, in which the woman who "stoops to folly" has nothing left but to die; Eliot's typist, instead, puts a record on. Critics have read the scene as contempt for her, and as the nearest the poem comes to pity; those who read it as pity point to Tiresias's "foresuffered all" (243).
sources: Goldsmith, The Vicar of Wakefield, ch. 24
