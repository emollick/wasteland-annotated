# Proposals from the Part IV debate for the thread "Debate: front matter and notes"

These are things a reader opens from lines 312–321 that live outside `part4/`, so the Part IV thread did not edit them. Each was put through the same scholar-and-reader debate; the record is in `debates/part4.md`. The wording below is what both sides accepted.

## 1. site/sources.txt · record `dans-le-restaurant` · the `note` field

The `what` line stands ("The French original of "Death by Water.""). The `trans` field should change in two places, the same two as the gloss card's translation in `part4/glosses.txt` (which the build may take from the same record): "carried him very far away" for "l'emporta très loin", and "Just imagine, it was a hard fate" for "Figurez-vous donc, c'était un sort pénible" (the old "Consider then" borrowed the English poem's word and hid the change the card exists to show).

Old note:
> One of the four poems Eliot wrote in French in 1917–18, in which a waiter tells a customer about a childhood fumble under a tree in the rain, and the customer, disgusted, tells him to go and wash; then, out of nowhere, the drowned Phoenician. Eliot translated the last stanza into English for Part IV, dropping the tin and Cornwall and adding "Gentile or Jew." He is the only poet the poem quotes in two languages.

What was wrong: "written in 1917–18" (all four were written by about April 1917 on Schuchard's account; September 1918 is the Little Review printing); "the only poet the poem quotes in two languages" is false (Dante is quoted in Italian at 427 and in English at 63–64, with Eliot's notes citing both), and Part IV does not quote the French, it translates it; the one sentence with three clauses and a fragment; "out of nowhere".

Proposed note:
> Eliot published four poems in French; this is one of them, written in 1917, printed in the <i>Little Review</i> in September 1918 and then in his two collections of 1920, <i>Ara Vos Prec</i> and <i>Poems</i>. A shabby waiter tells a customer about sheltering from a shower with a small girl when he was seven; the customer, disgusted, gives him ten sous to go and wash. Then, without transition, come seven lines on the drowned Phoenician Phlebas. Eliot turned those seven lines into the ten of Part IV, dropping the tin and Cornwall and adding "Gentile or Jew" and the address to the man at the wheel. (His "Cornouaille", without an s, is strictly the district of Brittany; the cargo of tin points to Cornwall, and that is how the line is read.)

Proposed trans (the same as the gloss card now carries):
> Phlebas the Phoenician, drowned a fortnight, forgot the cries of the gulls and the swell off Cornwall, and the profits and the losses, and the cargo of tin: an undersea current carried him very far away, taking him back through the stages of his former life. Just imagine, it was a hard fate; and yet he was once a handsome man, and tall.

## 2. site/ui.txt · the tarot tool's note on the card "The Drowned Phoenician Sailor"

The "what it shows" text stands. The "answers to" text says "He is drawn inverted on purpose: he is the Hanged Man the reading cannot find, gone into the water instead." That is the site's own reading stated as a fact, it assumes the reader remembers lines 54–55, and it ends on a flourish. The scholar also wanted the one fact a reader most needs on this card: no real Tarot pack has a drowned sailor (Eliot's note on 46: he departed from the pack "to suit my own convenience").

Proposed "answers to" text:
> 47–48, and Part IV (312–321). "Those are pearls that were his eyes" is Ariel's song from <i>The Tempest</i> (I.ii). No real Tarot pack has a drowned sailor; Eliot's note on line 46 says he departed from the pack "to suit my own convenience". He is drawn head down on purpose, in the posture of the Hanged Man, the card Madame Sosostris cannot find (54–55).

The note on the card "Phlebas the Phoenician" stands as it is (it describes the drawing and claims no source).

## 3. site/paths.txt · the pathway intros

Not Part IV's to change, but the reader met them before any Part IV stop and flagged them as carrying the voice Ethan objected to, in the same shapes as his examples:

- Water: "Follow the water and you follow the argument: water is what the waste land needs, water is what drowns you, and the two are never told apart." (the "X is a Y:" sentence with the list of three)
- Birds: "Every bird in the poem is a musician, and every song is a complaint." (the same shape)
- A first reading: "Each stop is a place to stand, not an explanation." and The poem in Eliot's life: "a map of where he was standing" (the "where the poem is standing" tic)
- The poem in Eliot's life promises "the poet's own year, 1921 into 1922"; its Part IV stop is a boyhood place (Gloucester), and the stop now says so. The intro may want to widen to "places from the poet's life".
