# Proposals from the Part II debate for text outside part2/

Written by the Part II debate thread ("Part II, A Game of Chess") for the thread "Debate: front matter and notes". These are texts a reader of lines 77–172 opens onto that live in site/, so the Part II thread did not edit them; each is the outcome of the same three-round debate (scholar, reader, editor) recorded in debates/part2.md, where every item below has a full entry under its key. For each: the record, the field, the text as it stands, the proposed text, and the reason. Fixed fields are untouched. Two further points for the build thread are at the end.


## S:shakespeare-antony  (the library record `shakespeare-antony` in site/sources.txt)

Field: note

As it stands:

    Enobarbus's set piece describing Cleopatra's first appearance to Antony on the river Cydnus, the most famous description of a woman in English drama. Eliot's parody keeps the meter, the gold, the perfume and the sense of a spectacle staged for a man, and takes away the river and the man. Elizabeth and Leicester's real barge on the Thames, at line 279, is the same picture with the Tudors in it.

Proposed:

    Enobarbus, Antony's officer, describes Cleopatra's first appearance to Antony on the river Cydnus. Eliot's lines keep the metre, the gold and the perfume, and move the scene indoors. The poem's real barge is Elizabeth and Leicester's on the Thames, at line 279, where Eliot's note sends the reader to Froude's history of Elizabeth's reign.

Why: 'the most famous description of a woman in English drama' is a superlative no one can check; 'takes away the river and the man' and 'the same picture with the Tudors in it' were the annotator's flourishes; the Froude note is what Eliot's own note on 279 gives (scholar, verified).


## S:virgil-aeneid1  (the library record `virgil-aeneid1` in site/sources.txt)

Field: note

As it stands:

    Lines 726–727, quoted in Eliot's note: the banquet at which Dido, queen of Carthage, falls in love with Aeneas, who will sail away and leave her to the pyre. The word comes into the poem as a piece of interior decoration, and brings the whole story with it. Carthage again at line 307.

Proposed:

    Lines 726–727, quoted in Eliot's note: the banquet at which Dido, queen of Carthage, falls in love with Aeneas, who will sail away and leave her to kill herself. In the poem the word names a ceiling; Dido's story comes in with it. Carthage returns at line 307, in Augustine's words.

Why: 'a piece of interior decoration, and brings the whole story with it' was a wink; the cross-reference to 307 now says why (Eliot's note on 307 quotes Augustine).


## S:ovid-met6  (the library record `ovid-met6` in site/sources.txt)

Field: note

As it stands:

    Tereus of Thrace, escorting his wife's sister Philomela to visit Procne, rapes her in a hut in the woods and cuts out her tongue; she weaves the crime into a cloth and sends it to her sister; Procne kills her son Itys and serves him to his father; Tereus, pursuing the sisters with a sword, and the sisters, fleeing, all become birds. Ovid does not say which sister became the nightingale; later tradition made Philomela the nightingale and Procne the swallow, and Eliot follows it. The story sits above the mantel in Part II, breaks into birdsong in Part III, and ends the poem as a swallow. Arthur Golding's 1567 translation, the one Shakespeare read, is the English to go to.

Proposed:

    Tereus of Thrace, escorting his wife's sister Philomela to visit Procne, rapes her in a hut in the woods and cuts out her tongue; she weaves the crime into a cloth and sends it to her sister; the sisters kill Procne's son Itys and serve him to his father; Tereus, pursuing them with a sword, and the sisters, fleeing, all become birds. Ovid names only Tereus's bird, the hoopoe; later tradition made Philomela the nightingale and Procne the swallow, and Eliot follows it. The story is on the wall at line 99, returns as birdsong at lines 203–206, and as the swallow at 429. Arthur Golding's translation of 1567 is the English Shakespeare knew.

Why: In Ovid both sisters kill Itys (VI.636–646), and Ovid names the hoopoe himself (VI.674): the old note had Procne alone and left the hoopoe to 'later tradition'. The triplet ('sits ... breaks ... ends') replaced by line numbers; 'the English to go to' was colloquial.


## S:lyly-campaspe  (the library record `lyly-campaspe` in site/sources.txt)

Field: note

As it stands:

    The Elizabethan convention that the nightingale sings "jug jug" and calls "tereu," the vocative of Tereus, is at its plainest in this song from Lyly's court comedy; the songs in Lyly's plays were first printed in the collected edition of 1632, and whether he wrote them is disputed. Eliot's "Jug Jug to dirty ears" and the four-line burst of birdsong at lines 203–206 are Lyly's bird, with the pretty convention turned back into what it describes.

Proposed:

    The Elizabethan convention that the nightingale sings "jug jug" and calls "tereu", the name Tereus, is at its plainest in this song from Lyly's court comedy. The songs in Lyly's plays were first printed in the collected edition of 1632, and whether he wrote them is disputed. Eliot uses the convention at line 103 and again at lines 203–206, where the rape is named ("So rudely forc'd").

Why: 'the vocative of Tereus' is grammar the reader does not need; 'Lyly's bird, with the pretty convention turned back into what it describes' had to be read twice.


## S:shakespeare-tempest  (the library record `shakespeare-tempest` in site/sources.txt)

Field: note

As it stands:

    Ferdinand, washed ashore after the shipwreck, believes his father Alonso drowned; Ariel's song tells him so, beautifully and falsely. Alonso is alive, and by the end of the play everyone has suffered a sea-change for the better. Eliot quotes the song twice and Ferdinand's speech twice, and never allows the change. The drowned father with pearls for eyes becomes the drowned Phoenician and Phlebas; Ferdinand weeping on the bank becomes the Fisher King by the gasworks.

Proposed:

    Ferdinand, washed ashore after the shipwreck, believes his father Alonso drowned; Ariel's song tells him so, beautifully and falsely, for Alonso is alive. Eliot quotes the song at lines 48 and 125 and Ferdinand's speech at 257, and adapts it at 191–192. In the play the drowned man turns out to be alive; in the poem the drowned stay drowned. The drowned father with pearls for eyes becomes the drowned Phoenician Sailor of the tarot pack (line 47) and Phlebas (Part IV); Ferdinand weeping on the bank becomes the man fishing in the canal at line 189, whom readers, following Eliot's headnote on Jessie Weston's book, take for the Fisher King of the Grail legend, the wounded king whose land lies waste.

Why: The old note said 'quotes the song twice and Ferdinand's speech twice' (257 is quoted, 191–192 adapted), dropped 'the Fisher King' and 'the gasworks' without saying what they were, and made 'the drowned Phoenician and Phlebas' read as two people. The Fisher King identification is marked as the readers' inference it is.


## S:webster-devils-law-case  (the library record `webster-devils-law-case` in site/sources.txt)

Field: note

As it stands:

    Act III scene ii. Two surgeons have just been bribed by Romelio, who has stabbed the wounded Contarino in his bed to finish him off; when the body groans, one of them asks whether "the wind" is "in that door still," meaning whether that is still how things stand. It is: the stab has lanced the old wound, and Contarino lives. Eliot's note gives only the line and the name.

Proposed:

    Act III scene ii. Romelio, disguised as a physician, bribes the two surgeons attending the wounded Contarino to leave him alone with the patient, and stabs him; the surgeons, who have kept near, come back. When Contarino groans, the Second Surgeon asks "Is the wind in that door still?", an idiom for "so that is how things stand?": the man is alive. The stab has drained the old wound, and Contarino recovers. Eliot's note gives only the line and the name.

Why: The old note had the surgeons bribed after the stabbing; in the play the bribe comes first (scholar, checked against the 1623 quarto). Now tells the scene the same way as the gloss at line 118.


## S:shakespearian-rag  (the library record `shakespearian-rag` in site/sources.txt)

Field: note

As it stands:

    Published in 1912 and advertised as a number from that year's Ziegfeld Follies, though it seems never to have been sung in the show and was first performed in vaudeville in January 1913; Shakespeare's characters are made to dance the ragtime steps of the day. Eliot knew the popular songs of his Harvard years well enough to misquote one on purpose: his extra syllable, "Shakespeherian," gives the word the rag's syncopation. No recording from the period is known.

Proposed:

    The song was published in 1912. B. R. McElderry, who identified it as Eliot's source in 1957, took it to have been in that year's Ziegfeld Follies, as the publisher's advertisements implied; Matthew Guerrieri, going through the record in 2017, found no trace of it in the show and a first traced performance in vaudeville in January 1913. Shakespeare's characters are made to dance the ragtime steps of the day. Eliot's extra syllable, "Shakespeherian", is deliberate: it gives the word the rag's syncopation. The cover spells the title "Shakesperian", the chorus inside "Shakespearian". No recording from the period has been found.

Why: The three notes on the rag (gloss, library, listening room) told the Follies story three different ways; this is the one account, with the two scholars named here and nowhere else. 'well enough to misquote one on purpose' was a wink. Also: the fixed author field says 'David Stamper' and the listening-room record 'Dave Stamper'; the cover says Dave. One form, please.


## S:middleton-wbw  (the library record `middleton-wbw` in site/sources.txt)

Field: what

As it stands:

    The game of chess played downstairs during a rape upstairs.
PASSAGE (fixed unless inaccurate):

Proposed:

    The game of chess played downstairs while a woman is forced upstairs.

Field: note

As it stands:

    Livia, a procuress, keeps Bianca's mother-in-law busy at chess while the Duke of Florence takes Bianca in the gallery above; the lines about the game double as commentary on the seduction. Eliot's section title comes from this scene and from Middleton's <i>A Game at Chess</i> (1624), an anti-Spanish satire so popular that it ran nine days and was closed by the Privy Council. Eliot's 1927 essay on Middleton praised him for "the perpetual instinct of the man of genius."

Proposed:

    Livia, a go-between, keeps Bianca's mother-in-law busy at chess while the Duke of Florence forces himself on Bianca in the gallery above; the talk over the board doubles as commentary on what is happening upstairs. Eliot's note names this play; commentators add Middleton's <i>A Game at Chess</i> (1624), a satire on Spain that ran nine days, then unheard of, before the Privy Council closed it.

Why: 'takes' and 'seduction' in the note against 'rape' in the summary line: one word now, Eliot's own ('forced', line 100). The quotation from Eliot's 1927 essay ('the perpetual instinct of the man of genius') could not be found in the essay and is dropped; the essay's attested phrase is 'he is merely a great recorder', not needed here. Eliot's note names only Women Beware Women; the second play is the commentators'.
