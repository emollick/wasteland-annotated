## phlebas
Cited: The Little Review, September 1918, pp. 12–13 (the first printing; the scholar opened the Modernist Journals Project copy, whose contents list gives the pages); King James Bible, Romans 2:9–10 (scholar opened Gutenberg 10); Frazer ch. XXXII, "The Ritual of Adonis" (Gutenberg 3623 opened: the image of Adonis carried by the women of Alexandria to the sea-shore and committed to the waves); Weston ch. IV, "Tammuz and Adonis" (Gutenberg 4090 opened); Schuchard (scholar opened: the four French poems completed by about April 1917, the manuscripts undated); Martz (1993) (scholar opened: "Gentile or Jew" taken as everyone); Southam, Guide, note on Part IV (search inside, 1996 edition: the Alexandrian rite, "images of Adonis and Aphrodite on couches", in the note on Death by Water, which supports "many critics have set Phlebas beside it").
Uncited: "The wheel is a ship's wheel, and because Madame Sosostris also turns up 'the Wheel' (51), readers hear the Wheel of Fortune in it too." Searched the scanned commentaries for "Wheel of Fortune" with Phlebas: no hit. Southam's note on line 320 or Ricks and McCue's commentary on 320 would settle whether the standard commentary records it.
Conflict: "written in 1917". Schuchard, the debate's authority, dates the four French poems to about April 1917. Southam's Guide, 1974 and 1990 editions (search inside), says "Dans le Restaurant" was "written May–June 1918"; the 1968 edition says "written 1916-17". Ricks and McCue's headnote to "Dans le Restaurant" would settle it. Reported, not changed.

## death-by-water-draft
Cited: Facsimile (1971), the typescript of Part IV, and p. 129 (the page of the editorial notes that the transcription the scholar opened cites for Pound's reply; a search-inside snippet of the facsimile has the reply, "'I DO advise keeping Phlebas' replied Pound. 'In fact I more'n advise...'"); Pound, Letters, ed. Paige, nos. 182–183 (Eliot's "Perhaps better omit Phlebas also???" and Pound's reply; scholar opened two archive.org copies); Paris Review interview (1959) (the Ulysses canto; the words confirmed by search inside the Cox and Hinchliffe casebook, which reprints the interview: "rather inspired by the Ulysses Canto in The Inferno, I think"); Ellmann (1971) and Perloff (1981) (scholar opened: Pound's "Bad--but cant attack until I get typescript", the voyage's course, the wall of ice); T. S. Eliot Foundation, "T. S. Eliot's Gloucester" (scholar opened: the Dry Salvages, Cape Ann, the fishing schooner).
All cited. Two notes. (1) The facsimile's editorial note (search-inside snippet) counts the voyage as "Lines 1-84" and Phlebas as "Lines 84-93", so the typescript ran to 93 lines by the editor's count and 92 by Perloff's; "some ninety lines" is safe. (2) The works check found no mention of the drafts in Perloff's The Poetics of Indeterminacy (1981) and suggests the excerpt the scholar read on the Modern American Poetry site may come from another Perloff text (21st-Century Modernism, 2002). The citation keeps the debate's attribution; whoever has Perloff's books should settle it.
