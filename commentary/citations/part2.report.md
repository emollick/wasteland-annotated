# Part II, A Game of Chess: sources report

20 records: 10 glosses, 9 ways in, 1 drafts record. 17 received sources (71 citations); 3 ways in need none. 19 claims left uncited. Primary texts were opened (MIT Shakespeare; Internet Shakespeare Editions old-spelling Folio and quarto texts; Latin Library Virgil and Ovid; Gutenberg Milton and Waste Land; Dyce's Webster, Bullen's Middleton and Bond's Lyly on archive.org; the Boni and Liveright 1922 and the Dial scans; Paige's Pound on archive.org; Hansard; the York University sheet-music record; the Ballad Index). Copyright books were confirmed by search inside the archive.org scans (ol.py); no "leaf" number has been turned into a page.

## burnished-throne
Cited: Antony and Cleopatra II.ii (opened, MIT; "The barge she sat in, like a burnish'd throne"); Aeneid I 726 (opened, Latin Library: "dependent lychni laquearibus aureis"); Paradise Lost IV 140 (scholar checked, a); Williams (1968) for sea-wood as driftwood coloured by copper ("green as well as orange") and for the reading of the room's allusions as ruined loves (snippet: "Dido and Cleopatra and their lovers had all suffered the same ...").
Uncited:
- "The passage is generally read as a parody of Enobarbus's speech": the scholar called it the standard reading (Southam; Ricks and McCue) but marked it from memory. Searches inside Southam's editions and Ricks and McCue for "parody" with "Enobarbus" or "burnished" found nothing (the only hit was a 1984 dissertation). Ricks and McCue's commentary on line 77 or Southam's note on line 77 would settle it.
- "standards" as posts and "laquearia" as a coffered ceiling of sunken panels: dictionary senses (scholar: b, OED). No source given; the OED entries "standard" and "lacunar" would settle it.
- "Commentators commonly read the room as furnished with ruined loves": Williams (1968) is one such commentator (cited); Southam's Guide, which the scholar had in mind (b), was not confirmed by search.

## philomel
Cited: Ovid VI 424–674 (opened; "nomen epops volucri" at 674, the sisters' birds unnamed); Titus Andronicus IV.i (opened, MIT: Lavinia's "stumps", Marcus's "Philomel", the book of Ovid); Unger (1981), confirmed by snippet ("the 'stumps' of the ravished and mutilated Lavinia and her use of Ovid's story of Tereus"); Southam's note on line 103, confirmed by snippet in the 1968, 1974 and 1990 editions ("a crude joking reference to sexual intercourse").
Uncited:
- "The 'withered stumps of time' ... are usually taken as other old stories in other pictures": Unger (1981) quotes an earlier critic to that effect ("suggests the repeated allusions") and Wilks (1971) reads them as the "antique splendours" on the walls, so the reading is recorded, but no standard commentary was confirmed. Ricks and McCue's commentary on lines 104–105 or Southam's note on line 104 would settle it.
Error: none. works.txt now carries Unger (1981), Eliot's Compound Ghost, which is the book the note means (the earlier unger-1966 record, Moments and Patterns, was replaced at 21:08); Unger (1966) is cited on nerves and way:2:3 for a different point and is added to new-works.

## nerves

- "to go out with her hair not put up, which a grown woman of her class then did not do": social history, no source found; a history of dress or the OED under "hair" would settle it.
- "'Walk the street' also carried ... the sense of working as a prostitute": the OED ("street-walker"; "walk the streets") would settle it; searches inside Ricks and McCue for "walk the street" found nothing.

## in-the-cage
Cited: Facsimile (1971), pp. 10–11 (the pages given by Letters, vol. 1's note "TWL: Facs, 10-11" for "Too tum-pum" and by Gordon's Eliot's New Life, "10-11, 16-17", for "In the Cage", both in snippet); Ricks and McCue's textual notes ("[II] 77-79 ... '3 lines too tum-pum at a stretch'", "78 glass] swinging glass ts2", "120-22 ... Carrying Away the little light dead people", all in snippet); Pound, Letters, ed. Paige, nos. 181–183 (opened: "I doubt if Conrad is weighty enough to stand the citation"; Eliot's "Do you mean not use the Conrad quote or simply not put Conrad's name to it?"; "Ditto re Conrad"); Litz (1973) ("In the Cage ... an obvious reference to James's novella"); Miller (1977) (the title "taken from a Henry James novella"; "met first"); Gish (1988) (the Sibyl "hanging in a cage"); Thormählen (1978) (the retitling; the epigraph joined after the poem was complete; the "swinging" deletion); Montgomery (1979) ("Don't see what you had in mind here"); the Cambridge Companion (2015), ch. 7, Brooker (Pound's mark on the first three lines; "closed carriage" queried; Vivien's words); Cox and Hinchliffe (1968) (the 1960 fair copy).
Uncited:
- "which is why that line is a foot short": arithmetic on the published line (eight syllables), not a claim needing a source; the scholar's Shaw, Blank Verse (2007) was seen in snippet only for Pound's "Too tum-pum", not for the foot.
- "Pound also queried 'closed carriage' as dated": the facsimile shows Pound's mark ("1880") against the phrase, and the scholar's sources were the TLS and Brooker; cited to the Companion (Brooker), but the TLS piece was not identified.
Errors and cautions:
- Paige prints letter 181 under the date "24 Saturnus, An 1 (24 December)", that is 1921; the accepted date is 24 January 1922 (Letters, vol. 1; Valerie Eliot's introduction to the facsimile; from memory, not opened). The note's "came in only in 1922" agrees with the accepted date, but a reader following the Paige citation will find December 1921.
- The record has ten citations, more than the brief's "about six", because it makes a dozen distinct documentary claims; drop Gish, Montgomery or Cox and Hinchliffe first if it must be cut.
