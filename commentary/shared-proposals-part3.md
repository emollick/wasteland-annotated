Written by the thread "Debate: The Fire Sermon" for the thread "Debate: front matter and notes", which owns the site/ files and the recurring headings. Nothing here has been edited; the Part III files themselves (part3/glosses.txt, ways.txt, paths.txt, drafts.txt) are done and in place. ## 2. Library records for Part III's sources (site/sources.txt)

Sixteen records, each read by a scholar and a reader in three rounds (batch D in debates/part3.md). Only the fields shown are proposed; ids, lines, titles and authors stay. Where a passage or a date is proposed, the reason is accuracy to the source.


### dante-purg5 (Purgatorio, canto V)

Present:

what: La Pia's "Siena mi fé, disfecemi Maremma" behind "Highbury bore me. Richmond and Kew / Undid me."
date: c. 1315
note:
Among the late-repentant who died by violence, a woman speaks seven lines and gives her name, her birthplace, the place of her death and, by implication, her murderer, her husband. Early commentators named her Pia de' Tolomei of Siena and her husband Nello dei Pannocchieschi; nothing certain is known of her. Eliot's first Thames-daughter borrows the grammar and keeps the anonymity.

Proposed:

note:
In Ante-Purgatory, among those who died by violence and repented at the last, a woman speaks seven lines. She names herself, says that Siena made her and the Maremma unmade her, and says that the husband who put his ring on her knows how she died; she does not name him, and the words are taken to mean that he had her killed. The Maremma is the marsh country on the Tuscan coast. Early commentators identified her as Pia de' Tolomei of Siena and the husband as Nello dei Pannocchieschi; nothing certain is known of her. Eliot's note at 293 cites the passage; his first Thames-daughter says where she was born and where she was undone in the shape of Pia's sentence, and gives no name.

How the proposal was reached: the reader's two rewordings.

### gotterdammerung (Götterdämmerung, Act III scene 1)

Present:

what: "Weialala leia / Wallala leialala": the Rhine-daughters' refrain.
date: 1876 (first performed Bayreuth)
note:
The last opera of the <i>Ring</i> opens its third act with the three Rhine-daughters swimming and singing for the gold that Alberich stole in the first scene of the first opera, and asking Siegfried to give back the ring. Their refrain is the "la la" that Eliot's Thames-daughters sing over oil and tar. Eliot's note is precise about the transfer: "The Song of the (three) Thames-daughters begins here. . . . V. <i>Götterdämmerung</i>, III, i: the Rhine-daughters."

Proposed:

trans (two changes): "whole and noble" for "heil und hehr"; "noble star of the deep" for "hehrer Stern der Tiefe".
note:
The last of the four <i>Ring</i> operas opens its third act with the three Rhine-daughters swimming and singing to the sun of the gold Alberich stole in the first scene of <i>Das Rheingold</i>; Siegfried comes by, and they ask him for the ring. Their refrain is sung syllables without meaning, which Wagner's printed poem leaves out and the scores supply at length. Eliot's Thames-daughters sing two lines of it at 277–278 and 290–291, and Eliot's note at 266 sends the reader to the scene.

How the proposal was reached: the reader's "of the gold" and the plain statement of the refrain's form. Round 3: the scholar corrected "the two-line form the librettos give it" (no printing gives Eliot's two lines as a unit; the printed poem has no vocalise, the scores spread a longer string among the three voices).

### shakespeare-tempest (The Tempest, Act I scene ii)

Present:

what: Ariel's song, "Those are pearls that were his eyes"; Ferdinand on the bank; "This music crept by me upon the waters."
date: c. 1611
note:
Ferdinand, washed ashore after the shipwreck, believes his father Alonso drowned; Ariel's song tells him so, beautifully and falsely. Alonso is alive, and by the end of the play everyone has suffered a sea-change for the better. Eliot quotes the song twice and Ferdinand's speech twice, and never allows the change. The drowned father with pearls for eyes becomes the drowned Phoenician and Phlebas; Ferdinand weeping on the bank becomes the Fisher King by the gasworks.

Proposed:

what: Ariel's song, "Those are pearls that were his eyes"; Ferdinand's "the king my father's wreck" and "This music crept by me upon the waters."
note:
Ferdinand, cast ashore, believes his father Alonso drowned in the wreck; Ariel's song tells him so, though Alonso is alive. The poem takes the song's line about the pearls twice (48, 125) and Ferdinand's "This music crept by me upon the waters" once (257), and at 191–192 turns his "Weeping again the king my father's wreck" into a musing on a brother's wreck and a father's death. Eliot's notes at 192 and 257 point to the scene. The song's next lines, the "sea-change / Into something rich and strange", are not quoted.

### ovid-met6 (Metamorphoses, Book VI: Tereus, Procne and Philomela)

Present:

what: "The change of Philomel."
date: AD 8
note:
Tereus of Thrace, escorting his wife's sister Philomela to visit Procne, rapes her in a hut in the woods and cuts out her tongue; she weaves the crime into a cloth and sends it to her sister; Procne kills her son Itys and serves him to his father; Tereus, pursuing the sisters with a sword, and the sisters, fleeing, all become birds. Ovid does not say which sister became the nightingale; later tradition made Philomela the nightingale and Procne the swallow, and Eliot follows it. The story sits above the mantel in Part II, breaks into birdsong in Part III, and ends the poem as a swallow. Arthur Golding's 1567 translation, the one Shakespeare read, is the English to go to.

Proposed:

passage: corpora Cecropidum pennis pendere putares:<br>pendebant pennis. quarum petit altera silvas,<br>altera tecta subit, neque adhuc de pectore caedis<br>excessere notae, signataque sanguine pluma est.<br>ille dolore suo poenaeque cupidine velox<br>vertitur in volucrem, cui stant in vertice cristae.<br>prominet inmodicum pro longa cuspide rostrum;<br>nomen epops volucri, facies armata videtur.
trans: As they fly from him you would think that the bodies of the two Athenians were poised on wings: they were poised on wings! One flies to the woods, the other rises to the roof. And even now their breasts have not lost the marks of their murderous deed, their feathers are stained with blood. Tereus, swift in pursuit because of his grief and eager desire for vengeance, is himself changed into a bird. Upon his head a stiff crest appears, and a huge beak stands forth instead of his long sword. He is the hoopoe, with the look of one armed for war. (Frank Justus Miller, 1916; <i>Metamorphoses</i> VI, 667–674)
note:
Tereus, king of Thrace, brings his wife Procne's sister Philomela from Athens to visit her, rapes her in a hut deep in the woods, and cuts out her tongue. She weaves the story into a cloth and sends it to her sister. Procne kills her son Itys and serves him to his father. Tereus pursues the sisters with a sword, and all three are turned into birds (VI.424–674). Ovid does not say which sister became the nightingale; the Latin poets and the later tradition made her Philomela, and Eliot follows them. The story is displayed above the mantel in Part II (99–103), and the bird's cry, "jug jug" and "Tereu" in the Elizabethan spelling, breaks in at 203–206 round "So rudely forc'd" from line 100. Eliot's note to the swallow at 429 refers back to "Philomela in Parts II and III".

How the proposal was reached: the scholar's "displayed above the mantel" (the poem's word at 97); the scholar's passage and Miller's translation for the empty passage field; the reader's sentence breaks.

### ovid-met3 (Metamorphoses, Book III: Tiresias)

Present:

what: The man who had been a woman, and who sees the future.
date: AD 8
note:
Lines 324–331 of the passage Eliot's note quotes at length, calling it "of great anthropological interest." Jupiter and Juno, disputing which sex takes more pleasure in love, consult the one person who has been both; his answer costs him his sight and earns him prophecy. Eliot's note makes him the poem's uniting consciousness. Ovid makes him a man who lost an argument with a goddess.

Proposed:

what: Tiresias, who lived seven years as a woman, and how he came to see the future.
note:
The passage is <i>Metamorphoses</i> III.324–331, the middle of the longer stretch (III.320–338) that Eliot's note quotes and calls "of great anthropological interest". Jupiter and Juno, disputing which sex has the greater pleasure in love, put the question to Tiresias, who had been both: he had struck two coupling snakes with his staff and lived seven years as a woman, then struck them again and become a man. He sided with Jupiter. Juno blinded him for it, and Jupiter, who could not undo what another god had done, gave him knowledge of the future instead. Eliot's note calls him "the most important personage in the poem, uniting all the rest".

How the proposal was reached: the reader's text.

### spenser-prothalamion (Prothalamion)

Present:

what: "Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song."
date: 1596
note:
A "spousal verse" for the double wedding of the Earl of Worcester's daughters, in which the poet, out of favour at court, walks by the Thames and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans who are the brides. Eliot keeps the refrain and empties the river. Notice that Spenser's poet is also unhappy, also walking off his "sullein care" by the river, before the wedding cheers him up.

Proposed:

note:
A "Spousall Verse" (Spenser's subtitle) for the double wedding in 1596 of the Earl of Worcester's daughters, Elizabeth and Katherine Somerset. The poet, disappointed of preferment at court, walks by the Thames to ease his "sullein care" and sees nymphs gathering flowers for two swans coming down the river, who stand for the brides. The refrain closes each of the ten stanzas. Eliot's note at 176 cites the poem; the refrain returns at 183–184, and the nymphs who "are departed" (175, 179) are usually taken as Spenser's.

How the proposal was reached: the reader's full stop.

### marvell-coy-mistress (To His Coy Mistress)

Present:

what: "But at my back . . . I hear."
date: written c. 1650; published 1681
note:
The great English argument for going to bed now, because later there is only the grave. Eliot quotes the turn of the argument twice and gives it a rattle of bones the first time and motor horns the second. His 1921 essay on Marvell, written months before the poem, praised "a tough reasonableness beneath the slight lyric grace."

Proposed:

note:
Marvell's poem argues that time is short and the lady should yield now. Eliot twice adapts "But at my back I always hear" (185, 196), and what is heard is first the rattle of the bones (186), then the horns and motors (197). His essay "Andrew Marvell" (<i>Times Literary Supplement</i>, March 1921) found in Marvell "a tough reasonableness beneath the slight lyric grace".

How the proposal was reached: the reader's sentence ("adapts"; what is heard, by line).

### day-parliament-of-bees (The Parliament of Bees)

Present:

what: Actaeon and Diana, turned into Sweeney and Mrs Porter.
date: printed 1641 (written c. 1608–16)
note:
A masque-like sequence of "characters" among the bees; the passage Eliot's note quotes describes a spring hunt bringing Actaeon to the goddess bathing. In Ovid the hunter who sees Diana naked is turned into a stag and torn apart by his own dogs. Eliot brings Sweeney to Mrs Porter in a motor car.

Proposed:

date: printed 1641; date of writing unknown
note:
Twelve verse "characters" of bees, satirical portraits in which bees stand for kinds of men. In the third, the ambitious Polypragmus describes the palace he will build, where, at a sudden "noise of horns and hunting", Actaeon will come upon Diana bathing and be torn by his own hounds (Ovid, <i>Metamorphoses</i> III). In Day "the spring" is the water Diana bathes in, as in Ovid ("Where all shall see her naked skin"); in Eliot's line it is the season. Eliot's note at 197 quotes the four lines. The earliest edition that survives is the quarto of 1641; when Day wrote it is not known.

How the proposal was reached: the reader's palace sentence (the roof of forests cut); the scholar's sentence on "the spring" (the scholar has the last word on what Day's line means); on the editions, the scholar's "earliest that survives" with the reader's cut of the phantom 1607 edition, which the date field already covers.

### verlaine-parsifal (Parsifal)

Present:

what: "Et O ces voix d'enfants, chantant dans la coupole!"
date: 1886
note:
Verlaine's sonnet on Wagner's last opera, written for the <i>Revue wagnérienne</i>, compresses the whole plot into thirteen lines and then, in the fourteenth, simply reports the sound of the boys' choir in the Grail temple. Eliot places that line after a barrack-room song about a brothel, so that the temptation and the temple stand one line apart. Parsifal is Weston's Grail knight; his healing of the king is the thing the poem never manages.

Proposed:

passage, line 7: "rentre sous la tente" (not "sa tente"); trans: "returns to the tent".
note:
Verlaine's sonnet on Wagner's last opera, one of a set of sonnets in homage to Wagner printed in the <i>Revue wagnérienne</i> in January 1886. Thirteen lines give the story: Parsifal resists the Flower Maidens and Kundry, wins back the spear, heals King Amfortas and becomes king and priest of the Grail; the last line is the sound of the boys' choir singing in the dome of the Grail temple. Eliot's note at 202 cites the poem; his line differs from Verlaine's only in punctuation and a capital O, and he sets it straight after the song about Mrs Porter.

How the proposal was reached: the reader's cuts (the day of the month, the 1888 collection, the itemised punctuation).

### lyly-campaspe (Campaspe (the nightingale song))

Present:

what: "Jug jug" and "Tereu."
date: 1584
note:
The Elizabethan convention that the nightingale sings "jug jug" and calls "tereu," the vocative of Tereus, is at its plainest in this song from Lyly's court comedy; the songs in Lyly's plays were first printed in the collected edition of 1632, and whether he wrote them is disputed. Eliot's "Jug Jug to dirty ears" and the four-line burst of birdsong at lines 203–206 are Lyly's bird, with the pretty convention turned back into what it describes.

Proposed:

date: play printed 1584; song first printed 1632
note:
In this song from Lyly's court comedy (Act V scene i) the "ravish'd" nightingale cries "jug, jug" and "tereu", the vocative of Tereus, as if calling the name of the man who raped her. The songs in Lyly's plays are missing from the early printings and first appeared in a collected edition of 1632, long after his death. Whether he wrote them has long been doubted; W. W. Greg argued in 1905 that they are not his. Eliot's "'Jug Jug' to dirty ears" (103) and the cry at 203–206 use the same "jug jug" and "tereu"; "So rudely forc'd" (205) repeats line 100.

How the proposal was reached: the reader's two sentences, with the scholar's correction that Greg was not the first to doubt the songs. Round 3: "take the song's" corrected to "use the same", since Eliot names no source for the words and other Elizabethan poems have them (Barnfield, 1598); Lyly is the editors' candidate.

### sappho-104 (Fragment 104a (Hesperus))

Present:

what: The evening hour that "brings the sailor home from sea."
date: c. 600 BC
note:
Two lines that survive because a critic, Demetrius in his treatise <i>On Style</i>, quoted them. Eliot's note says his lines "may not appear as exact as Sappho's" and that he was thinking of the "longshore" fisherman coming in at nightfall. Byron and Stevenson ("Home is the sailor, home from sea") are the English relay stations between Sappho and the typist's teatime.

Proposed:

note:
Sappho's address to the evening star, which brings back all that the dawn scattered. The two lines survive only as quotations, and editors have joined them: Demetrius, <i>On Style</i> 141, quotes the star and its three "you bring" as an example of graceful repetition, and a Byzantine dictionary, the <i>Etymologicum Magnum</i>, preserves "all that shining Dawn scattered". Eliot's note at 221 says his lines "may not appear as exact as Sappho's lines" and that he had in mind the "longshore" or "dory" fisherman coming in at nightfall. "Brings the sailor home from sea" also echoes Stevenson's "Requiem", "Home is the sailor, home from sea".

How the proposal was reached: the reader's order (what Sappho says first, then the textual history); Byron cut at the reader's asking, since nothing in Eliot's words echoes him; the last sentence split.

### goldsmith-vicar (The Vicar of Wakefield, chapter 24)

Present:

what: "When lovely woman stoops to folly."
date: 1766
note:
Olivia Primrose, seduced and abandoned by the squire, sings this to her family. Eliot's typist, in the same meter, puts on a record. The scene has been read as contempt for her and as the poem's one act of mercy toward a woman: she is allowed not to die.

Proposed:

Also: the passage's "soothe" and "is—to die" differ from the Gutenberg text's "sooth" and "bosom—is to die"; editions differ, and the gloss "lovely-woman" carries the same text, so settle both together or leave both.
note:
Olivia Primrose, seduced by Squire Thornhill and abandoned, sings this at her mother's request, on the spot where she first met him. Eliot's note at 253 cites the song; he takes its first line, adds "and", and rhymes it into a pentameter quatrain in which the typist, instead of dying, puts on a record. The lines have been read both as contempt for her and as pity.

### froude-elizabeth (History of England: the reign of Elizabeth)

Present:

what: Elizabeth and Leicester in the barge.
date: 1863
note:
Froude quotes a letter of 1561 from the Spanish ambassador, Álvaro de la Quadra, to Philip II. The marriage never happened; Dudley's wife had died the year before in circumstances that made it impossible. Eliot's Thames pageant of red and gold is this afternoon, seen from the water, with nothing coming of it.

Proposed:

passage: In the afternoon we were in a barge, watching the games on the river. She was alone with the Lord Robert and myself on the poop, when they began to talk nonsense, and went so far that Lord Robert at last said, as I was on the spot there was no reason why they should not be married if the queen pleased.
(Froude's words, checked against the 1911 Everyman page; the present passage is Eliot's note, with "(The queen)" for "She" and "the" dropped. The Part III gloss "elizabeth-leicester" now quotes Froude.)
note:
Froude prints, in his own translation, a letter of 30 June 1561 from the Spanish ambassador, Álvaro de la Quadra, to Philip II, reporting an afternoon on the river with the queen and Lord Robert Dudley, later Earl of Leicester. Dudley's wife, Amy Robsart, had been found dead at the foot of a staircase in September 1560, and the marriage never took place.

### augustine-confessions (Confessions, Books III and X)

Present:

what: "To Carthage then I came"; "O Lord Thou pluckest me out."
date: c. 400; translation 1838
note:
Augustine's account of his student years in Carthage, "a cauldron of unholy loves," and, six books later, of the pull of beautiful things on a man trying to look at God. The Latin of the first sentence puns Carthago with sartago, a frying pan, which is why the Fire Sermon ends with it. Eliot uses Pusey's Victorian translation, the one in Everyman's Library, and rewrites its first sentence in his note as "to Carthage then I came, where a cauldron of unholy loves sang all about mine ears."

Proposed:

note:

### buddha-fire-sermon (The Fire Sermon (Ādittapariyāya Sutta))

Proposed:

note:
According to the Mahāvagga, the Pali text Warren translates it from, the Buddha preached it soon after his enlightenment, on Gayā Head, to a thousand monks who had been matted-hair ascetics and tenders of the sacred fire. It says that every sense and everything it meets is on fire, with passion, hatred and infatuation. Eliot's note names Warren's <i>Buddhism in Translations</i> (Harvard Oriental Series, 1896), where the sermon is §73, pp. 351–353. It was the standard English anthology of the Pali texts when Eliot studied Sanskrit and Indian philosophy at Harvard in 1911–13, and the series it appeared in was edited by Charles Lanman, who taught him Sanskrit. It is generally assumed he met the sermon there. The sermon gives Part III its title and, at 308 and 311, its "burning"; Eliot's note says it "corresponds in importance to the Sermon on the Mount".

How the proposal was reached: the reader's three changes; the ground for the Harvard assumption added from the scholar's title-page check (Lanman edited the series and taught Eliot Sanskrit), in the reader's round-3 order.
